### Common Mistakes

These errors are the most frequent reasons strong engineers receive a "no hire" decision on behavioral rounds despite solid technical performance. Using "we" instead of "I" obscures your individual contribution and makes it impossible for interviewers to assess your ownership level. Fake weaknesses such as "I work too hard" signal low self-awareness, a trait that predicts poor performance in ambiguous, fast-moving environments.

```
❌ Rambling without structure
❌ Using "we" instead of "I"
❌ Not preparing enough stories
❌ Criticizing former employers
❌ Answers that are too short
❌ Not knowing the company's values
❌ Fake weaknesses
❌ Not quantifying impact
```

### Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Write Your Story Bank
Create 8 stories covering:
- Technical leadership (2 stories)
- Conflict resolution (2 stories)
- Failure and learning (2 stories)
- Mentoring/collaboration (2 stories)

### Exercise 2: Practice Recording
Record yourself answering:
- "Tell me about yourself"
- "Tell me about your biggest failure"
- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager"

Watch the recordings and note improvements.

### Exercise 3: Company-Specific Prep
Choose your target company and:
- List their values/principles
- Map your stories to each principle
- Prepare specific examples that demonstrate each value


### Junior → FAANG: Reviewing Behavioral Interview Key Takeaways

The key takeaways from this chapter are: behavioral interviews predict future performance using past behavior. The STAR method gives you a reliable structure. A story bank prepared in advance prevents blanking during the interview. And results with metrics are stronger than results without. The most actionable next step is to write out your 8-10 stories this week, practice them out loud, and have a friend or colleague give you feedback on clarity and specificity.

### Senior @ FAANG: Chapter Summary as a Behavioral Calibration Check

Senior candidates reviewing this chapter summary should use it as a calibration check: have you actually moved your behavioral preparation to the L6 scope level, or are you still telling L4 stories? The signal is in the scope and ambiguity of your stories. L4 stories involve individual technical work with local team impact. L6 stories involve driving direction across multiple teams, navigating organizational conflict, making decisions with incomplete information at significant scale, and producing results measured in business metrics. If your story bank is primarily L4 stories, you have two choices: find the L6 stories buried in your experience by reframing existing stories from the organizational perspective rather than the technical perspective, or explicitly seek out L6-scope opportunities before your next interview cycle.
