## 82.6 Adapting Stories for Different Questions

### The Pivot Technique

The pivot technique allows a candidate to redirect a question toward their strongest prepared story while still addressing the question's underlying competency dimension. The following examples demonstrate how to identify the evaluator's intent and smoothly transition to the most relevant story from your prepared library.

```
One story can answer multiple questions by changing emphasis:

CORE STORY: Database Migration Project

Question 1: "Tell me about technical leadership"
EMPHASIS: How you designed the migration, made technical
decisions, influenced the team

Question 2: "Tell me about managing stakeholders"
EMPHASIS: How you communicated timeline, managed expectations,
got buy-in from product team

Question 3: "Tell me about handling ambiguity"
EMPHASIS: How you dealt with unclear requirements, made
decisions with incomplete information

Question 4: "Tell me about mentoring"
EMPHASIS: How you brought junior engineers along, taught
them during the project

Same story, different focus based on the question asked.
```

### Scaling Stories for Seniority

Stories must be calibrated to the seniority level being evaluated: the same event described with scope, complexity, and organizational impact appropriate for a senior engineer reads very differently than a mid-level account of the same technical work. The following examples demonstrate how to scale the same story appropriately for L4, L5, and L6 evaluation criteria.

```
JUNIOR LEVEL:
- Focus on individual contribution
- Learning and growth
- Following best practices
- "I implemented..."

MID LEVEL:
- Ownership of larger features
- Mentoring others
- Cross-team collaboration
- "I led the implementation of..."

SENIOR LEVEL:
- Technical strategy and architecture
- Influence across teams
- Driving organizational improvements
- "I designed and led the effort to..."

STAFF LEVEL:
- Company-wide impact
- Long-term technical vision
- Developing other leaders
- "I established the strategy for..."

EXAMPLE SCALING:

Junior: "I implemented the caching layer using Redis"

Mid: "I designed and implemented a caching strategy that
reduced latency by 50%"

Senior: "I identified caching as a critical gap across our
services, designed a company-wide approach, and led the
implementation across three teams"

Staff: "I established our caching philosophy, created the
architectural standards, mentored tech leads on implementation,
and the pattern is now used across 20 services"
```


### Junior → FAANG: Using One Story to Answer Multiple Questions

A strong work story can address multiple behavioral questions depending on how you frame it. A story about leading a complex migration can address 'tell me about a time you drove a large project', 'tell me about a time you dealt with technical risk', 'tell me about a time you influenced without authority', and 'tell me about a time you simplified something complex'. The skill is in knowing which angle to lead with for each question. Practice this with a partner: have them ask different questions and practice reframing the same story from different angles without it feeling like you are avoiding the question.

### Senior @ FAANG: Story Adaptability as an Interview Agility Signal

Senior candidates who can fluidly adapt their stories to different question angles signal a quality that FAANG interviewers value at L6: the ability to read the room and adjust. The implicit message is that you understand what the interviewer is actually asking (not just the surface question) and you are directing your answer to the underlying concern. The governance concern for senior story banks is avoiding story reuse across multiple rounds on the same day: interviewers at most companies debrief together, and if multiple interviewers heard the same story, it raises a flag about the breadth of your experience. Track which story you used in which round and have a backup story ready for each theme.
