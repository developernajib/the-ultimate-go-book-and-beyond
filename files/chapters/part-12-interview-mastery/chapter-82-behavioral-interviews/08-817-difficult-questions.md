## 82.7 Difficult Questions

### "Tell me about yourself"

"Tell me about yourself" is the opening question in most technical interviews and sets the frame for everything that follows, making a polished 90-second response essential. The following template structures the answer to emphasize technical depth, recent impact, and motivation for the specific role rather than reciting a resume chronology.

```
STRUCTURE (1-2 minutes):

Present → Past → Future

"I'm currently a Senior Go Engineer at [Company] where I
lead the platform team building our core APIs. We serve
10 million requests per day.

Before this, I spent 3 years at [Previous Company] where
I started as a mid-level engineer and was promoted to senior
after leading a critical database migration.

I'm particularly interested in [Target Company] because of
your work on distributed systems at scale. I'm excited about
the opportunity to contribute to [specific team/product]."

DO:
✓ Keep it relevant to the role
✓ Highlight key achievements
✓ Show progression and growth
✓ Connect to why this company

DON'T:
✗ Tell your life story
✗ Read your resume
✗ Be too vague
✗ Talk for more than 2 minutes
```

### "Why are you leaving your current job?"

"Why are you leaving?" probes honesty, self-awareness, and professionalism, with most interviewers screening for negativity, poor cultural fit, or performance issues as disqualifying signals. The following response frameworks demonstrate how to answer truthfully while focusing on growth motivation rather than grievances about the current employer.

```
GOOD ANSWERS:
- "I'm looking for larger scale challenges"
- "I want to work on distributed systems at [Company]'s scale"
- "I'm excited about the opportunity to have broader impact"
- "I'm looking to grow into a Staff/Principal role"

AVOID:
- Negative comments about current employer
- Complaints about management
- Salary as the primary reason (even if true)
- "I'm bored" (sounds disengaged)

SAMPLE:
"I've had a great experience at my current company - I've
grown from mid to senior level and led several impactful
projects. However, I'm excited about the scale of problems
at [Target Company]. Working with systems that serve
billions of users is something I'm passionate about and
would be the ideal next step in my career."
```

### "What's your biggest weakness?"

The weakness question tests self-awareness and growth mindset, interviewers are not looking for a candidate with no weaknesses, but for one who recognizes limitations and actively addresses them. The following response structure demonstrates how to cite a genuine developmental area with a concrete improvement trajectory that shows maturity and credibility.

```
GOOD APPROACH:
- Choose a real weakness
- Show self-awareness
- Demonstrate you're working on it
- Make it relevant but not disqualifying

SAMPLE ANSWERS:

Technical:
"I tend to dive too deep into code before stepping back to
consider architecture. I've learned to time-box my exploration
and force myself to write design docs before coding."

Process:
"I sometimes underestimate task complexity. I've started
adding explicit buffer time to my estimates and checking
them against historical data."

Communication:
"I can be too direct when giving feedback. I've been working
on balancing candor with empathy, and I now write out
feedback before delivering it to ensure the right tone."

AVOID:
- "I work too hard" (eye-roll)
- "I'm a perfectionist" (cliché)
- Something disqualifying ("I can't work with others")
- "I don't have any weaknesses" (red flag)
```


### Junior → FAANG: Handling Difficult Behavioral Questions

Difficult behavioral questions are questions that probe uncomfortable experiences: a time you failed, a time you made a controversial decision, a time you were wrong. The instinct to soften or deflect these answers is understandable but counterproductive. Interviewers score answers to difficult questions more favorably when the candidate demonstrates genuine self-awareness and learning, not when they minimize the difficulty. The formula: acknowledge the situation honestly, explain what you did and why at the time, describe what went wrong, and articulate specifically what you learned and how it changed your behavior.

### Senior @ FAANG: Difficult Questions as Bar-Raiser Probes

Bar-raisers specifically use difficult questions to probe the candidate's relationship with failure and uncertainty. At L6 and above, the expectation is not that you made no mistakes but that you have made consequential mistakes, learned from them, and applied those learnings in visible ways that improved your judgment. A candidate who has no strong failure story is either not being honest or has not been working on hard enough problems. The bar-raiser also uses difficult questions to probe for defensive reasoning patterns: candidates who externalize blame, qualify extensively, or frame every failure as primarily someone else's fault signal poor self-awareness. The ideal answer to a failure question takes direct ownership, demonstrates clear-eyed analysis of what went wrong in your own decision-making (not just in the circumstances), and shows a specific behavioral change that resulted.
