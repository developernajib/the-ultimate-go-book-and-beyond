## 83.6 Handling Counteroffers

When you resign from your current job, there is a good chance your employer will make a counteroffer to keep you. This can be flattering and tempting, especially if the counter includes a significant raise or promotion. However, counteroffers are one of the most frequently mishandled situations in career transitions, and the data shows that most people who accept them end up leaving within a year anyway. This section helps you evaluate a counteroffer objectively and respond professionally regardless of your decision.

### From Your Current Employer

Receiving a counteroffer feels validating, but you need to evaluate it with the same analytical rigor you applied to your external offers. The critical question is whether the counter addresses the root causes that made you start looking in the first place. If you were leaving because of limited growth, a toxic team, or boring work, a salary bump alone will not fix those problems. The diagram below walks through the key considerations.

```
SCENARIO: You resign, and your current employer makes a counter.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              COUNTEROFFER CONSIDERATIONS                         │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  WHY COUNTEROFFERS ARE RISKY:                                    │
│  ├── You've shown you're a flight risk                           │
│  ├── Trust with management may be damaged                        │
│  ├── 50-80% of people who accept counteroffers leave within      │
│  │   6-12 months anyway                                          │
│  └── The issues that made you leave often remain                 │
│                                                                  │
│  WHEN IT MIGHT MAKE SENSE:                                       │
│  ├── The counter addresses root causes (not just money)          │
│  ├── You're getting a promotion/role change, not just raise      │
│  ├── You genuinely prefer staying but needed the push            │
│  └── The new opportunity has red flags you've discovered         │
│                                                                  │
│  QUESTIONS TO ASK YOURSELF:                                      │
│  ├── Why wasn't this offered before I resigned?                  │
│  ├── Will this change the underlying issues?                     │
│  ├── How will my manager view me going forward?                  │
│  └── Am I just taking the comfortable path?                      │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

DECLINE SCRIPT:
"I really appreciate this offer, and it shows how much you
value my contributions. However, my decision to leave wasn't
purely about compensation. I'm looking for [new challenges/
different scope/new industry experience], and I believe this
is the right time for me to make this move. I'm committed
to making the transition as smooth as possible."
```

### Using Your Current Comp as Leverage

Your current compensation is a legitimate data point in negotiations, but how you use it matters. Sharing your current pay honestly gives the recruiter context for your expectations, especially if you are walking away from unvested equity or a pending bonus. However, inflating your current compensation or fabricating offers to create artificial pressure is both unethical and risky, because companies do verify employment details and the tech industry is smaller than you think.

```
ETHICAL APPROACH:

DO:
✓ Share your current compensation if asked
✓ Explain what you're leaving behind (unvested equity, bonus)
✓ Use it as context for your expectations

DON'T:
✗ Inflate your current compensation
✗ Lie about other offers
✗ Create fake deadlines

EXAMPLE:
"I'm currently at $280K total compensation - $190K base,
$60K annual equity vest, and 15% bonus. I also have $80K
in unvested equity I'd be walking away from. I'm looking
for something in the $350K+ range to make this move make
sense financially."
```

### Junior → FAANG: Understanding Why Counteroffers Are a Trap for Early-Career Engineers

A counteroffer from your current employer feels like validation, and for a junior engineer who has not yet navigated a resignation before, the emotional pull to accept is real. The data point that frames the decision correctly: you are not being offered more because you are worth more. You are being offered more because replacing you costs two to four times your salary when recruitment, onboarding, and ramp-up time are factored in.

Prerequisite gap: before you resign, know your numbers. Calculate your current unvested equity, pending bonus dates, and any sign-on clawback obligations. These are the financial anchors that affect your walkaway math, and you need them before the resignation conversation.

First-job habit: use your current compensation honestly and only as context, not as the floor for the new offer. At the junior level, the biggest mistake is framing the conversation as "match my current pay." Frame it instead as "here is my market value based on research," which shifts the negotiation to forward-looking data rather than backward-looking salary history.

Competing-offer ethics: never fabricate or inflate an offer. The tech industry is smaller than it appears, and recruiters at competing companies often know each other. A single verifiable dishonesty here is permanent reputational damage in the networks that matter.

### Senior @ FAANG: Counteroffer Governance, Ethical Leverage, and Walking Away from Unvested Equity

At the senior and staff level, counteroffers are structurally more complex because the components being matched are not just salary. A retention package from your current employer might include accelerated vesting, a promotion to Staff, and a green card sponsorship commitment. Each of these requires independent analysis.

Accelerated vesting as a retention tool: ask for the specific vesting schedule in writing before evaluating it. "We will accelerate your vesting" is not a commitment. "We will move your remaining 200,000 shares to a 12-month cliff with monthly vesting thereafter" is. The difference between these two statements is often $200,000 to $400,000 at a mid-stage public company.

Green card commitment as leverage: a green card sponsorship promise in a retention counteroffer binds you to the employer for two to five years in a job market where your alternatives are limited by visa status. Senior candidates should evaluate this not as a benefit but as a constraint. The dollar value of the benefit must exceed the option value you are surrendering by removing yourself from the market.

ADR framing for the counteroffer decision: treat the accept-or-decline decision as an architectural decision with documented trade-offs. Write down the root causes of your job search, map each cause to whether the counter addresses it, and document what evidence you would need to believe the fix is durable. If the fix requires trusting a single manager's verbal commitment rather than a structural change, the risk of accepting is high.

Bar-raiser signal: senior candidates who decline a counteroffer professionally, with a clear written statement about their reasoning and a thorough transition plan, consistently receive better references and a warmer reception when they eventually cross paths with former colleagues again. The way you leave a company is remembered as long as the way you performed.

Using current compensation as leverage is most effective when you have written documentation: your current offer letter, a recent performance review with a specific rating, and an email thread showing a promotion discussion that was delayed or denied. These transform a subjective claim into a verifiable track record that gives recruiters something concrete to take to their compensation team.
