## 83.7 Beyond Compensation

Money is important, but it is not the only factor that determines whether a job will make you happy and advance your career. Two offers with identical total compensation can lead to very different outcomes depending on the team, the manager, the growth opportunities, and the work-life balance. This section helps you evaluate the complete picture so you make a decision you will not regret a year from now.

### Non-Monetary Factors

The evaluation matrix below assigns weights to different categories of factors that affect your job satisfaction and career growth. The default weights reflect a balanced perspective, but you should adjust them based on your personal priorities. For example, if you are early in your career and learning is your top goal, you might weight growth at 35% and reduce compensation to 25%. The key is to make your priorities explicit rather than letting the highest number win by default.

### Questions to Ask Before Accepting

Before you sign an offer, you should ask questions that reveal information you cannot easily find through research. The quality of your manager, the health of the team, and the real day-to-day expectations often determine your experience more than compensation does. These questions also signal to the company that you are thoughtful and serious about finding the right fit, which strengthens your position rather than weakening it.

```
ABOUT THE TEAM:
- How long has the team been together?
- What's the typical tenure of engineers?
- What happened to the previous person in this role?
- How are decisions made on the team?

ABOUT THE ROLE:
- What does success look like in 6 months? 1 year?
- What are the biggest challenges I'll face?
- What percentage of time is new development vs maintenance?
- Who will I be working with day-to-day?

ABOUT GROWTH:
- What's the typical promotion timeline?
- How are performance reviews conducted?
- What learning resources are provided?
- What does the staff/principal engineer path look like?

ABOUT THE COMPANY:
- What's the company's financial health?
- Any layoffs in the past 2 years?
- What's the runway (for startups)?
- Any planned organizational changes?
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building Your First Real Evaluation Framework

Most junior candidates accept offers based on the total compensation number and the company brand. These are real signals, but they are incomplete. The evaluation matrix in this section exists precisely to counter that bias.

Prerequisite gap: you likely do not have a reference point for what "good" looks like on the non-monetary dimensions. Build one before the offer stage. During interviews, ask at least one question about promotion timelines, one about how decisions are made on the team, and one about what happened to the previous person in the role. These questions cost nothing to ask and provide the data points the matrix requires.

Manager quality is the variable that most consistently predicts your experience in the first two years. A great manager at a mediocre company will teach you more and accelerate your growth faster than a weak manager at a prestigious company. Ask specifically: "How long have you been managing this team?" and "What is the career trajectory of engineers who have reported to you?" The answers are more revealing than any Glassdoor review.

First-job habit: complete the evaluation matrix with real numbers, not gut feelings. Assign a score from 1 to 10 to each factor for each offer, apply the weights, and total the results. This does not override your intuition, but it makes your intuition visible and auditable.

### Senior @ FAANG: Non-Monetary Factors as Strategic Career Infrastructure

At the senior and staff level, the non-monetary factors are not soft considerations. They are the infrastructure of your next two to five years of career capital.

Team trajectory matters more than team quality at a point in time. A team that is growing, owns an expanding surface area, and has visible executive sponsorship will give you more opportunity for impact than a team in maintenance mode regardless of how technically strong the individuals are. Ask about the roadmap for the next 18 months, the headcount plan, and which executive champion owns the team's domain. These are L6/L7 questions that most candidates do not ask.

Manager evaluation at senior levels is bidirectional. You are also evaluating whether the hiring manager has a track record of sponsoring their reports for promotion. Ask: "How many of your direct reports have been promoted in the last two years?" and "What is the highest level you have promoted someone to?" A manager who cannot answer these questions clearly is a signal about how invested they are in the growth of their team.

On-call and operational burden is a non-monetary cost that is rarely discussed before joining. For staff engineers, on-call expectations can consume 10-20% of your productive work time. Ask for specifics: how many incidents per week on average, what the escalation path looks like, and whether there is a defined SLA for on-call rotation frequency. If the answer is vague, that vagueness is itself a signal.

Governance over the evaluation process: after completing your offer comparison, document it. Write a one-page decision record that captures the criteria, weights, scores, and your recommendation to yourself. This document serves two purposes. It keeps you honest if you are tempted to rationalize a decision based on ego or anchoring bias, and it gives you a reference point 12 months later to evaluate whether your framework was predictive. Building this habit creates a personal database of decision quality that informs every future career move.

Questions about growth at senior level are not about promotion timelines. They are about influence surface: will you have the opportunity to define architecture across multiple teams, contribute to hiring and calibration, and represent the engineering organization in cross-functional forums? These are the activities that compound into staff-level impact, and if the role does not include them, you are likely being asked to operate one level below your title.
