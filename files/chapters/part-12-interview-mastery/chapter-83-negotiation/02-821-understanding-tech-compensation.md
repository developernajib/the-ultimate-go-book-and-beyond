## 83.1 Understanding Tech Compensation

Tech compensation is not just a single salary number. At most technology companies, your total compensation (often abbreviated TC) is made up of several distinct components, each with its own rules, tax treatment, and negotiability. Understanding how these pieces fit together is the foundation of every negotiation conversation you will have. If you only focus on base salary, you may overlook the majority of what a company is actually paying you.

### Total Compensation Components

The diagram below breaks down every major component you will encounter in a tech compensation package. Some components, like base salary, are predictable and stable. Others, like equity, can vary dramatically depending on the company's stock performance and your vesting schedule. Knowing what each piece means and how it is taxed helps you compare offers accurately and identify which levers to push during negotiation.

### Sample Compensation Packages

Looking at real-world numbers makes the abstract components concrete. The two examples below show how a FAANG offer and a growth startup offer might look side by side for the same seniority level. Notice how the FAANG offer has higher guaranteed cash through signing bonuses that taper off, while the startup offer carries more risk but potentially higher upside if the company succeeds. Always calculate your year-by-year total compensation, not just the headline number, because the mix shifts significantly over time.

### Junior → FAANG: Understanding Tech Compensation Packages

FAANG compensation packages typically include four components: base salary (paid biweekly or monthly), equity (RSUs that vest over 4 years, typically with a 1-year cliff), annual performance bonus (typically 10-20% of base salary, discretionary), and signing bonus (one-time payment). Total compensation (TC) is the sum of all components on an annualized basis and is the standard comparison unit for senior engineers. Levels.fyi provides crowdsourced TC data by company, level, and location that is essential for understanding your market position.

### Senior @ FAANG: Compensation Architecture at the Senior Level: Equity Complexity and Expected Value

At the senior level, equity complexity increases significantly. RSU refreshes (annual additional equity grants given to retain employees) can add 20-50% to the annual equity value for strong performers, but the refresh schedule and amounts are not typically disclosed in the initial offer. The expected value calculation requires estimating the refresh rate, which is harder than the initial grant calculation. Stock options (common at pre-IPO companies) have a more complex expected value calculation: the option strike price, the current estimated fair market value, the time to liquidity, and the tax implications of ISO versus NSO options all affect the real value. At the staff engineer level, some companies offer different equity structures (performance RSUs, participation in growth plans) that require understanding before signing. A financial advisor who specializes in tech compensation is worth consulting for offers above 00k TC.
