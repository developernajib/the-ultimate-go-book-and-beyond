## 83.3 Timing and Process

Timing is one of the most overlooked aspects of compensation negotiation. When you discuss numbers matters almost as much as what numbers you discuss. Bringing up compensation too early signals that you care more about money than the role, while waiting too long can leave you scrambling when an offer arrives with a tight deadline. This section covers the optimal timeline and what to say at each stage.

### When to Discuss Compensation

The negotiation timeline has four distinct phases, and each phase has a specific goal. During the recruiter call and interviews, your job is to build value and avoid anchoring yourself to a low number. The real negotiation only begins after you receive an offer, because that is when you have the most leverage: the company has invested time and money evaluating you, they have decided they want you, and they do not want to restart the search.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   NEGOTIATION TIMELINE                           │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  RECRUITER CALL:                                                 │
│  ├── Q: "What are your compensation expectations?"               │
│  │                                                               │
│  │   RESPONSE: "I'm focused on finding the right role and        │
│  │   team fit first. I'm confident we can work out               │
│  │   compensation if we're both excited about the opportunity.   │
│  │   Can you share the range for this role?"                     │
│  │                                                               │
│  └── Goal: Avoid giving a number first                           │
│                                                                  │
│  DURING INTERVIEWS:                                              │
│  ├── Focus on demonstrating value                                │
│  ├── Don't discuss numbers                                       │
│  └── Build your case for why you're valuable                     │
│                                                                  │
│  AFTER RECEIVING OFFER:                                          │
│  ├── This is when real negotiation begins                        │
│  ├── You now have leverage (they want you)                       │
│  └── Take time to evaluate (24-72 hours minimum)                 │
│                                                                  │
│  NEGOTIATION:                                                    │
│  ├── Counter with data and rationale                             │

```

### If Pressed for a Number

Sometimes recruiters will push hard for a specific number early in the process. This puts you at a disadvantage because whoever names a number first sets the anchor for the entire negotiation. The techniques below show you how to deflect gracefully without seeming evasive or uncooperative. If you absolutely must give a number, anchor high using your research data and always provide a range rather than a single figure.

```
DEFLECTION TECHNIQUES:

"I'd rather understand the full scope of the role before
discussing numbers. What's the budgeted range for this position?"

"I'm flexible on compensation. My priority is finding the
right opportunity. What range did you have in mind?"

"I'm currently at $X, but I'm looking for a significant step
up to reflect the responsibilities of this role and my growth.
What can you share about the range?"

IF YOU MUST GIVE A NUMBER:

1. Research thoroughly first
2. Give a range, not a single number
3. Anchor high (75th percentile)
4. Add context

"Based on my research and experience, I'm targeting a total
compensation package in the $350-400K range. This accounts
for base, equity, and bonus. I'm open to discussing how
we structure that."
```


### Junior → FAANG: Negotiation Timing and Process

Timing in the negotiation process matters: negotiate only after you have received the formal offer, not before. Before the offer, revealing your compensation expectations anchors the recruiter to a number, which limits your upside. When asked 'what are you looking for in compensation?' before the offer, redirect: 'I'm flexible and want to learn more about the role and the company's compensation philosophy before discussing specific numbers'. After the offer, you have leverage: the company has invested in your interview process and wants to close the hire.

### Senior @ FAANG: Timing as a Strategic Lever in Senior Negotiations

Senior candidates should actively manage the timing of competing offers to create negotiation leverage. The optimal scenario: multiple offer letters in hand within the same 2-week window, enabling a single negotiation conversation with each company that references the competing offers. Achieving this requires coordinating interview timelines: if Company A is 2 weeks ahead of Company B in the process, ask Company A for a 2-week extension while accelerating Company B's process. Most companies will grant one extension of 1-2 weeks without negative consequences. The risk of managing multiple timelines is managing the emotional pressure to accept a good offer before a great offer arrives. Pre-committing to your decision criteria before offers arrive reduces this risk.
