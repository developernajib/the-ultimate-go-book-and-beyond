## 83.15 International and Visa-Related Negotiation

If you are an international candidate or currently on a work visa, your negotiation has additional complexity. Visa status affects your leverage, your timeline, and the components you should prioritize. This section covers the unique considerations for H-1B holders, international hires relocating to the US, and the specific negotiation scripts that address visa-related concerns.

### H-1B and Visa Sponsorship Considerations

Being on a work visa creates both leverage and constraints in a negotiation. On one hand, the company has invested significant money and effort in sponsoring your visa, which means they are motivated to retain you. On the other hand, the 60-day grace period if you lose your job and the limited pool of sponsoring employers reduce your alternatives. Understanding both sides of this equation helps you negotiate from a position of informed confidence rather than fear.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │

│  ├── Companies invest $15K-$30K in visa costs                    │
```

### Visa Negotiation Scripts

The scripts below address the three most common visa-related negotiation scenarios: requesting premium processing to reduce uncertainty, discussing green card sponsorship as a long-term commitment, and handling the logistics of an H-1B transfer between employers. Each script is designed to frame your request as a mutual benefit rather than a personal favor, which makes the company more likely to agree.

```
SCRIPT 1: Negotiating Premium Processing

"I noticed the offer mentions standard H-1B processing. Given
that premium processing would provide certainty for both of us
on the timeline, would the company cover the premium processing
fee? This would allow me to start sooner and have peace of mind
about the visa status."

SCRIPT 2: Negotiating Green Card Support

"Long-term, I'm interested in staying in the US and at [Company].
Could you share the company's policy on green card sponsorship?
Specifically:
- When can the process begin?
- Does the company cover all legal fees?
- What's the typical timeline?

This is important for my family planning and I'd like to
understand the path before accepting."

SCRIPT 3: Addressing Grace Period Concerns

"I want to be transparent - I'm currently on H-1B with a 60-day
transfer window. I'll need to time my transition carefully.
Could we ensure:
- Premium processing for the H-1B transfer?
- A start date that allows for processing time?
- Written commitment on visa sponsorship as part of the offer?

I want to make sure we're set up for success."
```

### International Hire Considerations

Relocating internationally involves far more than just shipping your belongings. There are tax implications, temporary housing needs, school placement for children, spouse employment challenges, and cultural adjustments that all cost time and money. A thorough relocation package addresses these costs so you can focus on ramping up in your new role. Many candidates do not realize how many of these components are negotiable, so reviewing the checklist below before your negotiation call gives you a significant advantage.

```
NEGOTIATING AS INTERNATIONAL HIRE:

RELOCATION PACKAGE COMPONENTS:
□ Moving expenses (household goods)
□ Temporary housing (30-90 days typical)
□ House-hunting trip
□ Visa/legal fees
□ Tax gross-up (relocation income is taxable)
□ Spouse employment assistance
□ Children's school assistance
□ Language training
□ Cultural training
□ Return trip home annually

NEGOTIATION EXAMPLE:
"Given the international relocation, I'd like to discuss the
relocation package. Specifically:
- Could we include 90 days of temporary housing instead of 30?
- Would the company provide tax gross-up on the relocation?
- Can we include one annual return trip to my home country?

These would significantly help with the transition and let me
focus on ramping up quickly."
```

### Junior → FAANG: What H-1B Holders Must Know Before Negotiating

Visa status creates a real asymmetry in negotiation leverage, but most H-1B candidates underestimate the leverage they do have. Companies that sponsor H-1Bs are not doing you a favor. They are making a business decision that costs them $15,000 to $30,000 in legal and filing fees, plus the time of their immigration team. They have already committed that resource to you. That commitment gives you standing to negotiate the terms of your employment rather than simply accepting whatever is offered.

The most common mistake: disclosing visa dependency before receiving an offer. Visa status is personal information. Do not volunteer it during the screening process. The time to discuss it is after you have an offer and are reviewing terms. At that point, it is a logistical conversation, not a vetting conversation.

Prerequisite gap: understand the difference between H-1B transfer and cap-subject H-1B. If you are already on an H-1B with a different employer, a new employer can transfer your petition without entering the lottery. This significantly expands your alternatives and your leverage because many more employers are willing to do a transfer than a cap-subject filing. Knowing this changes how you frame your negotiation.

Premium processing negotiation: $2,500 is a rounding error in the context of total compensation packages. If the company is unwilling to pay for premium processing, that is worth knowing, because it signals something about how they treat operational details that matter to employees.

### Senior @ FAANG: Visa, Green Card, and International Relocation as Long-Horizon Governance Decisions

At the staff and principal level, visa and immigration decisions are long-horizon governance decisions with five to ten year implications. Treating them as a short-term logistical negotiation is the most common and most costly mistake senior international candidates make.

Green card sponsorship at L6 is not the same as green card sponsorship at L4. At the senior level, you have standing to negotiate the specific legal strategy (EB-1A extraordinary ability vs EB-2 national interest waiver vs EB-2 PERM, for example), who pays for the premium processing at each stage, and whether the company will support a self-petition strategy in parallel with the employer-sponsored route. EB-1A and EB-2 NIW are strategies that staff engineers with peer-reviewed publications, patents, or documented industry contributions can pursue without employer sponsorship. Knowing this changes your negotiating position: you are not dependent on the employer to sponsor your immigration if you qualify for self-petition routes.

PERM and employer lock-in: the PERM process, which is the prerequisite for the EB-2 employer-sponsored category, takes 12 to 24 months and creates a period of employer dependency that constrains your ability to change jobs without restarting the process. Senior candidates should factor this constraint into their company selection decision, not just their negotiation tactics. A company that moves quickly through PERM and into the I-140 stage creates less constraint than one that delays or de-prioritizes the process.

Relocation package governance for international hires: the checklist in this section captures the standard components, but senior candidates should also negotiate two items that are often overlooked. First, tax equalization for the relocation year: international moves create complex tax situations where you may owe taxes in two jurisdictions simultaneously, and a tax equalization policy ensures you pay no more tax than you would have paid as a pure US resident. Second, a return-trip allowance for the first two to three years: this is standard at many tech companies for international relocations but is not offered proactively. Ask for it specifically.

ADR framing for immigration strategy: treat your immigration path as a multi-year architectural decision with documented options, trade-offs, and a chosen strategy. Update this document annually. The candidates who navigate immigration most successfully are the ones who understand their path three to five years in advance, not the ones who react to each step as it arrives. At the staff level, the same proactive, structured thinking that makes you effective in system design should be applied to the system design of your own career infrastructure.
