## 83.12 Core Concepts Recap

This section distills the entire chapter into quick-reference checklists, key takeaways, and power phrases you can review right before a negotiation call. Use these as a final preparation tool after you have studied the detailed sections above.

### Negotiation Checklist

- [ ] Research market rates (levels.fyi, Glassdoor)
- [ ] Understand all compensation components
- [ ] Prepare your counter-offer range
- [ ] Practice negotiation scripts
- [ ] Evaluate non-monetary factors
- [ ] Get final offer in writing
- [ ] Plan resignation carefully

### Key Takeaways

These six principles are the foundation of every successful negotiation covered in this chapter. If you internalize these and nothing else, you will outperform the vast majority of candidates who negotiate without preparation or strategy.

1. **Don't give a number first** - Let them anchor
2. **Negotiate total comp, not just base** - Many levers available
3. **Use data to support your asks** - Makes you credible
4. **Stay positive and professional** - They should want to work with you
5. **Know your walkaway point** - But don't share it
6. **Get everything in writing** - Verbal promises aren't binding

### Negotiation Power Phrases

The phrases below are proven openers and transitions that keep negotiations collaborative rather than adversarial. Each one is designed to assert your position while maintaining a positive relationship with the recruiter. Practice weaving these into your conversations naturally so they do not sound scripted.

```
"Based on my research..."
"Given my experience with [X]..."
"What flexibility do you have on..."
"I'm excited about the opportunity, and I'd like to discuss..."
"If we can get to [X], I'm ready to accept today."
"I want to be transparent with you..."
"I appreciate you working with me on this."
```

### Junior → FAANG: Using This Summary as a Pre-Call Ritual

The checklist and power phrases in this section are most useful when treated as a ritual rather than a one-time read. Before every negotiation call, print or open the checklist, verify each item is done, and say each power phrase out loud at least once. This is not superstition. It is the same reason athletes have warm-up routines. Negotiation is a performance skill, and your verbal fluency degrades under pressure unless you have rehearsed it explicitly.

The power phrase to internalize first: "If we can get to X, I'm ready to accept today." This phrase does three things simultaneously: it signals commitment, it gives the recruiter a specific number to take to their team, and it creates urgency without an ultimatum. Most junior candidates never reach this phrase because they accept before they use it.

Entry-point framing: the six key takeaways map to a skill progression. Items 1 through 3 (don't anchor, negotiate total comp, use data) are the foundational skills to develop first. Items 4 through 6 (stay positive, know your walkaway, get it in writing) are the execution habits that prevent errors once the foundation is solid. Practice them in that order.

### Senior @ FAANG: Principles as Governance Anchors at L6 and Above

The ten commandments listed in this section are phrased as rules, but at the staff and principal level they function as governance anchors: constraints that prevent well-intentioned decisions from becoming costly errors under pressure.

"Never negotiate against yourself" deserves particular emphasis at senior levels. Experienced engineers with strong BATNA positions sometimes walk into negotiations and preemptively narrow their own asks out of misplaced humility. "I know the staff band probably won't go that high, so I'll ask for senior" is the engineer making their first and worst concession before the company has had a chance to respond. State your target, wait for the actual response, and then negotiate from the reality you are given, not the constraints you imagined.

"Know your walkaway point and don't share it": at the staff level, this is a governance rule with compounding effects. Your walkaway number is the output of a multi-factor calculation that includes unvested equity at your current company, the opportunity cost of the role itself (scope, learning, network), and your personal financial situation. Document that number before the call. If you are in a conversation and feeling pressure, you need a pre-committed anchor to return to, not a number you are trying to calculate live.

ADR application: treat the final pre-signing checklist as an architectural decision record for your career. The fields are: decision (accept or decline), context (offer details, competing options, personal goals), options considered, trade-offs, and decision with rationale. Writing this document takes 30 minutes and gives you something you can review 12 to 24 months later to evaluate whether your decision framework was calibrated correctly. This is how staff engineers build judgment: by tracking their decisions and their outcomes over time.

### Practice Exercises

These exercises give you a chance to practice the concepts from this chapter before you face a real negotiation. Treat them seriously: write out your answers in full, practice the scripts out loud, and build your comparison matrix with real numbers from levels.fyi. The candidates who perform best in negotiations are the ones who have rehearsed beforehand.

### Exercise 1: Research Your Market Rate
Use 3 different sources to research compensation for your target role and level. Create a target range for base, equity, and total comp.

### Exercise 2: Practice Your Scripts
Write out and practice:
1. Response to "What are your salary expectations?"
2. Your counter-offer after receiving an offer
3. Response to "That's the best we can do on base"

### Exercise 3: Offer Comparison Matrix
Create a decision matrix for comparing two offers, weighting factors like compensation, growth, work-life balance, and team culture based on your priorities.
