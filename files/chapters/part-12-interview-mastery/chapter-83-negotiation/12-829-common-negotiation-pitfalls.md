## 83.11 Common Negotiation Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Revealing Your Current Salary or Target First

**Illustrative scenario**: A candidate at Google was asked "What are your salary expectations?" and said "$180K." They later learned the budget was $250K. By anchoring low, they left $70K+ on the table. The company offered $185K, exactly what they'd asked for.

**The Wrong Approach:**
```
Recruiter: "What are you making now?"
Candidate: "I'm at $150K, hoping for $175K"
Result: Offer comes in at $178K (company wins)
```

**The Right Approach:**
```
Recruiter: "What are you making now?"

Option 1 (Deflect):
"I'd prefer to focus on the value I can bring to this role.
Can you share the budget for this position?"

Option 2 (Range based on research):
"Based on my research on levels.fyi and conversations with
folks at Google, this role typically ranges from $200K-$280K
total comp. I'd be looking at the higher end given my
experience with distributed systems."

Option 3 (In states where it's illegal to ask):
"I'm in California, where that's not something I'm required
to disclose. I'm happy to discuss your budget for the role."
```

### Pitfall 2: Negotiating Only Base Salary

**Illustrative scenario**: An Amazon candidate successfully negotiated base from $180K to $195K and stopped there. They didn't realize Amazon's TC is heavily RSU-weighted. Missing the stock negotiation cost them $100K+ over 4 years.

**The Wrong Approach:**
```
Focus only on: Base salary
Forget about: Stock, bonus, sign-on, level, title
```

**The Right Approach:**
```
Negotiate total compensation:

Component          | Initial  | Negotiated | Difference
-------------------|----------|------------|------------
Base Salary        | $185K    | $195K      | +$10K
Sign-On Bonus      | $50K     | $100K      | +$50K
RSU Grant (4 year) | $400K    | $500K      | +$100K
Target Bonus       | 15%      | 20%        | +$10K/yr
Level              | L5       | L6         | Matters!
-------------------|----------|------------|------------
Year 1 TC          | $335K    | $420K      | +$85K

Also negotiate:
- Start date (more time = peace of mind)
- Remote work policy
- Relocation package
- Vacation/PTO (especially if policy is "unlimited")
- Team placement
```

### Pitfall 3: Accepting on the Spot

**Illustrative scenario**: A candidate at Meta was so excited about the offer that they said "Yes! I'll sign today!" They later discovered a competing offer would've pushed Meta higher. Additionally, the verbal acceptance made it awkward to negotiate further.

**The Wrong Approach:**
```
Recruiter: "We'd like to offer you $400K TC!"
Candidate: "Wow, that's amazing! I accept! When do I start?"

Problems:
- No time to evaluate competing offers
- Closed door on negotiation
- May regret decision when excitement fades
```

**The Right Approach:**
```
Recruiter: "We'd like to offer you $400K TC!"

Candidate: "Thank you so much! I'm really excited about this
opportunity and the team. To make sure I give this the
consideration it deserves, could I have until [date 3-5 days
out] to review the details?

Could you also send the full offer letter with compensation
breakdown so I can review the specifics?"

THEN:
1. Get the offer in writing
2. Evaluate against competing offers
3. Research market rates
4. Prepare your counter
5. THEN respond
```

### Pitfall 4: Making Ultimatums or Empty Threats

**Illustrative scenario**: A candidate told Apple "I have an offer from Google for $500K, match it or I walk." Apple called the bluff - they didn't match, and the candidate had no real Google offer. Relationship destroyed, offer rescinded.

**The Wrong Approach:**
```
"I need $X or I'm going elsewhere"
"My other offer expires tomorrow" (when it doesn't)
"Google is offering me $Y" (exaggerated or fabricated)
"This is non-negotiable"

Risks:
- They might say "then good luck elsewhere"
- If caught lying, offer rescinded
- Burns bridges permanently
```

**The Right Approach:**
```
Be honest about competing offers:

"I'm fortunate to have an offer from [Company] that's
competitive. Their total comp is around $X. I'm genuinely
more interested in [Target Company], but I need the
compensation to be closer to make this work.

Is there flexibility to increase the RSU grant from
$400K to $500K? That would help me confidently choose
[Target Company] as my next step."

If no competing offer:
"Based on my research at levels.fyi and conversations
with peers, candidates with my background are typically
seeing packages around $X-$Y. I'd love to see this offer
closer to that range."
```

### Pitfall 5: Not Getting the Final Offer in Writing

**Illustrative scenario**: A candidate verbally agreed to terms including a sign-on bonus with their recruiter. The official offer letter arrived with no sign-on. When challenged, the company said "we never agreed to that." With nothing in writing, the candidate had no recourse.

**The Wrong Approach:**
```
Verbal agreement → Verbal acceptance → Start work

What can go wrong:
- Terms "forgotten" or changed
- Hiring manager rotation loses context
- Company restructuring changes comp
- No proof of what was promised
```

**The Right Approach:**
```
Always get written confirmation:

Before accepting verbally, say:
"I'm ready to accept once I see the final numbers in the
offer letter. Could you send the updated letter reflecting:
- Base: $195K
- Sign-on: $100K ($50K year 1, $50K year 2)
- RSU: $500K over 4 years
- Target bonus: 20%

I'll sign as soon as I receive it."

Keep email records of all negotiated terms:
"Hi [Recruiter], thanks for the call! Just to confirm
our discussion, the updated offer includes [X, Y, Z].
Please let me know if I've captured anything incorrectly."
```

### Negotiation Pitfalls Quick Reference

The table below summarizes all five pitfalls in a format you can quickly scan before a negotiation call. Each row identifies the mistake, the risk it creates, and the better approach you should use instead. Keep this reference handy alongside the scripts from Section 83.4.

| Pitfall | Risk | Better Approach |
|---------|------|-----------------|
| Saying number first | Anchor too low | Let them state budget first |
| Base salary only | Miss 50%+ of TC | Negotiate stock, bonus, sign-on |
| Accepting immediately | Leave money behind | Ask for 3-5 days to review |
| Ultimatums/lies | Offer rescinded | Be honest, use market data |
| No written offer | Terms "forgotten" | Get everything in writing |
| Burning bridges | Career damage | Stay professional, express gratitude |

### Junior → FAANG: The Five Pitfalls and Why First-Time Negotiators Fall Into Each One

The incident examples in this section are not edge cases. They represent the modal outcome when candidates walk into negotiation without preparation. Each pitfall has a structural reason it happens.

Anchoring low happens because candidates underestimate their market value and over-weight the risk of rejection. The framing error is treating the negotiation as a one-sided ask rather than a mutual market-matching exercise. Practice the deflection script until it is reflexive: "I'd prefer to focus on fit first. What is the budgeted range for this role?"

Negotiating only base salary happens because base is the only number most people learned to discuss from watching their parents or reading generic career advice. The prerequisite fix is understanding the total compensation architecture of your target companies before the call, which is a 30-minute research task on levels.fyi.

Accepting on the spot is driven by excitement and fear of appearing greedy. The reframe: every recruiter expects you to ask for time. It is not greedy, it is professional. A recruiter who interprets "I would like 48 hours to review this fully" as a negative signal is showing you something about the company's culture.

Making ultimatums or exaggerating offers is driven by anxiety about leverage. The honest version is always more effective: "I have a competing offer that is competitive and I am genuinely more interested in this opportunity. Is there room to close the gap?" This gives the recruiter a reason to advocate for you internally without creating the risk of being caught in an exaggeration.

Not getting it in writing is driven by trust, which is not the same as verification. Recruiters are not adversarial, but they are human, and verbal agreements passed through multiple internal systems accumulate errors. The confirmation email habit costs 10 minutes and protects thousands of dollars.

### Senior @ FAANG: Advanced Pitfalls at the Staff and Principal Level

Senior candidates encounter a different category of pitfall than the five documented here. These are the errors that experienced engineers make because they are operating with too much confidence or too little process.

Treating the negotiation as one-time rather than multi-stage: most FAANG negotiations have two or three rounds. After your initial counter, the recruiter will go to their compensation team and come back with a revised offer. Candidates who spend all their leverage in the first counter have nothing left for the second round. Stage your asks: lead with the highest-priority component, get movement, then ask about secondary components.

Conflating the recruiter's constraints with the company's limits: recruiters operate within a decision tree, and their "that is the best we can do" is often the limit of their individual authority, not the company's ceiling. Asking for escalation is appropriate and professional at the staff level: "I understand this may be at the edge of your authority. Is there a path to get this reviewed at a higher level given my specific background?"

Underinvesting in the relationship with the hiring manager: the hiring manager often has a separate influence channel into the compensation decision that bypasses the recruiter entirely. At the staff level, a hiring manager who is highly motivated to close you can advocate directly to their VP. Build that relationship during the interview process, not just during negotiation.

Governance pitfall: failing to audit the refresher grant cadence. At Google and Meta, annual refresher grants are real and significant, but they are discretionary, not contractual. Getting clarity on the historical refresh amounts for the team you are joining is due diligence, not greed. Ask: "What is a typical annual refresh grant for a strong performer at this level on this team?"

Bar-raiser signal: candidates who navigate these pitfalls effectively demonstrate one consistent behavior: they ask clarifying questions before making requests. "Help me understand how the equity refresher process works here" before "I would like more equity" is the difference between an informed stakeholder and a positional negotiator. Interviewers at the bar-raiser level are watching for this quality.
