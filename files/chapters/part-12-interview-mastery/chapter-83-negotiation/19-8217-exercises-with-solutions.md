## Exercises

The exercises below simulate real negotiation scenarios you are likely to encounter. Each exercise includes a detailed solution that walks through the reasoning, calculations, and scripts you would use. Working through these exercises before you have a live offer builds the analytical muscle and verbal confidence you need to negotiate effectively under pressure.

### Exercise 1: Market Research and Target Range

**Scenario**: You're a Senior Software Engineer with 6 years of experience, expertise in Go and distributed systems, interviewing for L5/E5 roles at FAANG companies and unicorn startups in San Francisco.

**Task**: Create a compensation target sheet with your research.

**Solution**:

```markdown
# Compensation Target Sheet

## My Profile
- Level: Senior SWE (L5 at Google, E5 at Meta, L6 at Amazon)
- Experience: 6 years
- Location: San Francisco Bay Area
- Specialization: Go, distributed systems

## Research Sources
1. levels.fyi - Verified compensation data
2. Glassdoor - Self-reported (adjust -10% for accuracy)
3. Blind - Anonymous discussions
4. Personal network - 3 contacts at target companies

## Market Data (Senior SWE, SF Bay Area)

| Company | Base | RSU (4yr) | Bonus | Sign-on | Year 1 TC |
|---------|------|-----------|-------|---------|-----------|
| Google L5 | $200-240K | $300-450K | 15% | $50-100K | $400-520K |
| Meta E5 | $210-260K | $400-600K | 15% | $75-150K | $480-650K |
| Amazon L6 | $185K cap | $400-600K | - | $100-200K | $380-500K |
| Stripe L4 | $250-300K | $300-500K | - | $50-100K | $400-550K |
| Uber 5b | $200-240K | $300-450K | 15% | $50-100K | $400-500K |

## My Target Range

| Component | Floor | Target | Stretch |
|-----------|-------|--------|---------|
| Base | $200K | $220K | $250K |
| RSU (4yr) | $350K | $450K | $550K |
| Bonus Target | 15% | 15% | 20% |
| Sign-on | $50K | $100K | $150K |
| Year 1 TC | $400K | $480K | $560K |

## Walk-Away Point
- Below $380K Year 1 TC
- Below L5 equivalent leveling
- No equity component

## Negotiation Priorities
1. Equity (highest variance, most negotiable)
2. Sign-on (one-time, often flexible)
3. Base (least flexible at big tech)
```

### Exercise 2: Counter-Offer Preparation

**Scenario**: You receive the following offer from Google for L5:
- Base: $195,000
- RSU: $320,000 over 4 years
- Sign-on: $45,000 ($30K year 1, $15K year 2)
- Bonus target: 15%
- Year 1 TC: $195K + $80K + $30K + $29K = $334K

Based on your research, this is below market. You also have a competing offer from Stripe.

**Task**: Write your counter-offer email/script.

**Solution**:

```markdown
# Counter-Offer Script

## Email to Recruiter

Subject: Follow-up on L5 SWE Offer - [Your Name]

Hi [Recruiter Name],

Thank you again for the offer to join Google as an L5 Software
Engineer on the [Team] team. I've had a great experience throughout
the interview process, and I'm genuinely excited about the
opportunity to work on [specific project/technology].

I've reviewed the offer and would like to discuss some adjustments.
Based on my research on levels.fyi and conversations with peers at
Google, as well as the competing offer I have, I believe there's
room to increase the package.

**Current offer:**
- Base: $195,000
- RSU: $320,000 over 4 years
- Sign-on: $45,000
- Year 1 TC: ~$334,000

**My ask:**
- Base: $210,000 (within band for L5)
- RSU: $420,000 over 4 years
- Sign-on: $100,000 (split $60K/$40K)
- Year 1 TC: ~$420,000

**Rationale:**
1. Market data shows L5 engineers with my experience in distributed
   systems typically receive $400K-$480K Year 1 TC.

2. I have a competing offer from Stripe at the Senior level that's
   currently above this offer. Google is my top choice because of
   [specific reason], and I'd like to make this work.

3. My specific experience with [relevant technology] directly
   addresses the challenges on the [Team] team, as [Hiring Manager]
   mentioned.

I'm hoping we can find a way to get closer on compensation. If
there's limited flexibility on base, I'm open to increasing equity
or sign-on to bridge the gap.

Would you be available for a call this week to discuss?

Thank you,
[Your Name]

---

## Phone Script (if recruiter calls)

"Hi [Recruiter], thanks for taking the time to chat. First, I want
to reiterate how excited I am about Google and specifically the
[Team] team. The interview process has been great, and I can really
see myself contributing there.

I've reviewed the offer and done my research. Based on levels.fyi
data and my competing offer, I was hoping we could discuss some
adjustments.

The current package is about $334K Year 1. Based on market data
for L5 with my background, I was expecting something closer to
$420K. Specifically, I'm asking for:
- Base to $210K
- RSU to $420K over 4 years
- Sign-on to $100K

I understand there may be constraints. If base is at the top of
the band, I'm flexible on how we get there - whether through more
equity or a higher sign-on.

I have another offer that I'm seriously considering, but Google
is my first choice. If we can get close on comp, I'm ready to
move forward.

What flexibility do you have?"
```

### Exercise 3: Offer Comparison Decision Matrix

**Scenario**: You have two offers:
- **Company A (Google L5)**: $195K base, $350K RSU (4yr), $50K sign-on, 15% bonus
- **Company B (Startup)**: $180K base, 0.15% equity (current valuation $500M), $25K sign-on, 10% bonus

**Task**: Create a weighted decision matrix and make a recommendation.

**Solution**:

```markdown
# Offer Comparison Decision Matrix

## Step 1: Define Criteria and Weights

| Criteria | Weight | Why This Weight |
|----------|--------|-----------------|
| Total Compensation (Year 1) | 30% | Important but not everything |
| Long-term Upside | 20% | Equity potential matters |
| Learning/Growth | 20% | Career development priority |
| Work-Life Balance | 15% | Quality of life |
| Company Stability | 10% | Risk tolerance |
| Team/Culture Fit | 5% | Hard to evaluate pre-start |

## Step 2: Calculate Compensation

**Company A (Google L5):**
- Year 1: $195K + $87.5K (RSU) + $50K (sign-on) + $29K (bonus) = $361.5K
- Year 2: $195K + $87.5K + $0 + $29K = $311.5K
- Year 3-4: $195K + $87.5K + $0 + $29K = $311.5K
- 4-Year Total: ~$1.3M guaranteed

**Company B (Startup):**
- Year 1: $180K + $18.75K (equity) + $25K (sign-on) + $18K = $241.75K
- Year 2: $180K + $18.75K + $0 + $18K = $216.75K
- Year 3-4: ~$217K/year
- 4-Year Total: ~$893K if company stays flat

**But if startup 5x (IPO at $2.5B):**
- 0.15% = $3.75M (pre-tax)
- 4-Year Total: $893K + $3.75M = $4.6M

## Step 3: Score Each Criteria (1-10)

| Criteria | Google | Score | Startup | Score |
|----------|--------|-------|---------|-------|
| Year 1 TC | $362K | 9 | $242K | 6 |
| Long-term Upside | Moderate refreshers | 6 | Potential 10x | 9 |
| Learning/Growth | Good, structured | 7 | Fast-paced, broad | 8 |
| Work-Life Balance | Good | 7 | Typical startup | 5 |
| Company Stability | High | 9 | Series C, moderate | 6 |
| Team/Culture Fit | Met 8 people, good | 7 | Met 5 people, excited | 8 |

## Step 4: Calculate Weighted Scores

**Google:**
- TC: 9 × 0.30 = 2.7
- Upside: 6 × 0.20 = 1.2
- Learning: 7 × 0.20 = 1.4
- WLB: 7 × 0.15 = 1.05
- Stability: 9 × 0.10 = 0.9
- Culture: 7 × 0.05 = 0.35
- **Total: 7.6**

**Startup:**
- TC: 6 × 0.30 = 1.8
- Upside: 9 × 0.20 = 1.8
- Learning: 8 × 0.20 = 1.6
- WLB: 5 × 0.15 = 0.75
- Stability: 6 × 0.10 = 0.6
- Culture: 8 × 0.05 = 0.4
- **Total: 6.95**

## Step 5: Recommendation

**Analysis:**
Google scores higher (7.6 vs 6.95), primarily due to:
- 50% higher guaranteed Year 1 compensation
- Better work-life balance
- Higher stability

Startup could be better if:
- You have high risk tolerance
- You believe in the company's 5x+ potential
- You prioritize learning breadth over compensation

**Recommendation:**
Choose Google if you value stability and guaranteed comp.
Choose Startup if you're early career and can afford the risk.

**Negotiation Action:**
Before deciding, try to negotiate Startup's base to $200K to
close the Year 1 gap. If successful, recalculate.
```

### Exercise 4: Handling a Counteroffer Scenario

**Scenario**: You've accepted an offer from Company B and submitted your resignation. Your current employer (Company A) counters with a 20% raise, promotion to Senior Engineer, and says they'll start your green card process.

**Task**: Write a script for handling this situation and make a decision.

**Solution**:

```markdown
# Counteroffer Analysis and Response

## Step 1: Evaluate the Counteroffer

**Company A Counter:**
- 20% raise: $150K → $180K (matches Company B base)
- Promotion: Engineer → Senior Engineer
- Green card: Significant long-term value ($20-50K in process costs)

**Key Questions:**
1. Why wasn't this offered before I resigned?
2. Will my relationship with management be damaged?
3. Do the underlying issues remain (why I was leaving)?
4. Is the promotion real scope change or just title?
5. What's the attrition rate for people who accept counteroffers?

## Step 2: Decision Framework

**Reasons I was leaving Company A:**
1. ❌ Limited technical growth - Counter doesn't address
2. ❌ Small team, no mentorship - Counter doesn't address
3. ✅ Below-market compensation - Counter addresses
4. ❌ Unclear path to Staff - Counter partially addresses

**Company B advantages:**
1. ✅ Larger engineering team
2. ✅ Clear Staff Engineer path
3. ✅ Cutting-edge technology
4. ✅ Competitive compensation

**Risk Assessment:**
- 50-80% of counteroffer accepters leave within 12 months
- Trust may be damaged with current manager
- Green card could take 2-5 years (ties me to company)

## Step 3: Response Script

**If Declining Counter (Recommended):**

"Thank you for this generous counteroffer. It means a lot that you
value my contributions and want me to stay. I've thought about
this carefully.

My decision to leave wasn't primarily about compensation. While
the raise and promotion are meaningful, I've been seeking
[technical growth/larger team/new challenges] that I believe
I'll find at [Company B].

I'm grateful for everything I've learned here and want to make
this transition as smooth as possible. I'll document all my
projects thoroughly and am happy to help train my replacement.

This isn't an easy decision, but I believe it's the right one
for my career at this stage. I hope we can stay in touch, and
I wish the team continued success."

**If Accepting Counter (Risky):**

"I really appreciate this offer and the commitment it shows.
Before I accept, I'd like to understand:

1. Can we document the promotion and compensation in writing?
2. What's the timeline and commitment on the green card process?
3. What specific growth opportunities will I have in this new role?
4. How can we ensure our relationship remains strong going forward?

If we can align on these, I'd be willing to withdraw my
resignation. But I need these assurances in writing before
I make any changes to my plans."

## Step 4: Final Decision

**My recommendation: Decline the counteroffer.**

Reasons:
1. Core issues (growth, team size) aren't addressed
2. Trust damage is likely
3. Already committed to Company B
4. Green card is a tool to keep me, not a genuine benefit

**Action items:**
1. Politely decline counter with script above
2. Commit to strong handoff
3. Maintain professional relationship
4. Don't burn bridges
```

### Junior → FAANG: Getting the Most Out of These Exercises

The exercises in this section are most effective when you treat them as rehearsal rather than reading comprehension. Do not read the solution before completing your own version. The deliberate struggle of producing your own compensation target sheet, counter-offer email, and decision matrix is where the learning happens. Reading the solution directly gives you a false sense of readiness.

Exercise 1 prerequisite: before building your compensation target sheet, you need real data. Spend 30 minutes on levels.fyi for each company you are targeting, and add one data point from a network contact who has interviewed or worked there recently. Aggregate data is useful. One human data point from someone with your background is more useful.

Exercise 2 is the most practically valuable for first-time negotiators because writing the counter-offer email before you have a real offer removes the performance anxiety from the task. When the real offer arrives, you have already done the cognitive work. You are editing a template, not drafting from scratch under pressure.

Exercise 3 teaches a decision-making discipline that most junior engineers do not develop until their second or third job change. Building the weighted matrix once, even for a hypothetical scenario, installs the habit. The specific weights you choose for your first real decision matrix will be wrong, but having a structured process is more valuable than optimizing the weights.

Exercise 4 is emotionally the hardest exercise because it requires you to simulate declining an offer that feels flattering. Practice the declination script out loud. The words need to come out of your mouth naturally before you need them in a real conversation.

### Senior @ FAANG: Using the Exercises as Calibration Tools for Senior-Level Decision-Making

Senior candidates should treat these exercises not as skill-building for basic negotiation tactics but as calibration tools for the more complex decisions they actually face.

Exercise 1 extended: at the L6 level, your compensation target sheet should include a four-year model, not a year-one model. Build columns for Years 1 through 4 for each company, incorporating the actual vesting schedule, your estimate of the annual refresher grant at strong performance, and the after-tax value given your state tax rate. This four-year view often reverses the apparent ranking of offers that look similar in Year 1.

Exercise 2 extended: the counter-offer email in the solution is appropriate for junior-to-senior candidates. For staff-level candidates, rewrite the email in a more peer-to-peer register that acknowledges the company's internal process. "I would like to request that this be escalated for review given my specific background in distributed systems at scale" is a staff-level ask. "Can you increase this number" is not.

Exercise 3 extended: rebuild the decision matrix for your actual current decision, not the hypothetical one. Replace the generic criteria with the criteria that actually matter for your career stage. At the L6 level, likely criteria include: technical surface area of the role, cross-team influence scope, quality of the engineering organization's technical bar, manager track record of sponsoring staff promotions, and the team's roadmap visibility within the company. Weight these against compensation and run the model.

Exercise 4 extended: the counteroffer analysis in the solution uses a binary framework (decline vs accept). For senior candidates, a third option often exists: use the counteroffer to accelerate a conversation that was already overdue. If your current employer offers you a promotion and a raise, and you believe the offer is genuine and addresses the root cause of your search, the correct answer may be to accept and simultaneously withdraw from the external process with professional grace. The key governance rule: never accept a counteroffer that does not address the specific reason you started your search in the first place, in writing, with a named owner and a documented timeline.
