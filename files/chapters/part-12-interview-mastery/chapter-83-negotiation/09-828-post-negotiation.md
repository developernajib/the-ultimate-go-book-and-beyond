## 83.8 Post-Negotiation

The negotiation is not truly over when you shake hands or say "I accept" on the phone. The post-negotiation phase, from verifying your offer letter to giving notice at your current job, has its own set of important steps that protect you and set you up for a strong start.

### Getting It in Writing

Verbal promises made during negotiation calls are not binding. The only terms that matter are the ones printed in your official offer letter. Before you accept, carefully verify that every component you negotiated, including base salary, equity grant details, signing bonus amounts and payment schedule, and any special terms, appears correctly in the written document. If something is missing or different from what was discussed, raise it immediately before signing.

```
WHAT TO VERIFY IN YOUR OFFER LETTER:

□ Base salary (annual amount)
□ Equity grant (number of shares/units, not just dollar value)
□ Vesting schedule
□ Stock price used for valuation (if relevant)
□ Signing bonus amount and payment timing
□ Clawback clause on signing bonus (if any)
□ Bonus target percentage
□ Start date
□ Job title and level
□ Any negotiated special terms
□ At-will employment status
□ Non-compete terms (if any)
```

### Giving Notice

Resigning professionally is important because the tech industry has a long memory. Your current manager, teammates, and colleagues will remember how you handled your departure, and you may cross paths with them again at future companies, conferences, or as references. Keep your resignation brief, positive, and focused on helping with the transition. Never use your resignation as an opportunity to air grievances or gloat about your new offer.

```
TIMELINE:
- Accept offer and get written confirmation
- Plan your resignation conversation
- Give notice (2 weeks standard in US)
- Offer to help with transition
- Document your projects

RESIGNATION SCRIPT:
"I wanted to let you know that I've accepted a position at
another company and will be resigning. My last day will be
[date]. I'm grateful for the opportunities I've had here
and want to make this transition as smooth as possible.
I'm happy to help document my projects and train whoever
takes over my work."

KEEP IT POSITIVE:
✓ Express gratitude
✓ Offer to help transition
✓ Stay professional (you may need the reference)
✗ Don't badmouth or burn bridges
✗ Don't gloat about your new offer
✗ Don't check out during your notice period
```

### Junior → FAANG: What to Actually Do After You Shake Hands

The verbal agreement is not the offer. This distinction matters more than it sounds. Recruiters sometimes move faster than their own administrative process, and terms that were agreed verbally can arrive in the written letter differently, through honest error or through deliberate test of your attention.

Prerequisite gap: most junior candidates do not know what to look for in an offer letter beyond base salary. The checklist in this section exists for this reason. Read every line, including the clawback clause on the signing bonus (which typically requires repayment if you leave within 12 to 24 months), the at-will employment language, and the IP assignment scope (some clauses are written broadly enough to claim ownership of personal projects).

Non-compete review: most US states outside California do not enforce non-competes against employees, but the clause can still create practical friction and legal costs. If the offer letter includes a non-compete, ask specifically about its geographic and temporal scope before signing.

Resignation habit: keep your resignation conversation to under five minutes, send a formal resignation email immediately afterward for documentation, and offer a specific transition plan. Never resign verbally without a written follow-up.

### Senior @ FAANG: Post-Negotiation Protocol as a Platform Concern

Senior candidates completing a post-negotiation phase have a longer checklist than the one in this section, because the stakes and the complexity are higher.

Offer letter audit at staff level: at L6 and above, the equity components often include performance-based refresh schedules, spot bonus eligibility, and committee-level calibration clauses. Read the equity award agreement, not just the offer summary. The award agreement is the legally binding document, and it may differ from the summary in material ways around exercise periods, clawback conditions under a "for cause" termination, and change-of-control acceleration.

IP assignment scope is a governance concern for staff engineers who consult, advise, or contribute to open source. At many large companies, the IP assignment agreement claims any invention that is "related to the company's business or anticipated business." If you maintain open source projects or advise startups, negotiate a carve-out for specific activities before signing, and get the carve-out in writing.

Resignation at senior levels involves more complexity because you likely have cross-team dependencies, projects in flight, and institutional knowledge that cannot be transferred in two weeks. Offer a four to six week transition, create written handoff documentation, and identify a named successor for each critical responsibility. This is not generosity. It is professional governance and reputation management. The engineers you hand off to will remember the quality of your transition, and the tech industry is a small world at senior levels.

Counter-resignation dynamic: when a senior or staff engineer resigns, the probability of a retention conversation from a skip-level or executive is high. Prepare for this conversation in advance. Know what, if anything, would actually change your mind, and be honest about it. "The only thing that would change my decision is a Staff promotion with documented scope expansion" is a legitimate and professional answer. "I need a few more days to think" without a concrete ask is not.

Post-signing: once you have signed, notify all other companies in your pipeline promptly and professionally. The person you decline graciously at Company B may be the hiring manager who recruits you at Company C in three years. Every interaction at this level is a long-horizon reputation event.
