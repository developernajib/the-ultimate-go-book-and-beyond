## 83.9 Common Mistakes

Even well-prepared candidates make avoidable errors during negotiation. The mistakes listed below are the ones that compensation coaches and recruiters see most frequently. Each one has a simple fix, but you need to be aware of the pattern before you can avoid it. Read through this list before you enter any negotiation so the warning signs are fresh in your mind.

```
NEGOTIATION MISTAKES TO AVOID:

1. ACCEPTING IMMEDIATELY
   "I'd like to take 24-48 hours to review everything."

2. NEGOTIATING ONLY BASE SALARY
   Consider: equity, signing bonus, level, start date, PTO

3. NOT HAVING DATA
   "Based on my research at levels.fyi..."

4. MAKING ULTIMATUMS
   "I'd love to work together on reaching a number we're
   both happy with."

5. LYING ABOUT OTHER OFFERS
   Be honest. It's a small industry.

6. NEGOTIATING AFTER ACCEPTING
   Once you accept, you're done. Negotiate before.

7. BEING ADVERSARIAL
   Maintain positive relationship with recruiter.

8. OVER-NEGOTIATING
   Know when to stop. Don't nickel-and-dime.

9. FORGETTING THE BIG PICTURE
   Best compensation doesn't always mean best opportunity.

10. NOT GETTING IT IN WRITING
    Verbal promises mean nothing without documentation.
```

---

### Junior → FAANG: The Three Mistakes That Cost Junior Candidates the Most Money

Of the ten mistakes listed above, three disproportionately hurt early-career candidates because they stem from the same root cause: not believing you deserve to negotiate.

Accepting immediately is the most expensive single mistake. Recruiter calls are designed to generate excitement, and excitement generates immediate acceptance. The 24-to-48-hour ask is not a stalling tactic. It is standard professional practice at every level. Any recruiter who treats a reasonable review request as a negative signal is showing you something important about how the company operates.

Not having data is the second highest-cost mistake for junior candidates. Levels.fyi is free and takes 30 minutes to research. Walking into a negotiation without market data is walking in without a basis for your position. The recruiter has data. You need to have data too.

Not negotiating equity is the third. Junior candidates often treat the stock grant as abstract future money. At FAANG companies, the RSU grant over four years frequently exceeds the signing bonus by a factor of three to five. Negotiating the RSU up by $50,000 over four years is worth more than negotiating base up by $5,000 per year.

### Senior @ FAANG: The Subtle Mistakes That Derail Senior Candidates

Senior candidates make different mistakes than junior ones. The ten listed in this section are real, but the ones that most commonly damage experienced candidates are more nuanced.

Over-negotiating is a real risk at the staff level. Staff engineers are being evaluated on their judgment throughout the hiring process, and a negotiation that feels adversarial, drags beyond two rounds of counter, or demands incremental improvements on every single component sends a signal about how you will operate on the team. Know when you have extracted the value available and close professionally.

Negotiating against yourself happens when a candidate volunteers concessions that were not requested. "I know the equity is at the top of the band, so I am okay with the current amount" is negotiating against yourself. Wait for the company to tell you what the constraints are before you preemptively accept them.

Accepting comp without understanding the performance modifier is a common mistake at senior levels. At Google, Meta, and Amazon, your annual comp is a function of your base and equity multiplied by a performance rating. An L6 engineer rated "Exceeds Expectations" can earn 40-60% more in total compensation than an L6 rated "Meets Expectations" due to refresher grants and bonus multipliers. Ask during the negotiation what the expected distribution of ratings looks like on the team and what the typical refresher grant is for a strong performer. This is not pessimistic. It is how staff engineers think about the financial model of their employment.

Governance failure: the mistake candidates least often discuss is failing to document the negotiation. Keep a written record of every conversation, including the date, who you spoke with, and what was agreed. Send a follow-up email after every call that confirms the terms discussed. This is the only protection you have if the offer letter arrives with discrepancies.

Bar-raiser signal: the candidates who navigate post-offer negotiation most effectively treat it as a collaborative problem-solving exercise rather than an adversarial transaction. "Help me understand what flexibility exists and where the constraints are" produces better outcomes than positional bargaining, because it invites the recruiter to be your advocate rather than your opponent.
