## 83.5 Special Negotiation Scenarios

Not every negotiation follows the standard base-equity-bonus pattern. Some situations require specialized knowledge and different tactics. This section covers the most common special scenarios you will encounter: negotiating equity at public companies versus startups, pushing for a higher level, and adjusting your start date.

### Negotiating Equity

Equity is often the largest and most negotiable component of a tech compensation package, yet many candidates spend all their energy negotiating base salary and barely glance at the stock grant. At public companies, RSUs are deferred cash with some stock price risk. At startups, options are a bet on the company's future that could be worth nothing or could be life-changing. The questions you ask and the points you negotiate differ significantly between these two scenarios.

```
FOR PUBLIC COMPANY RSUs:

Questions to ask:
- What's the current stock price used for the grant?
- What's the vesting schedule (4-year with 1-year cliff typical)?
- Are there refresher grants? What's typical?

Negotiation points:
- Request higher initial grant
- Ask about accelerated vesting in first year
- Discuss refresher grant expectations

EXAMPLE:
"The equity component is important to me. I noticed the
grant is valued at today's stock price. Given market
volatility, would you consider adding 10-15% more shares
to provide some buffer? Alternatively, could we discuss
a signing bonus that compensates for this risk?"


FOR STARTUP OPTIONS:

Questions to ask:
- What's the current 409A valuation?
- What's the strike price?
- What's the total share count (for % ownership)?
- What's the company's fundraising history and runway?
- What's the exercise window if I leave?

Negotiation points:
- Request more shares
- Ask for extended exercise window (90 days is standard, 5-10 years is better)
- Discuss acceleration on acquisition/IPO

EXAMPLE:
"I'm excited about the equity component. I noticed the
standard exercise window is 90 days. Given the investment
I'd be making in the company's success, would you consider
a 5-year exercise window? This is becoming common at
competitive startups."
```

### Negotiating Level

Level determines your compensation band, the scope of work you are expected to perform, and your long-term career trajectory at a company. Being underleveled by even one step can cost you hundreds of thousands of dollars over several years, because promotions at large companies often take 18-24 months and are never guaranteed. If you believe you are being placed at a level below what your experience and the role's scope warrant, it is worth raising the issue directly.

```
WHEN TO NEGOTIATE LEVEL:

- If you believe you're being underleveled based on experience
- If the level significantly impacts long-term compensation
- If the scope of the role matches a higher level

APPROACH:

"I'm excited about the role, but I wanted to discuss the
leveling. Based on my conversation with the team, the
scope of this position - leading the platform architecture
and mentoring 3 engineers - seems to align with what I
understand to be Staff Engineer responsibilities.

I've been operating at a similar scope in my current role.
Is there flexibility to consider this at the Staff level,
or alternatively, could we discuss a clear path to Staff
with specific milestones within the first 6 months?"

NOTE: This is harder to negotiate. Focus on demonstrating
the scope matches the level, not just years of experience.
```

### Negotiating Start Date

Your start date might seem like a minor detail, but it affects your mental readiness, your transition from your current employer, and sometimes even your compensation. Starting too quickly can mean a messy handoff at your current job, while a delayed start gives you time to decompress, relocate if needed, and begin your new role fully focused. Most companies are flexible on start dates as long as you provide a reasonable justification and a specific date.

```
STANDARD TIMELINE:
- 2 weeks notice at current job (US standard)
- 1 week gap for rest
- Start date 3 weeks from accepting

REASONS TO NEGOTIATE LONGER:
- Finishing critical project
- Already scheduled travel/time off
- Relocation logistics
- Need for decompression

EXAMPLE:
"I want to give my current team time to transition my
projects properly. Would a start date of [4 weeks out]
work? I want to make sure I join [New Company] fully
focused and ready to contribute."

WHEN THEY PUSH FOR SOONER:
"I understand the urgency. If starting sooner is important,
could we discuss adding a few days of PTO to my first month
so I can handle the personal obligations I'm deferring?"
```

### Junior → FAANG: Navigating Equity, Level, and Start-Date Asks for the First Time

Most early-career candidates treat the offer letter as a fixed document. The mindset shift that unlocks better outcomes is recognizing that equity, level, and start date are all negotiable, often independently of base salary.

Equity prerequisite gap: if you cannot explain the difference between an ISO and an RSU, or what a 409A valuation is, learn those definitions before the negotiation call. Showing up without that vocabulary signals you will accept whatever framing the recruiter uses.

Level negotiation entry point: do not argue using years of experience. Argue using scope. Read the job description carefully, map specific bullets to the level framework for that company (Google's L4 vs L5, Amazon's L5 vs L6), and ask: "The role involves setting technical direction for the platform team. On your level framework, does that map to Senior or Staff?" Asking the question shifts the burden of proof to them.

Start-date habit: always ask for more time than you think you need. Companies expect a counter-request, and the worst answer is no. A three-to-four week start date gives you breathing room for notice periods, decompression, and any personal logistics without burning goodwill.

### Senior @ FAANG: Strategic Equity, Level Governance, and Timing as a Staff-Level Candidate

Senior and staff candidates should treat every component of the offer as a distinct negotiation track, each with its own lever and its own approval chain inside the company.

Equity governance matters here. At L6 and above, RSU grants often require VP or SVP approval to exceed the band ceiling. Knowing this changes your tactics: instead of asking the recruiter to "increase the grant," you can ask them to "escalate to see if there is flexibility above the standard band given my specific background in X." That framing signals you understand how the internal process works, which recruiters respect because it makes their job easier.

Level ADR framing: if you believe you are underleveled, write a one-page case before the call. Structure it as an architectural decision record: the options considered (L5 vs L6), the evidence for each (scope, cross-team surface, IC vs management responsibilities), the trade-offs, and the recommendation. Sending this document to the recruiter before the conversation is unusual and highly effective at the staff level because it demonstrates you operate at the level you are claiming.

Start-date negotiation at senior levels also affects unvested equity from your current employer. Calculate the exact dollar value of unvested grants on a weekly basis. A two-week delay in start date can save or cost tens of thousands of dollars. Use that math transparently: "My current unvested RSU schedule shows $X vesting on date Y. A start date after Y is worth $X to me. If you cannot offer that start date, could we add a sign-on component that bridges that gap?"

Platform thinking: the level you negotiate into at a large company sets your title for potentially two to three years, because promotion cycles at L6 and above are 18-36 months and involve committee review. Being accurate about your level at entry is a governance decision, not just a financial one. Entering at the wrong level either caps your scope or sets you up to fail a bar that was calibrated for someone more senior.
