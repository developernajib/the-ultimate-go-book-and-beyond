## 83.17 Interview Questions: Compensation and Negotiation

During the interview process, recruiters and hiring managers will ask questions that are directly related to compensation and your motivations for changing jobs. How you answer these questions sets the stage for the negotiation that follows. This section provides frameworks for the four most common compensation-related interview questions, with strategies for each scenario you might face.

### Question 1: "What are your salary expectations?"

**Why They Ask**: To filter candidates, anchor the negotiation, determine budget fit.

**Framework for Answering:**

```markdown
# Response Strategies

## Best: Deflect and redirect
"I'm focused on finding the right role and company fit. I'm
confident we can work out compensation if we're both excited.
Could you share the budget or range for this position?"

## Alternative: Research-based range
"Based on my research at levels.fyi and conversations with
peers, this role typically ranges from $X to $Y in total
compensation. Given my experience with [relevant skill], I'd
be looking at the upper end of that range."

## If pushed hard: High anchor with flexibility
"I'm targeting total compensation in the $X-$Y range, which
includes base, equity, and bonus. I'm open to discussing how
we structure that based on the company's compensation philosophy."

## Red flags to avoid:
- Giving a single number
- Anchoring low out of fear
- Saying "I'm flexible" without context
- Revealing current salary (in many states, they can't ask)
```

### Question 2: "Why are you leaving your current job?"

**Why They Ask**: To understand your motivations and whether you're running from something.

**Framework for Answering:**

```markdown
# Response Framework

## Structure: Push (from current) + Pull (to new)
Focus 80% on pull, 20% on push. Never badmouth current employer.

## Strong Answer Example:
"I've had a great experience at [Current Company], but I'm
looking for [specific growth opportunity]. [New Company]'s
work on [specific technology/product] directly aligns with
where I want to develop my career.

Additionally, the scope of this role - [specific responsibilities] - 
is the kind of challenge I'm ready for. I'm excited about the
opportunity to [specific contribution you'd make]."

## What NOT to say:
- "I hate my manager" (red flag: conflict)
- "They don't pay enough" (makes you seem money-motivated only)
- "The work is boring" (suggests you'll get bored here too)
- "I got passed over for promotion" (could be performance issue)

## Compensation-related honest answer:
"I'm looking to grow into a [next level] role with the compensation
that reflects that growth. I've done the research and believe
my market value is [X]. I'm excited that [Company] recognizes
and rewards talent competitively."
```

### Question 3: "Do you have any other offers we should know about?"

**Why They Ask**: To gauge urgency, calibrate offer, understand competition.

**Framework for Answering:**

```markdown
# Response Strategies

## If you have competing offers (ideal):
"Yes, I'm fortunate to be in final stages with a few companies.
I have an offer from [Company X] that I'm evaluating. My timeline
is [specific date]. [Your Company] is my top choice because of
[specific reason], but I want to be thoughtful about my decision."

## If you don't have other offers:
"I'm focused on finding the right opportunity, so I'm being
selective about where I interview. I'm early in my search but
have interest from other companies. What's your typical timeline
from offer to decision?"

## If asked about competing offer details:
"I want to be respectful of the other company's confidentiality.
I can share that it's competitive in the [Senior/Staff] range.
I'm more interested in discussing what [Your Company] can offer
and ensuring we're both excited about moving forward."

## Key principles:
- Never lie about having offers you don't have
- Don't reveal specific numbers from other offers
- Use offers to accelerate timeline, not pressure unfairly
- Make clear this company is a top choice (if true)
```

### Question 4: "What's most important to you in your next role?"

**Why They Ask**: To understand your priorities and tailor the pitch.

**Framework for Answering:**

```markdown
# Response Framework

## Structure: 3 priorities, ranked
Be honest but strategic. If compensation is #1, don't lead with it.

## Strong Answer Example:
"Three things are most important to me:

1. **Technical challenge**: I want to work on problems that stretch
   me. The distributed systems work at [Company] fits perfectly.

2. **Growth opportunity**: I'm looking to grow into a [next level]
   role in the next 2-3 years, and I want a manager and company
   that will support that.

3. **Competitive compensation**: I've done my research and have a
   clear sense of my market value. I want to be compensated fairly
   for the value I bring.

This role checks all three boxes, which is why I'm excited."

## How this helps negotiation:
- Establishes you care about more than money (makes you seem
  genuine)
- But signals compensation matters (sets up negotiation)
- Shows you've done research (harder to lowball)
```

### Junior → FAANG: Handling Compensation Questions Before You Have an Offer

The four questions in this section arrive during the screening and interview process, before you have any negotiating position at all. The goal at this stage is not to negotiate. It is to avoid anchoring yourself low and to build the recruiter's confidence that you are a serious, market-aware candidate.

"What are your salary expectations?" is the most dangerous question for junior candidates because the instinct is to answer it. The best answer is a redirect. Practice this until it is reflexive: "I would prefer to focus on fit first. Could you share the budget for this role?" If they push past the redirect twice, give a range based on your research anchored at the upper end.

"Why are you leaving?" has a compensation-related version that you should prepare specifically: "I am looking for a role that matches my market value." This is honest, forward-looking, and does not require you to criticize your current employer. Practice connecting this to the specific opportunity: "I want to work on problems at the scale that this team operates at, and I want to be compensated competitively for the impact I can deliver there."

Competing offers: if you have one, say so without revealing the number. "I am fortunate to be in final stages with another company" is enough information. It establishes you are a real candidate in a real process without creating an obligation to match a specific number.

Habit to build: maintain a running document of your market research from the day you start your job search. By the time these questions arrive, you should already have three to five data points from levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and your network. Having the data removes the anxiety of answering compensation questions because you are stating research, not guessing.

### Senior @ FAANG: The Compensation Question Subtext at the Staff Level

For L6 and above candidates, these four questions have a layer of subtext that junior candidates do not face. At the staff level, the recruiter is not just calibrating your compensation expectations. They are evaluating whether your self-assessment matches your interview performance.

"What are your salary expectations?" at the staff level is also a check on whether your expectations are calibrated to the level you are interviewing for. A candidate who names a number in the L4 range while interviewing for an L6 role creates doubt about their self-awareness. A candidate who names a number above the L6 band creates budget concerns. The right answer at this level is a research-backed range with an explicit level reference: "For an L6 role with this scope in this market, my research shows the range is $X to $Y in total comp. I am targeting the upper portion of that range given my background in Z."

"Why are you leaving?" at the staff level often reveals how the candidate thinks about scope and impact. The best answers name something specific and positive about the destination: a specific technical domain, a specific organizational challenge, a specific mission. Answers that are primarily about leaving (compensation, politics, boredom) are a yellow flag for interviewers at the staff level because they raise the question of whether the candidate is reactive rather than proactive in their career management.

"What is most important to you?" is a bar-raiser question at the staff level. The interviewer is not asking about your personal priorities. They are evaluating whether your stated priorities are consistent with operating at L6. An answer that ranks "financial compensation" first without context reads as junior. An answer that ranks "organizational leverage and scope to solve ambiguous problems" first, with compensation acknowledged as a necessary constraint, reads as senior. The content is less important than the structure of the reasoning.

Governance over these answers: prepare written drafts of your answers to all four questions before any recruiter call and review them the day before each conversation. This is not about being scripted. It is about having thought through your position carefully enough that you can state it clearly under the mild pressure of a screening call. Staff engineers who have not prepared often give inconsistent answers across multiple recruiter conversations at the same company, which creates noise in the hiring process and occasionally surfaces as a concern in the debrief.
