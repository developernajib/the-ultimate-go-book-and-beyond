## 83F.14 Post-Acceptance: Your First 90 Days

Offer accepted. The interview preparation is over, but a new kind of preparation begins. Your first 90 days at a FAANG company set the trajectory for your entire tenure. Here is how to make them count.

### Surviving the Ramp-Up Period

**Weeks 1-2: Orientation and Setup**

Every FAANG company has a structured onboarding program. Your job in these first two weeks is to absorb, not produce.

| Priority | Activity |
|----------|----------|
| 1 | Complete all onboarding training and compliance requirements |
| 2 | Set up your development environment (IDE, repos, build tools, VPN) |
| 3 | Read your team's documentation (design docs, runbooks, README files) |
| 4 | Meet every person on your immediate team (1:1 coffee chats) |
| 5 | Understand the on-call rotation and incident response process |
| 6 | Identify the 2-3 people who will be your go-to mentors |

**Weeks 3-6: First Contributions**

| Priority | Activity |
|----------|----------|
| 1 | Pick up small, well-defined starter tasks (bug fixes, documentation updates) |
| 2 | Submit your first code review and request reviews from senior teammates |
| 3 | Attend all team meetings and start asking informed questions |
| 4 | Build your understanding of the codebase through reading, not just writing |
| 5 | Start a personal "learnings" doc - capture architectural decisions, team norms, gotchas |

**Weeks 7-12: Increasing Scope**

| Priority | Activity |
|----------|----------|
| 1 | Take ownership of a small feature or project |
| 2 | Write a design document for your first project (get feedback from your manager) |
| 3 | Participate in code reviews for others (learn the codebase through reviewing) |
| 4 | Start contributing to team discussions and planning meetings |
| 5 | Identify areas where you can add unique value based on your background |

### Building Relationships in Your New Team

**The people you must build relationships with:**

| Person | Why | How |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **Your manager** | Sets expectations, provides feedback, controls promotion | Weekly 1:1s, be transparent about progress and blockers |
| **Your tech lead** | Guides technical decisions, reviews your code | Ask for code review, seek advice on design decisions |
| **Your onboarding buddy** | Answers "dumb" questions without judgment | Use them liberally in weeks 1-4 |
| **Senior team members** | Know the codebase and history | Ask about why things were built certain ways |
| **Cross-team partners** | Depend on or support your team's work | Introduce yourself, understand dependencies |

### Setting Expectations with Your Manager

In your first 1:1 (ideally within your first week), ask these questions:

1. "What does success look like for me in the first 90 days?"
2. "What are the most important projects for our team this quarter?"
3. "How do you prefer to communicate? (Slack, email, in-person, scheduled 1:1s)"
4. "What should I prioritize learning first?"
5. "Who on the team should I reach out to for different types of questions?"
6. "When is my first performance review, and what will it evaluate?"

### First Project Selection Strategy

Your first project matters more than you think. It sets your reputation.

**Choose a project that is:**
- Well-scoped (clear requirements, defined timeline)
- Visible (used by the team, not a throwaway script)
- Achievable (you can deliver it within 4-6 weeks)
- Stretching (requires learning something new about the codebase or technology)

**Avoid:**
- Massive refactors (too risky for a new hire)
- Deeply entrenched legacy code (political and technical landmines)
- Projects that depend heavily on knowledge you do not have yet
- "Innovation" projects that are not aligned with team priorities

### Avoiding Common New-Hire Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| Trying to change everything in Week 1 | You see things that could be better | Keep a list. Wait 90 days. Then propose changes with context. |
| Not asking enough questions | Fear of looking incompetent | Everyone expects new hires to ask questions. Not asking is the red flag. |
| Working alone instead of collaborating | Interview mode is individual, work mode is collaborative | Seek code reviews, pair programming, and design discussions proactively |
| Comparing to your previous company | "At my last company, we did it this way..." | Learn the new company's way first. Suggest improvements later, diplomatically. |
| Over-working to prove yourself | Imposter syndrome, fear of being "found out" | Sustainable pace > heroic sprints. Your manager wants consistency over 6 months, not a burnt-out engineer at month 3. |
| Ignoring non-coding work | Engineers value code, companies value impact | Documentation, process improvement, mentoring - these all count. |

### Planning for Your First Performance Review

FAANG performance reviews typically happen at 6-month or annual intervals. Start preparing from Day 1.

**Keep a "brag document" (weekly):**
- What you shipped this week
- Code reviews you gave or received
- Design discussions you participated in
- Problems you solved (technical and non-technical)
- Feedback you received (positive and constructive)
- Things you learned

**This document serves three purposes:**
1. Self-review input when review season arrives (you will forget what you did 4 months ago)
2. Manager conversation material for 1:1s
3. Promotion case evidence (start building the case from Day 1)


### Junior → FAANG: Your First 90 Days at FAANG

The first 90 days at a FAANG company are a trust-building period. The common mistake is trying to demonstrate expertise immediately by proposing large changes. The more effective approach: spend the first 30 days learning (codebase, team norms, deployment process, on-call rotation, communication culture), spend days 30-60 contributing incrementally (ship one small feature with high code quality, run point on one on-call incident to completion), and spend days 60-90 beginning to form a view on where you can contribute most in the coming quarter.

### Senior @ FAANG: First 90 Days at the Senior Level: Building Credibility Through Contribution

Senior engineers at FAANG have higher expectations for onboarding speed because they are expected to contribute at staff scope within 6 months. The 90-day strategy: build relationships broadly (coffee chats with 15-20 engineers across the team and adjacent teams in the first 30 days), identify the team's top engineering problems (ask the manager and tech lead directly: 'what are the three biggest technical problems this team has that are not currently being addressed?'), and choose one problem to own completely in the first quarter (design the solution, drive the implementation, present the results). This ownership signal establishes your operating mode as a senior engineer in the team's mental model. Staff-level engineers should also identify the cross-team collaboration opportunities in the first 90 days: which adjacent teams does your team work with frequently, where are the API contract or process friction points, and which cross-team problem would benefit most from senior-level attention?
