## 83G.9 Between Rounds: The Reset Protocol

In a full-day onsite (4--6 interviews), the break between rounds is critical. What you do in those 5--15 minutes determines whether you carry momentum or carry baggage into the next round.

### The 5-Minute Reset Between Sessions

```
BETWEEN-ROUNDS RESET PROTOCOL (5 minutes)

Minute 0-1: PHYSICAL RESET
  - Stand up and stretch
  - Bathroom break (even if you don't need it --- the walk helps)
  - Drink water
  - Eat a small snack if available

Minute 1-2: MENTAL RELEASE
  - The previous round is over. You cannot change it.
  - Whatever happened --- good or bad --- is done.
  - Do NOT replay the round in your head.
  - Do NOT calculate whether you "passed" or "failed."

Minute 2-3: PREPARE FOR NEXT ROUND
  - What type is the next round? (Coding, design, behavioral?)
  - Remind yourself of the format and your playbook
  - Glance at your quick reference card if appropriate

Minute 3-4: CENTERING
  - Three deep breaths
  - Recall one thing you did well today (any round)
  - "I am prepared for this."

Minute 4-5: ACTIVATE
  - Smile
  - Sit up straight
  - The next interviewer is a blank slate. Fresh start.
```

### Letting Go of the Previous Round

This is the hardest skill in a full-day interview. You just bombed a coding round --- you could not finish the problem, your code had bugs, the interviewer seemed disappointed. Now you have 5 minutes before a system design interview.

If you carry the stress from the coding round into the design round, you will underperform in both. The coding round is already scored. Nothing you think about it now will change the outcome. But spiraling about it will damage the next round.

**The Mental Release Technique:**

1. Acknowledge the feeling: "That round was tough. I'm disappointed."
2. Accept it: "I can't change it now."
3. Reframe it: "The hiring committee evaluates all rounds together. One bad round doesn't mean a rejection."
4. Release it: Physically exhale as if pushing the experience out.
5. Redirect: "The next round is a fresh start with a new interviewer."

This is not denial. You are not pretending the round went well. You are compartmentalizing so that the next round gets a fair chance.

### Energy Management Across 5--6 Hours

A full onsite is an endurance event. Your cognitive performance will decline throughout the day unless you actively manage your energy.

```
ENERGY MANAGEMENT ACROSS A FULL-DAY ONSITE

8:00 AM   Full energy. Use this for the hardest round if you have a choice.
           Schedule coding interviews early if possible.

10:00 AM  Still sharp. Good for system design (requires sustained focus).

12:00 PM  Lunch break. EAT. Even if you're not hungry.
           Complex carbs + protein. Avoid sugar crash.
           Walk outside for 5-10 minutes if possible.

1:00 PM   Post-lunch dip. This is the danger zone.
           This is often when behavioral rounds are scheduled (less demanding
           cognitively). If you get a coding round here, the centering
           exercise is critical.

3:00 PM   Second wind for most people. Use it well.

4:00 PM   Fatigue setting in. Lean on your playbooks and frameworks.
           This is where preparation pays off --- you can run on structure
           even when your creative problem-solving is depleted.

5:00 PM   Almost done. Finish strong. The last round's interviewer
           doesn't know you've been going for 8 hours. Perform as if
           it's your first round.
```

**Critical tip:** Bring snacks. Many companies provide lunch during onsites, but the gap between morning rounds and lunch --- and the gap between lunch and afternoon rounds --- can leave you hungry and coggy. A handful of nuts or an energy bar between rounds is a small investment with a large return.

### When to Eat During Long Onsite Loops

Your brain's primary fuel is glucose. When blood sugar drops, your cognitive performance drops with it --- specifically, your ability to hold information in working memory, make decisions, and resist impulse responses. All of these are critical in technical interviews.

```
EATING STRATEGY FOR A FULL-DAY ONSITE

Before the loop:
  Eat a substantial breakfast 1.5-2 hours before the first round.
  Bring a water bottle and keep sipping between rounds.

Between morning rounds (if a 10-minute break):
  A handful of almonds or a banana. Small, quick, no crash.

Lunch (typically 45-60 minutes):
  DO:
    - Eat. Even if you're nervous and not hungry. Force it.
    - Choose protein + complex carbs (chicken + rice, salad + grains)
    - Drink water
    - Walk outside for 5 minutes if weather permits
    - Chat with your lunch companion (often an engineer, not an
      interviewer --- but assume everything you say is being evaluated)

  DON'T:
    - Skip lunch ("I'll study instead" --- this always backfires)
    - Eat a massive meal (blood goes to digestion, not your brain)
    - Eat heavy carbs without protein (pasta alone = sugar crash)
    - Drink alcohol (some tech company lunches include it --- decline)
    - Complain about the process or earlier rounds to your lunch companion

Between afternoon rounds:
  Another small snack. Dark chocolate is excellent: caffeine + sugar
  in a small package with slower absorption than candy.

Emergency:
  If you feel lightheaded or shaky, ask for a 5-minute break and
  eat something immediately. No interview is worth passing out.
```

### When a Round Goes So Badly You Want to Leave

Rarely --- but it happens --- a round goes catastrophically. You could not write a single line of code. You blanked on every design question. The interviewer asked something completely outside your knowledge. You are sitting in the break room thinking: "Should I just go home?"

**Do not leave.**

Here is why:

1. **You cannot predict the committee's decision.** Hiring committees look at the full picture. A "No Hire" on one round with "Strong Hire" ratings on others can still result in an offer. If you leave, you guarantee a rejection.

2. **The remaining rounds are independent.** The next interviewer knows nothing about the previous round. You have a clean slate.

3. **You are practicing.** Even if this particular company does not work out, every round you complete is practice for the next company. The round you want to skip might be the one where you practice a recovery technique that saves you in a future interview.

4. **Your recruiter will notice.** Walking out mid-loop is a red flag that follows you. Completing the loop with grace, even after a bad round, is noted positively.

The only legitimate reason to leave an onsite early is if you are experiencing a medical emergency or if an interviewer behaves in a way that is genuinely inappropriate (discriminatory comments, harassment). In those cases, excuse yourself, contact the recruiter, and report the incident.

### Dealing with a Round That Went Badly

Here is the uncomfortable truth: even strong candidates who ultimately receive offers often have one round that goes poorly. Google's hiring committee uses a "packet" approach where all feedback is evaluated together, and a single "No Hire" rating does not automatically mean rejection. Amazon evaluates "bar raiser" rounds more heavily but still considers the full picture.

**If a round went badly:**
- It is not over. You have more rounds to demonstrate your ability.
- A "Lean No Hire" on one round can be overcome by "Strong Hire" on others.
- The interviewer may have rated you higher than you think. Many candidates who feel they "bombed" a round actually received "Lean Hire."
- Your job now is to make the next round your best round.


### Junior → FAANG: Between-Round Reset Protocol

Between interview rounds, you have typically 5-15 minutes. Use this time for a specific reset protocol: physically move (stand up, walk if possible), take 3 deep breaths, drink water, and mentally release the previous round (whatever happened is done, each round is evaluated independently). Do not review notes intensively between rounds. There is not enough time for meaningful learning, and the effort competes with mental recovery. Brief review of your 2-3 key behavioral stories is acceptable as a memory primer.

### Senior @ FAANG: Between-Round Reset at the Senior Level: Managing Cognitive Fatigue

Senior interview loops often run 5-6 rounds over 4-5 hours, which creates significant cognitive fatigue. The reset protocol at the senior level is also a fatigue management protocol: after rounds 3-4, the cognitive budget for complex reasoning is noticeably depleted. Mitigations: eat a small snack between rounds (blood glucose depletion degrades working memory), prioritize physical movement to reset the sympathetic nervous system, and use the between-round time to do something completely different from interview thinking (listen to 2 minutes of music, call a friend briefly, or simply stand outside if the interview is in-person). The goal is genuine recovery, not more preparation.
