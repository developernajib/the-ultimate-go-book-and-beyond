## 83G.14 Managing Multiple Interview Processes

If you are interviewing at multiple companies simultaneously (which you should be --- never put all your eggs in one basket), you need a strategy for timing, communication, and decision-making.

### Timing Applications Strategically

Ideally, you want all your interview processes to converge around the same time. This gives you maximum leverage for negotiation and reduces the pressure of expiring offers.

```
STRATEGIC INTERVIEW TIMELINE

Week 1-2: Apply to all target companies simultaneously
           ├── Submit applications
           ├── Reach out to referrals
           └── Contact recruiters on LinkedIn

Week 3-4: Phone screens begin
           ├── Schedule them close together if possible
           └── Use earlier screens as warm-ups for target companies

Week 5-6: Coding challenges / technical screens
           ├── Schedule "practice" companies first
           └── Save your top choices for when you're warmed up

Week 7-8: Onsite interviews
           ├── Aim for all onsites within a 2-week window
           └── Ask recruiters to accommodate your schedule

Week 9:   Offers and negotiation
           ├── All offers arrive within days of each other
           └── Maximum negotiating leverage

This ideal timeline rarely works perfectly. Adjust as needed,
but the principle stands: keep processes synchronized.
```

### The "Offer Deadline" Dance

You receive an offer from Company A with a one-week deadline. You have not heard back from Company B yet, and Company B is your first choice.

**Step 1:** Contact Company B's recruiter immediately. "I've received another offer with a deadline of [date]. Your company is my top choice, and I'd like to explore whether we can accelerate the process."

**Step 2:** Contact Company A's recruiter. "Thank you for the offer. I'm very interested, but I'm also in late stages with another company. Would it be possible to extend the deadline by [X days] so I can make a fully informed decision?"

Most companies will extend by 1--2 weeks. If they refuse to extend at all, that is a data point about the company's culture.

### Using Competing Offers Ethically

Competing offers are a normal part of negotiation. Using them is not dishonest or aggressive --- it is expected.

**What to Say:**
> "I have an offer from [Company A] at a total compensation of approximately [range]. I'm very interested in [Company B], but I want to make sure the compensation is competitive. Is there flexibility in the package?"

**What NOT to do:**
- Fabricate offers you do not have
- Exaggerate the compensation of real offers
- Use offers as threats ("Match this or I'm leaving")
- Play companies against each other in a bidding war (one round of negotiation is appropriate. A prolonged auction is not)

### When to Tell Company A About Company B

- Tell Company A about Company B when you have a concrete offer from Company B and you want Company A to accelerate or match.
- Tell Company B about Company A when you want Company B to speed up their process.
- Do NOT tell anyone about competing offers during the interview itself. That is for the recruiter conversation, not the technical discussion.

### Requesting Deadline Extensions

**What to Say:**
> "I'm taking this decision seriously because I want to make sure it's the right fit for both sides. I have another process that should conclude by [date]. Could we extend the deadline to [date + a few days] so I can make a fully informed decision? I'm genuinely excited about this offer and want to give it the consideration it deserves."

**What NOT to Say:**
> "I'm waiting for a better offer." (Even if true, never frame it this way.)
> "I need more time because I'm not sure." (Frame it as diligence, not uncertainty.)

### The Exploding Offer Problem

An "exploding offer" has a very short deadline (24--72 hours). This is a pressure tactic. Companies that use exploding offers are betting that you will accept out of fear rather than choice.

**How to handle it:**
1. Ask for more time. Many "exploding" offers can be extended if you push back.
2. If they refuse to extend, evaluate whether you want to work at a company that pressures candidates this way.
3. In rare cases, accepting an exploding offer and then reneging if a better offer comes is legally permissible (in most jurisdictions), but it burns bridges. Use this as a last resort.

### Declining Offers Gracefully

When you decline an offer, do it with class. The tech world is small, and you may want to work at that company in the future.

**What to Say:**
> "Thank you so much for the offer. After careful consideration, I've decided to accept a position at another company that I feel is a better fit for my career goals at this time. I really enjoyed meeting the team and learning about [specific thing]. I hope to stay in touch, and I'd love to be considered for opportunities in the future."

**What NOT to Say:**
> "I got a better offer." (Unnecessarily competitive.)
> Nothing. (Ghosting is unprofessional and burns bridges permanently.)
> "The pay was too low." (Even if true, frame it positively.)


### Junior → FAANG: Managing Multiple Interview Loops Simultaneously

Running multiple interview processes simultaneously maximizes your negotiation leverage and ensures you have options if one company's process moves slowly. The logistics require active management: maintain a tracking spreadsheet with each company's status, next step, deadline, and contact person. Coordinate offer timelines by asking companies for extensions when needed (most will grant 1-2 weeks), and accelerate slow processes by requesting expedited scheduling when you have a competing offer deadline.

### Senior @ FAANG: Portfolio Management of Senior Interview Loops

Senior candidates managing multiple interview loops should think of the portfolio as a constraint satisfaction problem: maximize the number of strong offers from companies at the appropriate level, with interview days separated by at least 2-3 days for recovery, and final offer deadlines overlapping enough for negotiation leverage. The sequencing of loops matters: starting with companies that are slightly below your top priority gives you interview practice in the real stakes environment, builds confidence, and may produce offers that can be used as negotiation leverage for your top-priority company. The common mistake is starting with your top-priority company before you are fully sharp.
