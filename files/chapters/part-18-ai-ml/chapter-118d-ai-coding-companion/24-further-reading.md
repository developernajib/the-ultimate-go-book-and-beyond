## Further Reading

- **"Effective Go"**: the official Go resource. AI tools are more useful to you when you deeply understand idiomatic Go, because you cannot evaluate output you could not have written.
- **Claude Code Documentation**: Anthropic's official guide to Claude Code workflows, `CLAUDE.md` configuration, and advanced features. Tooling in this space changes fast, so prefer the vendor's current docs over any book's description of features, including this one's.
- **"The Pragmatic Programmer" by Hunt & Thomas**: the chapter on "Good Enough Software" applies directly to evaluating AI output.
- **Google's Engineering Practices**: their code review guidelines translate directly to reviewing AI-generated code.
- **OWASP Top 10**: essential reading for anyone using AI to generate web application code. A model trained on public code has read a great deal of vulnerable public code, and it can reproduce any vulnerability on that list with complete fluency.
- **"Designing Data-Intensive Applications" by Martin Kleppmann**: the architectural thinking in this book is the kind of judgment AI cannot supply, because it depends on constraints only you know.

**On the evidence for AI productivity**, read both of these and notice that they disagree:

- **"The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot"** (Peng et al., 2023, arXiv:2302.06590): the source of the widely-quoted 55.8% figure. Read the methodology, not the headline: a single greenfield task (write an HTTP server in JavaScript), recruited participants, a 95% confidence interval spanning 21% to 89%, and authorship by the tool's vendor.
- **"Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity"** (METR, 2025, arXiv:2507.09089): experienced developers working real issues in repositories they knew well took 19% *longer* with AI tools, while estimating afterwards that they had been 20% faster. METR itself labels the result historical, since it studied early-2025 tooling.

Reading these two together is the single most useful calibration exercise in this chapter. They are not contradictory so much as measuring different work: greenfield versus mature codebases, unfamiliar versus deeply familiar. The transferable lesson is the perception gap, which no later tooling improvement repeals: your felt sense of speed is not evidence, and it was wrong by roughly 39 percentage points for the developers in the second study.

### Junior → FAANG: Building a Reading Practice That Compounds

The core resources in this list address three distinct competency gaps for engineers early in their AI-augmented development journey. "Effective Go" and Claude Code Documentation address the Go-and-AI integration gap: you need deep idiomatic Go knowledge before AI tool output is reliably useful, because you cannot evaluate AI-generated Go code without knowing what good Go looks like. "The Pragmatic Programmer" and Google's Engineering Practices address the quality evaluation gap: the discipline of evaluating AI output is the same discipline as evaluating any code, and these resources provide frameworks for that evaluation. "OWASP Top 10" and "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" address the judgment gap: security and architecture judgment are the domains where AI is least reliable and where human expertise is most irreplaceable.

For FAANG interview preparation, prioritize the judgment gap resources first. FAANG system design interviews test architectural judgment, not Go syntax knowledge. Reading "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" and then practicing system design with AI as a sounding board (asking it to critique your designs and explain the tradeoffs you missed) combines interview preparation with AI workflow practice. Reading "OWASP Top 10" and then asking AI to generate intentionally vulnerable code (then identifying the vulnerabilities) builds the security review muscle that separates engineers who use AI safely from those who ship AI-generated vulnerabilities.

A structured reading practice for engineers targeting FAANG in 12 months: spend the first month on "Effective Go" and Claude Code Documentation to establish the foundation, the second and third months on "The Pragmatic Programmer" and Google's Engineering Practices to build review discipline, and months four through twelve on "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" (one chapter per week, with AI-assisted practice problems after each chapter). This reading roadmap produces both interview preparation and genuine engineering depth simultaneously.

### Senior @ FAANG: Curating the Team Reading List as Knowledge Infrastructure

At senior level, a team reading list is a knowledge infrastructure decision, not a personal learning choice. The core resources in this list represent different domains of AI-augmented engineering competency: Go language depth, AI tool configuration, software craft, code review discipline, security, and architecture. A team that is systematically weak in one domain needs reading resources targeted at that gap. A team that has strong Go knowledge but weak security awareness needs OWASP and "Writing Secure Code" more than it needs "Effective Go." Diagnosing the team's knowledge gaps and curating a reading list that addresses them is a staff engineer's learning infrastructure responsibility.

The book club model for this reading list is more effective than individual reading at the team level. A monthly book club session where engineers discuss one chapter of "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" in the context of the team's actual AI systems produces both knowledge transfer and alignment on architectural principles. The discussion question "how does the consistency model described in this chapter apply to our RAG pipeline's vector store updates?" connects abstract theory to the team's concrete systems, which is the connection that produces lasting architectural understanding rather than passive reading comprehension.

The reading list is also incomplete relative to the full AI-augmented development domain. Resources not on the list but relevant for teams building production AI systems include: the Anthropic API documentation (for engineers building LLM integrations), the pgvector documentation (for engineers building vector search), the OpenTelemetry Go SDK documentation (for engineers building AI system observability), and the Google SRE Book (for engineers applying reliability engineering principles to probabilistic AI systems). A staff engineer who curates and maintains this extended reading list, organized by role and experience level, is building the team's knowledge infrastructure in a way that compounds: every new engineer who joins the team gets an accelerated path to the knowledge the team has already accumulated.

The most important resource not on this list is the team's own postmortems and architecture decision records. These internal documents, when written with enough detail to be educational, are more relevant to the team's specific AI systems than any published book. A staff engineer who treats postmortem writing as an educational investment, writing postmortems at a level of detail that teaches the reader about the system's failure modes and the team's debugging process, is building institutional knowledge that published resources cannot provide. Combined with the external reading list, this internal knowledge base forms the complete learning infrastructure for an AI-native engineering organization.
