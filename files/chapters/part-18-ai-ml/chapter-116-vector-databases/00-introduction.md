# Chapter 116: Vector Databases

> *"A vector database is not a faster key-value store. It is a mathematical structure that makes meaning searchable."*

Semantic search, recommendation systems, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) all share a common infrastructure primitive: the ability to find "things that are similar to this other thing" quickly, across millions or billions of items. Vectors, the dense numerical representations of meaning produced by embedding models, are the mathematical encoding that makes this possible. A sentence, an image, a product, or a user behavior sequence can all be projected into a high-dimensional space where proximity correlates with semantic similarity. Vector databases are specialized engines designed to answer nearest-neighbor queries in this space with the performance characteristics that production systems demand.

The algorithmic core of vector search is Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search, and the most widely deployed algorithm is Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs. The word *approximate* is the one to sit with. These structures do not find the true nearest neighbors, they find most of them most of the time, and the fraction they get right is **recall**. Every tuning knob in this chapter buys recall with latency and memory, or sells it for the same. There is no setting that gives you all three, and an engineer who has internalized that will out-reason one who has memorized parameter values.

Understanding HNSW, meaning how it builds a navigable hierarchy of proximity connections during indexing and how it traverses that hierarchy during queries, explains where the performance comes from and what it costs. Single-digit-millisecond queries at high recall are readily achievable on a well-tuned index of a few million vectors held in memory. Billion-scale is a different regime that is not a matter of turning up a parameter: it requires sharding, quantization, or on-disk indexes, and each of those buys scale by giving back some recall or latency. Be suspicious of any figure quoted without its dataset, its dimensionality, its recall target, and its hardware, since a latency number without those four is not a claim, it is a slogan. This chapter teaches you to tune with empirical understanding, not cargo-culting, which means measuring on your data.

The Go ecosystem has usable clients for every major vector database: Pinecone (managed cloud), Weaviate (open-source with built-in embedding), Milvus (high-performance self-hosted), and pgvector (PostgreSQL extension for teams that want to start without new infrastructure). This chapter implements a complete production RAG pipeline using all four, so you can choose the right tool for your context: pgvector for an existing PostgreSQL shop, Milvus for billion-scale self-hosted, Pinecone for zero-ops managed. Start with pgvector unless you can articulate why it fails for you, since operating one database is cheaper than operating two. Hybrid search, combining ANN vector similarity with BM25 keyword scoring, is implemented and benchmarked, because pure semantic search reliably underperforms on exactly the queries users care most about: rare proper nouns, error codes, part numbers, and identifiers, where the user wants the *exact* token and embeddings offer them something merely similar.

**What you'll learn in this chapter:**

- Vector embeddings explained: how text, images, and structured data are projected into high-dimensional spaces, and what "distance" means semantically
- Similarity metrics in practice: cosine similarity, dot product, and L2 distance, when each is appropriate, why cosine and dot product coincide only on normalized vectors, and how embedding model training affects the choice
- HNSW algorithm internals: graph construction, query traversal, the M/efConstruction/ef tradeoff, and how to benchmark recall vs. latency for your data
- Working with Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, and pgvector from Go: client setup, index creation, upsert, query, and filtered search
- Building a production RAG pipeline: document chunking strategies, embedding generation with batching, retrieval with metadata filtering, and context assembly for LLM prompts
- Hybrid search: combining vector similarity with keyword scoring using Weaviate's BM25+vector fusion and pgvector's full-text search integration
- Embedding management at scale: batched generation, caching with content-addressable hashing, incremental index updates, and handling embedding model version changes
- Performance tuning and cost optimization: index parameter selection, quantization (PQ/SQ), approximate vs exact search tradeoffs, and tiered storage for hot/cold vectors

**Why this matters at scale:**

Vector search has become infrastructure rather than a novelty, and the tell is that it stopped being a product category and became a feature of things you already run. Established search engines and general-purpose databases have added HNSW indexes, which is why "do we need a vector database?" is now a genuine question rather than a rhetorical one.

Three properties of the technology drive the architecture you will build. Retrieval quality gains over pure keyword search are real but are largest where vocabulary mismatch is the problem, meaning the user asks in different words than the document uses, and are smallest, sometimes negative, where the user knows the exact term. That asymmetry is the entire argument for hybrid search. Embeddings are expensive to produce and cheap to store, so the reindexing cost when you change embedding models dominates the design and is the constraint most teams discover too late. And the retrieval layer, not the LLM, is where RAG quality is usually won or lost: a strong model reasoning over the wrong three chunks produces a fluent, confident, wrong answer, and no prompt fixes it.

### Junior → FAANG: Vector Databases as the Foundation of Modern AI Search

Vector databases are the infrastructure component that makes LLMs useful for questions about your specific data. A general-purpose LLM knows about the world up to its training cutoff but knows nothing about your company's internal documentation, your product catalog, or your customers' support history. The pattern for bridging this gap is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): embed your documents as vectors, store them in a vector database, and at query time, retrieve the most semantically similar documents and include them in the LLM prompt. Understanding this pattern is a prerequisite for most senior AI infrastructure interviews at FAANG.

The mathematical intuition behind vector similarity is approachable: an embedding model converts text into a point in high-dimensional space (typically 768 to 3072 dimensions), and semantically similar texts produce points that are close together in that space. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, and ranges from 1.0 (identical direction, very similar meaning) through 0.0 (perpendicular, unrelated) to -1.0 (opposite direction).

Two caveats that interviewers probe. First, do not read -1.0 as "opposite meaning": text embedding spaces are not laid out that way, negative cosine is rare between real sentences, and the scores you will actually see cluster in a narrow positive band. This has a practical consequence that surprises people: a raw cosine score of 0.8 is not "80% relevant" and may well be a poor match, because the scale is compressed and its meaning is model-specific. Never hardcode a similarity threshold without calibrating it against your own data and model.

Second, know the relationship that half the field gets wrong: **dot product equals cosine similarity only when both vectors are unit length**. Cosine is the dot product divided by both magnitudes, so normalization makes those divisors 1 and the two coincide. If your vectors are normalized, choosing between cosine and dot product is a performance decision (dot is cheaper, skipping the magnitude computation) with no effect on ranking. If they are not normalized, the two produce genuinely different orderings, because dot product rewards long vectors. Confusing these silently degrades retrieval quality without any error, which is why it is worth being exact about.

Interview entry point for this chapter's content is the question: "Design a document Q&A system that can answer questions about a large corpus of internal documentation." Your answer should cover: document ingestion and chunking strategy, embedding generation and storage, query-time retrieval, prompt construction with retrieved context, and quality evaluation. Having a specific, technical answer for each step demonstrates hands-on experience rather than superficial familiarity.

### Senior @ FAANG: Vector Database Platform Architecture

At senior level, a vector database is a platform component with multiple consumers. The document indexing team ingests and embeds new content. The search team queries the index at low latency. The evaluation team runs recall benchmarks to validate retrieval quality. The monitoring team tracks latency, throughput, and index staleness. Designing the vector database infrastructure as a platform with stable interfaces for each of these consumers, rather than a single application's private data store, is the architectural shift that enables the entire organization to build on top of it.

The choice between managed vector database services (Pinecone, Weaviate Cloud, Qdrant Cloud) and self-hosted solutions (pgvector, Milvus, Chroma) is a build-versus-buy decision with concrete financial and operational implications. Managed services have lower operational overhead (no index management, automatic scaling, provider-managed reliability) but higher per-query cost and data residency constraints. Self-hosted solutions require operational investment (index tuning, scaling, backup, monitoring) but have lower compute cost at scale and keep data on-premises. At FAANG scale, the cost difference typically justifies self-hosting, but the operational investment is significant and must be factored into the staffing plan.

At L6, the vector database ADR must address: which embedding model produces the best retrieval quality for your specific domain (not just the highest benchmark scores on general datasets), what the index type and parameters are for your query latency and recall requirements (HNSW versus IVF versus brute force, with the relevant tradeoff analysis), how the index will be updated as new documents are added or existing documents are modified (incremental update versus periodic full rebuild), and what the backup and disaster recovery strategy is for a database that may contain billions of vectors. These decisions, documented in an ADR and reviewed by principal engineers, are the foundation of a production-grade vector search infrastructure.
