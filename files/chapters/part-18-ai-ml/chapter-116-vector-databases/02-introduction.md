## 116.1 Overview

Vector databases have become essential infrastructure for modern AI applications. Unlike traditional databases that store and query exact values, vector databases store high-dimensional embeddings and enable similarity-based retrieval. This capability powers semantic search, recommendation systems, RAG applications, and more.

In this chapter, we'll explore vector databases from first principles, implement integrations with major providers, and build complete production systems including a full RAG implementation.

### Junior → FAANG: Why Vector Databases Matter for Backend Engineers

The shift from exact-match retrieval to similarity-based retrieval is one of the most significant architectural changes in backend engineering in the past several years. Traditional search systems use keyword matching, inverted indexes, and boolean logic. Vector search systems use learned representations, approximate nearest neighbor algorithms, and mathematical similarity. Backend engineers who understand both paradigms and can design systems that combine them are significantly more valuable than those who only know one.

For a junior engineer, the most important thing to understand about vector databases is what they are not: they are not a replacement for a traditional database, they are not a search engine that handles keyword queries well, and they are not a key-value store. They are a specialized data structure for one specific operation: finding the most similar vectors to a query vector. Everything else in this chapter builds on that single capability.

The RAG pattern is the production use case that makes vector database knowledge directly relevant to most backend engineering interviews. "Design a system where users can ask questions about a corpus of documents" is a question that appears in interviews for roles at Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft. Your answer starts with the vector database and the RAG pipeline. Having this answer ready is the payoff for studying this chapter.

### Senior @ FAANG: Vector Infrastructure as Strategic Investment

At senior level, investing in vector database infrastructure is a strategic decision with a multi-year horizon. The initial investment (embedding model selection and evaluation, vector database deployment and tuning, ingestion pipeline development, monitoring infrastructure) is significant. The return is a general-purpose semantic retrieval capability that can be applied to search, recommendations, deduplication, clustering, anomaly detection, and any other use case that benefits from semantic similarity. The long-term ROI of this investment compounds as the number of use cases grows.

The architectural principle that makes vector infrastructure strategic is composability. A well-designed vector search platform can be composed with different front-end retrieval interfaces (keyword search, semantic search, hybrid search), different embedding models (text, image, code, multimodal), different filtering criteria (metadata filters, date ranges, access control), and different downstream consumers (RAG pipelines, recommendation engines, clustering jobs). This composability means the platform investment benefits an ever-growing set of products without proportional incremental investment.

At L7, vector database strategy intersects with the organization's data strategy. The same corpus of embeddings that powers semantic search can also power content deduplication, near-duplicate detection for moderation, and clustering of user feedback for product insights. Designing the embedding infrastructure to be shared across these use cases, rather than building a separate pipeline for each, is a significant efficiency gain that requires coordination across multiple product teams and is a quintessential staff-level architectural initiative.

Push that idea carefully, though, because "one embedding to serve them all" is a genuinely appealing proposal that is sometimes wrong, and a staff engineer is expected to know which case they are in. An embedding space is shaped by its training objective. A model trained so that a *question* lands near its *answer* is optimized for retrieval, and that is not the same geometry as one where similar *documents* land near each other, which is what clustering and deduplication want. Recommendations are further still, since collaborative signal ("people who liked this also liked that") is often not semantic similarity at all: two items with near-identical descriptions may be substitutes that no one buys together. Sharing the *pipeline* (ingestion, batching, versioning, storage, monitoring) is nearly always right. Sharing the *vectors* is an empirical claim that has to be measured per use case, and forcing it for elegance is how you ship a recommender that only ever suggests near-duplicates of what the user just bought.
