## Summary

Cache invalidation is the most complex aspect of caching, requiring coordination across multiple layers. Key mechanisms include:

- **Invalidation strategies**: TTL, event-driven via Kafka, version-based keys, surrogate/tag-based CDN purging
- **HTTP cache headers**: `Cache-Control` directives (max-age, s-maxage, stale-while-revalidate, stale-if-error, immutable), ETag for conditional GET, Vary for content negotiation
- **CDN integration**: Cloudflare (tag purging, analytics API), Fastly (surrogate keys, instant purge, soft purge), AWS CloudFront (path invalidation)
- **Multi-CDN**: parallel purge across providers for geo-redundancy
- **Stale-while-revalidate**: background refresh to eliminate user-visible cache misses
- **Cache poisoning prevention**: cache key normalization, Vary header management, HTTPS enforcement
- **Production handler**: ETag + cache-control + surrogate keys + invalidation trigger in one Go handler
- **Case studies**: GitHub multi-CDN, Netflix Open Connect Appliance, Varnish banlist purging

The golden rule of cache invalidation: **invalidate at the finest granularity possible** (surrogate keys, not "purge everything") to maintain cache efficiency while ensuring correctness.

### Junior to FAANG: prerequisite gaps, worked examples, first-job context

If you leave this chapter with only one mental model, let it be the invalidation granularity ladder. From coarsest to finest: global purge, prefix purge, URL purge, surrogate-key purge, versioned-key rotation. Each rung up the ladder reduces blast radius and preserves hit rate, and your job in any CDN design is to pick the finest rung that solves the correctness requirement. Junior engineers default to the coarsest rung because it is conceptually simple. Senior engineers default to the finest because they have watched hit rate collapse to zero during a deploy and seen the origin buckle under the re-warm. The first-job takeaway is to resist the urge to purge, and when you must purge, tag first.

The second mental model is the three-way join between cache key, cache-control, and invalidation. A cache entry is only useful if all three are coherent: the key captures the right request dimensions, the control headers express the right freshness, and the invalidation path can find the entry when the underlying data changes. Every CDN bug you will debug in your first two years resolves to one of these three being out of sync with the other two. When you diagnose an incident, write down the three on a whiteboard and check them in order. That discipline alone clears most of the first-job CDN workload.

The "why did this fail" reflex for everything in this chapter: when a cache misbehaves, the cause is in the cache key nine times out of ten, in the invalidation path one time out of ten, and almost never in the cache-control headers. Start your diagnosis at the key, not at the TTL. Interviewers know this and will test whether you do too.

### Senior at FAANG: staff and principal material, ADRs, maturity models, governance

The chapter as a whole is a starter kit for CDN engineering, and the staff reading of it is as follows. Cache invalidation at scale is not a technical problem, it is an organizational one. The technical primitives (surrogate keys, stale-while-revalidate, shield nodes, multi-CDN) are solved in every major vendor. What is not solved, and what principal engineers are hired to build, is the organizational layer that keeps those primitives coherent across hundreds of services and thousands of engineers. That layer consists of policy as code, CI enforcement, per-route telemetry, purge cardinality budgets, provider-agnostic abstractions, and quarterly game days. Every case study in the chapter (the New York Times at Fastly, Shopify's mesh, Vercel ISR, Netflix OCA, GitHub Pages with Cloudflare, Pinterest multi-CDN, Slack edge, Discord edge, Reddit post-Fastly, Stripe static assets) is a different tradeoff inside the same organizational problem.

The maturity model L1 to L5 on CDN operations, compressed into a one-paragraph summary for an ADR. L1 teams configure their CDN through the vendor UI and discover problems through customer complaints. L2 teams have dashboards and a runbook but still operate reactively. L3 teams express cache policy as code and enforce it in CI, with per-route SLOs driving proactive remediation. L4 teams run multi-provider with third-party health checks, purge cardinality budgets, and quarterly game days that exercise full failover. L5 teams automate traffic steering, cost rebalancing, and edge config promotion through the same pipeline as application code. Most FAANG organizations sit at L3 to L4 in 2026, with a small number of properties at L5 for specific high-revenue traffic segments. The principal's job is to know where their organization sits, where it needs to be, and which of the quarterly investments move the needle.

Governance priorities for a new principal inheriting a CDN program. Week one, read every postmortem from the last two years and categorize them into provider incidents, edge-config incidents, and origin incidents, then graph the trend. Week two, measure per-route hit rate for the top one hundred routes by revenue and flag any route below ninety percent that should be higher. Week three, audit the purge cardinality per service and identify any service pushing more than ten thousand purges per minute (often a sign of a feedback loop). Week four, read the CDN contract and the renewal date, because the contract lever is almost always the largest cost optimization available, and the renegotiation must start six months before renewal. Month two, publish the maturity model assessment and the roadmap to the next level. Month three, start the first game day. This is the calendar that turns "we have a CDN" into "we operate a CDN at staff-plus maturity", and it is the work the chapter has been preparing you to do.

Final staff-level principle. The CDN is not a cache, it is a distributed system with weak consistency across hundreds of POPs, and every design decision should be evaluated against that framing. Strong consistency on the edge is a fiction and any runbook that depends on it will fail during an incident. Design for eventual consistency, assume purges are best-effort and eventually applied, make every invalidation idempotent, and verify every resilience control (stale-if-error, failover, purge fan-out) with synthetic probes running continuously. That is the mental shift from senior to staff, and it is the shift that separates the engineer who configures a CDN from the engineer who owns one.
