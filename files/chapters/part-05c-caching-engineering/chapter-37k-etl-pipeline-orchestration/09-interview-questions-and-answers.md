## Interview Questions and Answers

**Q: How do you ensure exactly-once semantics in an ETL pipeline?**

A: True end-to-end exactly-once across heterogeneous systems is a distributed-systems unicorn. The pragmatic target is **at-least-once delivery plus idempotent writes**, often called *effectively-once*. Four mechanisms combine to get there: (1) **idempotent sinks** - `INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE` in Postgres, `MERGE` in BigQuery/Snowflake, content-addressed S3 keys so a re-upload is a no-op. (2) **checkpoint after commit** - persist the source watermark or Kafka offset only after the destination transaction has committed, never before. (3) **deduplication by event_id** - a bounded-TTL `sync.Map` or a Redis set in front of the sink catches replays. (4) **transactional outbox at the source** - the producer writes the business change and the event to the same database transaction, so CDC cannot miss or duplicate. Within a single Kafka ecosystem you can also lean on the transactional producer + `read_committed` consumer + `enable.idempotence=true` combination that gives exactly-once across topics.

*What FAANG expects:* A senior candidate must distinguish **delivery semantics** (at-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once) from **processing semantics** (idempotent, non-idempotent), explain why the former alone is insufficient, and give a concrete commit ordering for a Kafka-to-Postgres pipeline. Mentioning Kafka's transactional producer, the transactional outbox pattern, and the cost of dedup state is expected at L5+.

Follow-ups: *What breaks if you commit the Kafka offset before the Postgres transaction commits?* *How do you dedup when `event_id` is not provided by the source?* *What's the memory cost of a 24-hour dedup window at 100k events/sec, and how would you bound it?*

**Q: What is backpressure and how does Go's channel model handle it?**

A: Backpressure is the mechanism by which a slow downstream consumer signals a fast upstream producer to slow down, preventing unbounded memory growth. In Go, bounded buffered channels implement backpressure automatically: `make(chan Record, 256)` blocks the sender once 256 records are in flight. This propagates backwards through the DAG - if the loader is slow, the batch stage blocks, which blocks the transform stage, which blocks the extractor, which blocks `Reader.FetchMessage` on Kafka, which naturally pauses the partition. No explicit flow-control protocol is required. Monitor `len(ch)` or expose it as a gauge to detect bottlenecks. A consistently-full channel is the stage immediately downstream of the bottleneck.

*What FAANG expects:* Explain why unbounded channels or unbounded in-memory queues are an anti-pattern (OOM under a downstream stall), contrast with explicit credit-based flow control (gRPC, HTTP/2), and describe how backpressure at the extractor translates to Kafka consumer lag that operators can actually alert on.

Follow-ups: *What happens when the slowest stage is external (a REST sink with a rate limit)?* *How do you pick the buffer size - 1, 256, 100000?* *How does backpressure interact with a `select` that also reads `ctx.Done()`?*

**Q: How do you handle schema evolution in ETL pipelines?**

A: Classify every change: (1) **backward-compatible** (new optional field, widened type) - tolerate missing fields with defaults, `encoding/json` already ignores unknown fields. (2) **forward-compatible** (consumers tolerate older producers) - achieved by keeping required fields stable. (3) **breaking** (renamed, removed, retyped) - require a version bump and dual-write. The operational tools are: a **schema registry** (Confluent Schema Registry, Apicurio, AWS Glue Schema Registry) enforcing compatibility at publish time, **Avro or Protobuf** as the wire format (JSON is fine for small scale but loses schema enforcement), and a **schema version field** carried on every record so transforms can branch. In the warehouse, add new columns as nullable and backfill asynchronously. Never rename a column in place - add the new one, dual-write, migrate consumers, drop the old column in a later release.

*What FAANG expects:* Knowledge that Protobuf forbids changing field numbers and that Avro requires reader/writer schema pairs, the three compatibility levels (BACKWARD, FORWARD, FULL) and what each allows, and a migration playbook for renaming a column without downtime.

Follow-ups: *A producer team wants to change a field from `int32` to `int64` - safe or not in Protobuf? in Avro?* *How do you roll out a new required field?* *What fails if you drop a field that old consumers still read?*

**Q: ETL vs ELT - when would you pick each in 2026?**

A: **ELT is the default now** because cloud warehouses (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks SQL, ClickHouse) are cheaper and faster at SQL transformation than any external compute you would provision. Load raw data with Fivetran/Airbyte, transform with dbt inside the warehouse, orchestrate with Airflow or Dagster. **ETL still wins** in four cases: (1) **regulated data** that cannot land in the warehouse unredacted (PII, PHI, PCI) - transform and tokenise in flight. (2) **streaming analytics** where latency must be sub-second - Flink or a Go service on Kafka materializes the view before the warehouse would even see the row. (3) **cost control for massive raw data** where loading everything would waste warehouse storage - pre-aggregate before load. (4) **destinations that are not SQL warehouses** - feature stores, search indexes, graph databases.

*What FAANG expects:* Recognition that this is an economic and latency trade-off, not a religious one. Mentioning dbt's role, Snowflake/BigQuery storage-compute separation, and knowing when a lakehouse (Iceberg/Delta/Hudi on S3) makes the distinction irrelevant.

Follow-ups: *Why did ETL dominate in the Hadoop era but lose to ELT in the cloud era?* *Where does reverse-ETL (Hightouch, Census) fit?* *If the warehouse is ClickHouse, does the answer change?*

**Q: How does change-data capture (CDC) with Debezium actually work, and why is it better than `SELECT * WHERE updated_at > ?`?**

A: Debezium tails the database's **transaction log** - the Postgres WAL via logical replication slots, the MySQL binlog, the SQL Server change tables, the MongoDB oplog - and emits one Kafka message per row change with `before`, `after`, `op` (c/u/d), and a log-sequence number. Advantages over watermark polling: (1) **captures deletes** - watermark queries cannot see rows that no longer exist. (2) **captures every intermediate state**, not just the latest. (3) **near-zero load on the source** - reading the log is orders of magnitude cheaper than a full-table scan with an index seek. (4) **consistent ordering** tied to transaction commit order. (5) **no clock-skew or updated_at lies** - some applications forget to bump the watermark column. Cost: you run a Kafka Connect cluster, manage replication slot lag (an abandoned slot fills the WAL and can take a Postgres instance down), and need a schema registry because the CDC payload is typed.

*What FAANG expects:* Knowledge that a Debezium connector is stateful (holds an offset in Kafka Connect), that PII requires `transforms.Unwrap` and column masking, and awareness of the failure mode where a Postgres replication slot blocks WAL cleanup. Knowing that the Debezium 1.x series is long past end-of-life and that production setups should run a current major release (the 3.x line) is a bonus.

Follow-ups: *What happens if the Debezium worker is offline for a week?* *How do you handle DDL (a new column)?* *How do you capture a snapshot of existing data alongside the live change stream?*

**Q: When would you reach for Airflow vs Dagster vs Prefect vs Temporal?**

A: **Airflow** - the incumbent for batch DAG scheduling. rich community, huge operator catalog, strong dbt integration, best when your workload is "run these 300 Python/SQL tasks every day on a cron". Pain points are Python-only task code and historical awkwardness around dynamic DAGs (largely fixed by TaskFlow and dynamic task mapping). **Dagster** - asset-oriented rather than task-oriented. First-class lineage, typed inputs/outputs, local dev ergonomics, a great fit when your mental model is "these tables depend on those tables." **Prefect** - hybrid cloud with a generous OSS core, Pythonic, strong dynamic/imperative flows, easier to self-host than Airflow. **Temporal** - not a data orchestrator, a **durable workflow engine**. You write workflows as ordinary Go/Java/TypeScript code and Temporal persists every step so crashes and restarts resume exactly where they left off. Pick Temporal when your "pipeline" is actually a long-running business process (order fulfilment, multi-step onboarding, saga transactions) with retries, timeouts, and human-in-the-loop steps - not when your pipeline is "scan a table and load a warehouse."

*What FAANG expects:* Ability to articulate that Airflow/Dagster/Prefect are **schedulers for data assets** and Temporal is a **durable execution engine for arbitrary code**. Recognizing the anti-pattern of doing business workflows in Airflow or batch ETL in Temporal.

Follow-ups: *A single Airflow task runs for 12 hours and sometimes OOMs - what's wrong architecturally?* *Your Temporal workflow code changes - what happens to running workflows?* *How would you express asset lineage in Airflow?*

**Q: Design a dead-letter queue strategy for a production pipeline.**

A: Three pillars. **(1) Error classification** at the point of failure: mark errors as `retriable` (network timeout, 5xx, lock conflict, rate limit) or `permanent` (validation failed, schema mismatch, deserialization error, 4xx). Retry retriable errors with capped exponential backoff plus jitter (e.g. 3 attempts, 100ms-10s), and send permanent errors straight to DLQ. **(2) DLQ storage** with enough context to debug and replay: the original payload (raw bytes, not the parsed struct, so you can re-parse with a new schema), failure reason, stack trace, pipeline version, attempt count, first-failed-at timestamp, source offset/key. Kafka dead-letter topic keyed by error type is the standard. S3 JSONL works for lower-volume batch pipelines. **(3) Operational tooling**: a CLI or admin UI to inspect, filter, fix, and replay. Alerts on **rate-of-change** of the DLQ (growth > 10x in 5 minutes) not absolute size, and per-source error ratio alerts to catch upstream schema breaks early. Never let a DLQ grow unbounded - set a TTL or archive to cold storage.

*What FAANG expects:* The retriable-vs-permanent classification (most candidates miss this and blindly retry forever), alerting on derivatives not absolutes, and the replay story - "what does it take to reprocess a week of DLQ records after a bug fix?"

Follow-ups: *A single poison message keeps crashing the consumer - how do you avoid a head-of-line block?* *How do you prevent a replay from double-writing to an already-consistent sink?* *Can the DLQ itself fail, and how do you handle that?*

**Q: Walk through the metrics and traces you would emit from a Go ETL pipeline.**

A: Four categories via OpenTelemetry, with Prometheus for metrics and a tracing backend (Jaeger, Tempo, Honeycomb) for traces. **Metrics** - per-stage counters (`records_in_total`, `records_out_total`, `records_errored_total`, `records_dropped_total`) with `pipeline` and `stage` labels. A histogram of per-record processing latency (`stage_duration_seconds`), a gauge of source lag (`kafka_consumer_lag`, watermark age for batch sources), and sink health (`sink_write_duration_seconds`, `sink_write_errors_total`). Derive SLOs as ratios: error rate, stage throughput, end-to-end latency p95/p99. **Traces** - extract W3C trace context from the source message headers (Kafka records can carry it), create one span per stage in `Process`, attach `record.id`, `record.schema`, and `record.source` as span attributes, propagate context to the sink so a single trace spans Kafka → pipeline → Postgres. **Logs** - structured JSON, enriched with `trace_id` and `span_id` so a single trace view links to every log line. **Alerts** - on error rate derivative, lag exceeding SLO, DLQ growth rate, and on the absence of records (a pipeline that silently stops is worse than one that visibly fails).

*What FAANG expects:* Metrics, traces, and logs correlated via trace ID (the "three pillars" of observability as OpenTelemetry frames them), plus awareness that Prometheus is cumulative-counter only and OTel delta metrics need a translator. picking **symptom-based SLOs** (lag, error rate) over **cause-based** (goroutine count).

Follow-ups: *Your p99 latency is fine but users complain of stale data - what do you check?* *How do you trace a single user's event through a pipeline with a 60-second batch stage?* *A stage's `records_out_total` is less than `records_in_total` - what are the possible causes?*

**Q: How do you design a pipeline to be resumable after a crash?**

A: Resumability is a function of **idempotent writes plus durable checkpoints**. Pick a monotonically-increasing watermark on the source (`updated_at`, Kafka offset, S3 ETag + listing order, CDC LSN) and persist it *after* the destination transaction commits - never before, never in memory only. Store checkpoints somewhere durable and shared (Redis with AOF, Postgres table, S3 object) so a restarted worker on a different host resumes from the same point. Make sinks idempotent so that the 0-to-N rows written before the crash do not duplicate when the same batch is re-processed. Handle the restart case where the last checkpointed batch was partially loaded: either the sink is idempotent (upsert by primary key), or you wrap the batch write and checkpoint update in a single transaction (possible for same-DB loads, impossible cross-system - fall back to idempotency). For Kafka, use consumer-group offset commit with `auto.commit=false` and commit only after the sink confirms.

*What FAANG expects:* The checkpoint-after-commit invariant, a concrete story for what "idempotent" means in each sink type (upsert, content-addressed S3 key, dedup table), and the cross-system transaction impossibility that forces you back to idempotency.

Follow-ups: *The checkpoint store itself is down - do you block or proceed?* *How do you checkpoint when a batch spans multiple source partitions?* *What if the source has no natural watermark (a paginated API that reshuffles)?*

**Q: Your pipeline throughput drops 50% at 3am every night. How do you debug it?**

A: A structured incident triage: (1) **Confirm scope** - is it this pipeline only, or all pipelines on the host? If all, suspect noisy neighbor (backup, cron job, VACUUM, network maintenance). (2) **Look at per-stage metrics** - which stage's `records_out / records_in` dropped? The slowest stage is your bottleneck. (3) **Check source lag** - is the source producing less (upstream change) or the pipeline consuming less (local change)? (4) **Check sink latency** - `sink_write_duration_seconds` p99 spike points to the warehouse (maintenance window, concurrent heavy query, autovacuum, index rebuild). (5) **Check resource metrics** - CPU throttling, page cache eviction, disk IO saturation on the checkpoint store, goroutine count explosion. (6) **Check external correlations** - database replication lag, Kafka broker GC, DNS or TLS handshake latency. At 3am the usual suspect is a scheduled maintenance job on a shared dependency (warehouse, database, Kafka cluster). Build a runbook listing the top five suspects ranked by frequency.

*What FAANG expects:* A systematic approach, not jumping to conclusions. using metrics before logs before code changes. Knowing that correlated timing almost always points to a scheduled external job.

Follow-ups: *Metrics show the loader is slow but the database reports normal load - what else?* *How would you prove it's not a GC pause in the Go process?* *Once you find the cause, how do you prevent it without eliminating the 3am job?*

### Junior to FAANG: Entry-Point Interview Map and Worked Answers

A junior candidate walking into a FAANG data-engineering loop in 2026 should expect four question archetypes. The first is a definitional pair drill: at-least-once versus exactly-once, idempotent versus non-idempotent, event-time versus processing-time, push versus pull. Answer in one sentence each, then name a concrete system that exemplifies the choice. The second is a SQL idempotency drill: given `orders(id, status, updated_at)`, write a `MERGE` that upserts from a staging table without creating duplicates even if the same staging row appears twice. The third is a backfill walk-through: your pipeline had a bug from April 1 to April 15, the output is wrong, describe the steps to rebuild the affected partitions without taking downstream dashboards offline. The fourth is a debugging scenario: lag is climbing, walk me through the first ten minutes.

Worked answer for the backfill drill. You freeze the normal schedule for the bug window. You write to a shadow partition or a side table. You reconcile by row count and by a sampling query against source-of-truth. You cut over with an atomic rename or a `CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE AS SELECT`. You announce a downstream refresh window. You keep the old partition for seven days in case of regression. This answer demonstrates three things interviewers look for: you know data is a product with consumers, you do not mutate data in place, and you plan rollback before you plan cutover.

"Why did this fail?" reflex for junior candidates. When the interviewer says "the pipeline is stuck", do not say "I would look at logs". Say "I would check, in order, source lag, stage-level records-in versus records-out, sink write latency p99, DLQ growth rate, and then logs filtered by trace ID for the stage with the biggest drop". Naming the ladder in that order signals that you have done on-call at scale.

### Senior at FAANG: Signal Ladders L3 to L7, Bar-Raiser Questions, Maturity Framing

Interview signal ladder by level. At L3 the bar is the junior map above. At L4 the bar is an end-to-end design for a single pipeline at ten thousand rows per second with backpressure, DLQ, idempotency, and a checkpoint story. At L5 the candidate designs for one hundred thousand rows per second across regions, handles schema evolution without an outage, and can explain the cost envelope within plus-or-minus thirty percent. At L6 (staff) the candidate designs the platform that L5 pipelines run on: shared orchestrator, shared contracts, shared observability, shared governance, and the migration plan that moves fifty teams onto it without breaking production. At L7 (principal) the candidate negotiates with peer platform teams (serving, feature store, search, lakehouse), writes the multi-year bet ("we are consolidating on Iceberg and Kafka, sunsetting Hive and RabbitMQ over six quarters"), and owns the company-level data reliability SLO. The question "design an ETL for a billion rows per day" is an L4 question. "Design a near-real-time CDC pipeline for a reporting warehouse serving five hundred analysts" is an L5 question. "Migrate a batch pipeline ecosystem to streaming across fifteen teams" is an L6 question. "Consolidate three acquired companies' data platforms onto one" is an L7 question.

Bar-raiser question 1: exactly-once at one million events per second. The candidate who answers "Kafka transactional producer" fails. At one million per second Kafka transactions add unacceptable coordinator overhead. The strong answer separates the guarantee from the implementation. Use at-least-once transport, shard the stream by a deterministic key, make the sink idempotent (upsert keyed on `event_id` plus `schema_version`), bound the dedup state (a probabilistic structure like a HyperLogLog or a rotating Cuckoo filter for approximate dedup, exact dedup only for a bounded recent window backed by Redis or RocksDB). Discuss the cost: exact dedup at one million per second with a twenty-four hour window is roughly one terabyte of keyed state, which you shard across workers consistent-hashed by `event_id`. Name the trade-off: you can have exact exactly-once within a window, approximate outside the window, and let the downstream warehouse do a final dedup via `MERGE` on primary key for audit correctness.

Bar-raiser question 2: handle schema evolution without an outage. Weak answer: "use Avro". Strong answer: there are three kinds of change (additive, compatible widening, breaking) and three kinds of consumer (new, old, old-that-will-never-be-redeployed). For additive changes set BACKWARD compatibility in the registry and the old consumer ignores the new field. For compatible widening (int32 to int64 in Avro works, in Protobuf the wire-compatible widening is int32 to int64 or sint32 to sint64 but not int32 to string) validate via CI contract tests before publish. For breaking changes run a dual-write: producer emits both v1 and v2 to two topics for a migration window, consumers migrate independently, v1 is sunset when traffic drops to zero plus a buffer. Governance: every schema change goes through a pull request against a schema repo, CI runs compatibility check, deploys to registry on merge. No outage means the deployment pipeline is designed to have overlap between old and new, never a gap.

Bar-raiser question 3: debug a pipeline that is six hours behind. The strong answer distinguishes catch-up rate from current rate. If the current rate equals the source rate, you are keeping up at a lag but not closing it, and you need to scale the bottleneck stage horizontally. If the current rate exceeds the source rate, you are closing the gap and can estimate ETA. If the current rate is below the source rate, lag grows unboundedly and you must either scale or shed load. The debugging walk starts with `source_rate`, `sink_rate`, `lag_seconds` metrics. Then stage-level rates to find the bottleneck. Then per-operation latency histograms on the bottleneck stage. Then resource metrics on that stage's workers. Then external dependencies (is the warehouse throttling, is a network path saturated, is the checkpoint store slow). In parallel you mitigate by scaling the bottleneck horizontally if stateless, by sharding if stateful, by shedding non-critical downstream consumers, and by pausing backfills. You communicate: every thirty minutes on the incident channel with "current lag, current catch-up rate, ETA to zero lag, confidence".

Bar-raiser question 4: cost-optimize a one hundred thousand dollar per month warehouse pipeline. The strong answer audits in this order. Storage format: are raw landings in Parquet with ZSTD, partitioned by date and a high-cardinality key that prunes most queries? Compute shape: are transformations scheduled at off-peak, using smaller warehouse sizes with auto-suspend? Query patterns: are analysts running `SELECT *` on ten-billion-row tables because they did not know about cluster keys? Pipeline frequency: does the executive dashboard really need every-five-minute refresh or is hourly acceptable? Data retention: are you keeping raw events for two years when ninety days is enough for audit, with older data tiered to S3 Glacier? Duplicate work: are multiple pipelines computing the same aggregation because teams do not know about each other? Typical savings: thirty to sixty percent on a pipeline that has never been reviewed, usually split across storage tiering (twenty percent), query optimization and cluster keys (fifteen percent), pipeline frequency reduction (ten percent), and de-duplication of transforms (ten percent). The governance piece: quarterly cost reviews with chargeback visible to the owning team, because without chargeback no engineer prioritizes optimization.

Maturity framing when interviewing for staff and principal. The candidate who describes a single pipeline is interviewing for L4. The candidate who describes the platform and the governance model around pipelines, the migration plan from L2 to L4 across dozens of teams, and the cost and SLO reporting that makes the platform accountable to its consumers is interviewing for L6 or L7. Explicitly reference maturity levels. Say "this team is at L2, the path to L4 requires these three investments in this order, and here is how I would sequence them over four quarters".

Follow-up bar-raiser that separates L6 from L7: "you have two years and twenty headcount, what do you build versus buy". The L7 answer names a stack (Kafka, Iceberg, Flink, dbt, Dagster or Temporal, Great Expectations, DataHub), justifies each pick against alternatives with specific failure modes from the last three years, identifies the one or two places where build beats buy for your company's scale or differentiation, and presents the plan as a portfolio of bets with review dates, not as a waterfall. The L6 answer names the stack and the plan but does not convincingly tie the bets to company strategy or to competitor moves.
