## Summary

This chapter built a complete ETL framework in Go:

- **Pipeline primitives**: Channels connect stages with automatic backpressure. `FanOut` parallelizes CPU-bound stages. `MergeChannels` collects from parallel workers. The `instrumentStage` wrapper routes errors to the DLQ.

- **Extractors**: `PostgresExtractor` uses cursor-based pagination with checkpoint persistence for resumability. `KafkaExtractor` commits offsets after successful processing for at-least-once semantics.

- **Transformation stages**: `FilterStage`, `MapStage`, `BatchStage`, `DeduplicateStage`, and `ValidateStage` compose to build complex transformation logic. The `RetryableStage` wrapper adds resilience.

- **Loaders**: `PostgresLoader` builds batch upserts using parameterized queries. `S3Loader` writes Hive-partitioned JSONL for analytics query engines.

- **Reliability**: Dead letter queues capture failed records for inspection and reprocessing. Checkpointing enables resumability after crashes. Idempotent upserts make re-processing safe.

- **Monitoring**: Per-stage metrics (in, out, errors) drive Prometheus dashboards and OpenTelemetry traces follow individual records across stages. Alert on rate-of-change of errors and DLQ size, not absolute thresholds.

- **Where Go fits in the 2026 stack**: Managed ingestion (Fivetran, Airbyte) + warehouse-native transformation (dbt) + orchestration (Airflow, Dagster, Prefect) is the default ELT path. Reserve hand-written Go pipelines for custom extractors, latency-sensitive stream enrichment on Kafka, and Temporal workflows that coordinate the rest. Use Debezium for CDC, a schema registry (Confluent, Apicurio) with Avro or Protobuf for wire safety, and classify errors into retriable vs permanent so retries help instead of hurting.

### Junior to FAANG: What to Carry Into Your First On-Call Shift

The chapter introduced many primitives. At a junior level, four habits matter more than any library. First, never advance a watermark before the sink commit. The one line of code that separates a pipeline that loses data from one that does not is the ordering between the sink write and the checkpoint write. Burn this into muscle memory by writing the crash-drill exercise from the exercises section. Second, make every sink idempotent. Upsert keyed on a stable primary key, content-addressed object storage keys, or a dedup table in front of a non-idempotent sink. If you cannot name the idempotency strategy of every pipeline you own, you do not own them, you are hoping on their behalf. Third, reconcile row counts. A pipeline that silently loses one in ten thousand rows is worse than one that crashes every hour, because the crash alerts you and the loss does not. Fourth, classify errors into retriable and permanent at the point of failure. Retrying a 4xx forever is the most common bug in junior ETL code.

Interview entry-point recap. For an L3 or L4 data engineering loop the bar is: explain at-least-once versus exactly-once with a concrete example, write an idempotent `MERGE` from memory, sketch a pipeline with DLQ and checkpoint, describe why event time differs from processing time, and walk through the first ten minutes of a lag-climbing incident using metrics before logs. The chapter's Go code is a vehicle for internalising these patterns. The patterns transfer to Python, Java, Scala, SQL, and any future language you work in.

### Senior at FAANG: Platform Mindset, Maturity Framing, and the Next Three Years

At staff and principal level, the takeaway from this chapter is not a framework. It is a maturity ladder and a governance model. An organization at L1 (cron plus scripts) cannot buy its way to L5 (self-service platform with contracts and lineage) by installing Airflow. The journey is three years of investment across orchestration, observability, contracts, cost, and governance, sequenced so that each level delivers independent value and each team can opt in at its own pace.

The sequenced bets for a data platform team chartered in 2026 look like this. Year one delivers L2 to L3: pick one orchestrator (Airflow, Dagster, or Prefect, with Temporal for durable workflows that are not batch DAGs), roll out OpenTelemetry instrumentation as an SDK so every pipeline emits the same metrics and traces, stand up a shared DLQ pattern with a triage CLI, and publish an SLO framework (freshness, completeness, accuracy) that every pipeline owner commits to. Year two delivers L3 to L4: schema registry with enforced compatibility, data contracts in a central repo with CI enforcement, OpenLineage emission wired into the orchestrator, and cost attribution per pipeline via warehouse query tagging. Year three delivers L4 to L5: self-service pipeline generation from contract YAML, automated data quality gating via Great Expectations or equivalent, column-level lineage for impact analysis, and chargeback visible to owning teams. At every level, the platform team publishes a charter with non-goals so scope does not accrete without limit.

ADR discipline separates senior engineers who build durable systems from senior engineers who build load-bearing career risk. Every significant pipeline decision (orchestrator choice, storage format, delivery guarantee, CDC versus batch, buy versus build) gets an ADR with context, options, decision, consequences, and review date. After eighteen months the ADR archive is the organizational memory that lets new staff engineers audit drift, unwind bad decisions, and onboard quickly. Without ADRs every migration starts from scratch and every incident post-mortem has to reconstruct intent from git history.

The bar-raiser lens on this chapter. A staff interviewer hearing a candidate summarize this material is listening for three things. Does the candidate distinguish tactical anti-patterns (unbounded channels, missing DLQ) from organizational anti-patterns (no contracts, no chargeback, no charter)? Can the candidate place a given company on the L1 to L5 maturity ladder and name the next one to two investments that would move it up? Can the candidate articulate the cost and operational burden of each architectural choice in dollars and on-call pages per quarter, not just in abstractions? Candidates who stay in tactical territory cap at L5. Candidates who fluently move between tactical and organizational framing are in L6 and L7 territory.

The industry trajectory through 2026 to 2029 is clear. Data contracts graduate from a movement to a default. Lineage becomes table-stakes, not a differentiator. Lakehouse formats (Iceberg as the likely winner over Delta and Hudi given the 2024 to 2025 consolidation) replace proprietary warehouse-native formats for the landing zone. Streaming becomes the default transport, with batch as an optimization for cost. AI-generated pipelines and LLM-assisted data quality rules become a meaningful productivity lever, with the corresponding governance problem of verifying generated code against contracts. Staff engineers who are investing in the platform primitives in this chapter (contracts, lineage, observability, idempotency, cost attribution) will ride the trend. Staff engineers who are investing in one-off pipeline optimizations will find themselves out of alignment with where their companies are moving.

Close with a single discipline. Every pipeline you own should have an on-call runbook, an owner, an SLO, a DLQ triage story, and an ADR that justifies its existence. If any of those five are missing, close that gap before you write the next feature. This is what "production grade" means at FAANG, and it is what separates a senior engineer who writes code from a senior engineer who runs a platform.
