## 37H.6 Company Case Studies

### Uber: ClickHouse for Real-Time Analytics

Uber processes 100+ trillion rows in ClickHouse for real-time trip analytics. Key patterns:
- **RealtimeMergeTree**: custom ClickHouse engine for Uber's low-latency ingestion
- **Materialized views**: pre-aggregate trip data by city+hour for dashboard queries (< 100ms)
- **Distributed tables**: shard by `city_id` for geo-locality - queries for NYC trips only scan NYC shards
- **Zookeeper-less replication**: Uber patched ClickHouse to use their own consensus system at scale
- **Scale**: 1 million events/second ingested, petabytes of compressed storage

### Time-Series at Scale: TimescaleDB Patterns

Large-scale IoT and observability platforms adopt TimescaleDB for its PostgreSQL compatibility and automatic chunk management. Common production patterns include:
- **Hypertable partitioning**: 1-day chunks for high-write workloads, so expired chunks are dropped instantly rather than row-by-row deleted
- **Continuous aggregates**: pre-compute play counts, request rates, or sensor averages per hour, so queries read pre-built rollups instead of scanning raw data
- **Compression**: 10-50x compression ratios on older chunks, since time-series values within a segment tend to be similar
- **Retention policies**: automatic drop of chunks older than a threshold, keeping storage bounded without manual cron jobs
- **Cross-domain joins**: because TimescaleDB runs on PostgreSQL, time-series data can be joined with relational metadata (users, devices, regions) in a single query

### LinkedIn: Graph Database for Professional Network

LinkedIn uses a custom graph engine (LIquid) to serve its social graph:
- **2nd-degree connections**: "People you may know" requires 2-hop graph traversal - 800M+ nodes
- **Degree of connection**: fastest path between any two LinkedIn members calculated in real-time
- **Endorsements/Skills graph**: weighted edges for skill endorsements drive hiring search ranking
- **PYMK (People You May Know)**: hybrid graph + ML - graph provides candidate set, ML model ranks
- **Scale**: 5 billion edges, sub-100ms 2-hop traversal at 100K QPS

### Airbnb: Druid for Real-Time OLAP

Airbnb uses Apache Druid for sub-second analytics on booking data:
- **Pre-aggregation**: Druid ingests raw events and pre-computes rollups - avoids full scans
- **Bitmap indexes**: efficient filtering on low-cardinality dimensions (country, device_type, status)
- **Real-time segment**: Druid maintains a real-time segment for last 1 hour, merges into historical segments
- **Superset**: Apache Superset (Airbnb's open-source contribution) queries Druid for dashboards

### Junior → FAANG: Prerequisite Gaps and First-Job Context

Read these case studies with a specific lens. For each company, ask three questions. What was the original bottleneck that forced them off their previous stack. What specific property of the new engine solved it. What did they pay (in cost, complexity, or consistency) for the fix. If you cannot answer those three for any case study, you are reading the marketing, not the architecture. Hiring managers at FAANG test this exact reading skill in system design interviews. "Tell me why Netflix chose ClickHouse" is a trap question if you just say "for analytics." The right answer includes the specific access pattern (high-cardinality filtering on bounded time ranges), the failure mode of the prior system (Druid's segment management cost or Redshift's concurrency cap), and the cost (operational learning curve and eventual-consistency tolerance).

Worked example, Uber's choice of ClickHouse for Logging (the Logging platform, internally called M3DB for metrics and ClickHouse for logs and traces since 2021). The pain: petabyte-scale log ingestion was too expensive on Elasticsearch because Elasticsearch's inverted-index-per-field approach exploded storage cost for high-cardinality fields like trace IDs. ClickHouse's columnar storage with ZSTD compression achieved 10x better compression on the same data, and the MergeTree engine absorbed 1M+ events/sec without the JVM GC pauses Elasticsearch suffered at that rate. The cost: ClickHouse has weaker text search (no native inverted index until 2023's experimental `full_text` index, still experimental in 2026), no per-field analyzers, and a query language (SQL) less ergonomic for log exploration than KQL or Lucene. They accepted the trade because their primary access pattern was "find all logs for this trace ID in the last hour," a point lookup on an indexed column, not full-text search. If your primary access pattern is "find logs containing this error phrase" across unknown fields, Elasticsearch is still right.

Interview entry-points. Why do streaming platforms (Discord, Cloudflare) converge on ClickHouse for logs. (Compression economics and ingestion throughput.) Why does Netflix use ClickHouse for observability but Cassandra for user data. (Access pattern: analytical aggregates vs high-concurrency key lookups with low-latency writes.) Why did LinkedIn build a custom graph database instead of using Neo4j. (Scale: 5B edges and 100k QPS exceeded Neo4j's single-leader write ceiling in 2013, though modern Neo4j plus Fabric could handle it now.) Why does PayPal use a graph for fraud detection when they already have a data warehouse. (Fraud rings are multi-hop relationships that relational queries express poorly past depth 3.)

First-job context. When you join a team that "uses ClickHouse" or "uses Neo4j," your first week should be spent reading the ADR (architecture decision record) that justified the choice. If there is no ADR, write one by interviewing the people who made the call. You will learn more in those conversations than from any documentation, and you will be the person who knows why the system is shaped the way it is. That knowledge compounds.

### Senior @ FAANG: Extended Case Studies and Architectural Patterns

**Netflix on ClickHouse.** Netflix runs ClickHouse for observability and experimentation analytics, replacing and supplementing their prior Druid deployment. The scale in 2026 is roughly 30 PB of logs and metrics across a few dozen clusters, ingesting tens of millions of events per second. Netflix contributions to ClickHouse include `Keeper` integration work (Netflix was an early adopter of ClickHouse Keeper to replace ZooKeeper), S3-backed storage tiers, and query-level observability hooks. The pattern to learn from Netflix: **storage tiering with S3 as cold tier.** Hot data on local NVMe lives for 7 days, warm on gp3 EBS for 30 days, cold on S3 forever (or until retention), all transparent to the query via ClickHouse's disk and storage policies. This is the pattern that makes petabyte retention economical.

**Uber on Pinot and ClickHouse.** Uber runs both. **Pinot** for user-facing dashboards that need sub-second queries with high concurrency (UberEats restaurant dashboards, driver earnings, rider receipts), because Pinot's star-tree indexes and segment pruning optimize for predictable aggregates at high QPS. **ClickHouse** for ad-hoc exploration and logs, where flexibility matters more than latency. The architectural lesson: **one engine per user-facing latency requirement.** Pinot is for 100ms p99 at 10k QPS on known queries. ClickHouse is for 1 second p99 at 100 QPS on unpredictable queries. Trying to make one engine do both means compromising on one axis. Uber also built **Apache Pinot's real-time segment loading** to handle Kafka-sourced streaming ingestion with exactly-once semantics, which is still the strongest real-time OLAP story in open source.

**Cloudflare Logpush.** Cloudflare processes tens of trillions of HTTP request logs per day across 300+ edge PoPs. Logpush is their customer-facing pipeline that ships logs from edge to customer-chosen destinations (S3, GCS, Splunk, Datadog, Cloudflare R2). Internally, Cloudflare uses ClickHouse at massive scale for analytics (since 2018, documented in their engineering blog "HTTP Analytics for 6M requests per second using ClickHouse"). The lesson: **edge computation before ingestion.** Cloudflare pre-aggregates at the edge (counting, sampling, dimensional reduction) so the central ClickHouse cluster receives aggregated tuples rather than raw logs for the analytics product, while Logpush ships raw logs as a separate pipeline for customers who need them. This two-path architecture (aggregated hot path, raw cold path) is the pattern for any system where raw data volume exceeds analytical query needs.

**Discord migrations.** Discord migrated messages from Cassandra to ScyllaDB in 2022 (documented in "How Discord Stores Trillions of Messages") due to Cassandra's Java GC pauses at their scale (trillions of rows, billions of messages daily). ScyllaDB's shard-per-core architecture and C++ implementation eliminated the GC tail latencies. The staff-level lesson is not "use ScyllaDB," it is the **migration methodology**: they built a dual-write path, then dual-read with comparison logging, then shifted reads gradually while keeping writes dual until confidence. Total migration took 9 months for 177 TB. The pattern applies to any engine swap at scale: dual-write, compare-read, gradual cutover, irreversible cutover only when drift has been zero for weeks.

**PayPal fraud graph.** PayPal's fraud detection graph models users, devices, IPs, funding instruments, and transactions, with billions of edges. The primary query is ring detection: "given this user, find other users reachable within 4 hops through shared attributes (device, IP, card fingerprint)." On relational this was 4-way self-joins that took minutes at P99. On a graph (originally Neo4j, later evolved) the same query is sub-second. The architectural lesson is **graph for anomaly detection, relational for settled state.** PayPal does not run money movement on the graph. It runs fraud scoring on the graph, and the fraud score is written back into the transactional store as a column. The graph is a system of reference projected from the system of record. This is the pattern for any graph-in-fraud deployment.

**LinkedIn economic graph.** LinkedIn's economic graph models members, companies, jobs, skills, schools, and their relationships, served historically by custom in-house graph layers over its distributed stores, later consolidated into a dedicated graph engine called LIquid (described in their 2020 paper). The access pattern at 1B members is "second-degree connections for people you may know" at 100k QPS. They solved it with **partitioned graph storage plus precomputed 2-hop neighborhoods cached in the serving tier**, because recomputing 2-hop traversals at 100k QPS against cold storage is infeasible. The lesson: **at extreme scale, graph databases are often not general-purpose engines but purpose-built indexes for a specific traversal pattern, with the graph recomputed offline and served from cache.** If your QPS is in the thousands and graph depth is 3 or less, Neo4j is fine. If your QPS is in the hundreds of thousands and the graph is 1B+ nodes, you will end up building something custom.

**Airbnb listings graph.** Airbnb uses graph models for listing similarity (listings that travelers view together, book together, or share amenities) to drive search ranking and recommendations. The graph is computed offline in Spark and served through a graph-shaped index (internally called Zipline for feature store, with graph embeddings computed via GraphSAGE or node2vec). The pattern: **graph as a feature engineering substrate for ML**, not as an online query engine. The staff lesson: when your graph question is "what is the embedding of node X given its neighborhood," you do not need Neo4j. You need a graph-aware ML pipeline (PyG, DGL, NVIDIA cuGraph) and a vector store (FAISS, pgvector, Milvus) for serving. Neo4j's GDS library covers this, but at Airbnb scale you run it outside the operational database.

**Walmart fraud detection.** Walmart uses Neo4j for retail fraud (return abuse, loyalty abuse, organized retail crime rings) at a scale involving hundreds of millions of customers and billions of transactions. Their architecture pattern: **Neo4j with hourly batch ingest from Teradata and Snowflake, Cypher queries issued by fraud analysts and automated scoring jobs, results written back as flags to the transactional system.** This is the typical enterprise graph deployment: graph as a **complementary store for relationship-heavy queries**, not a replacement for the data warehouse. The operational lesson is that keeping Neo4j in sync with the warehouse via hourly batch (rather than CDC) is acceptable for fraud where the use case tolerates hours of staleness. Real-time fraud (transaction-time scoring) requires CDC streaming into graph, which is harder.

**FINRA on TigerGraph.** FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) runs market surveillance on TigerGraph, processing 75 billion market events per day looking for manipulation patterns (spoofing, layering, wash trading). TigerGraph was chosen over Neo4j for its distributed architecture and its GSQL language, which expresses multi-hop analytics as MapReduce-style accumulators. The lesson: **for graphs beyond 10 billion edges with heavy batch analytics, TigerGraph or NebulaGraph is stronger than Neo4j.** Neo4j 5.x with Fabric has closed much of the gap, but at the 100B+ edge scale, the purpose-built distributed graph engines still win.

**NASA lessons learned.** NASA built a Neo4j-based knowledge graph for cross-mission lessons learned, connecting incidents, root causes, remediations, and project documents. The scale is modest (millions of nodes, tens of millions of edges) but the use case is archetypal **knowledge graph**: heterogeneous entity types, flexible relationship modeling, exploratory queries by analysts, semantic enrichment via LLMs in the 2024+ architecture. The lesson: **knowledge graphs are about modeling flexibility, not scale.** If your domain has 30+ entity types and the relationships change as understanding evolves, a graph database's schemaless flexibility is worth more than raw performance.

**eBay knowledge graph.** eBay built a product knowledge graph (2019 onward) connecting products, categories, attributes, brands, and their semantic equivalents across locales, used for search query understanding and product recommendations. The architecture uses JanusGraph (Gremlin) on top of Cassandra and Elasticsearch, chosen because JanusGraph's pluggable storage let them reuse their existing Cassandra infrastructure. The staff lesson: **JanusGraph is the right choice only if you already run Cassandra or HBase and the operational reuse is valuable.** Greenfield, you would pick Neo4j, Dgraph, or a managed service. eBay's architecture also illustrates **search-graph hybrid**: Elasticsearch for text retrieval, JanusGraph for structural traversal, joined in the application layer for query-time enrichment.

**Cross-cutting architectural patterns.** Looking across all these case studies, five patterns repeat. One, **storage tiering** (hot NVMe, warm EBS, cold S3) for any engine above a few TB. Two, **system of record plus systems of reference** with CDC fan-out, not dual writes. Three, **purpose-built engines per access pattern**, accepting polyglot cost as the price of performance. Four, **migration by dual-write and gradual cutover**, never big-bang swaps. Five, **graph as a projection for specific traversal queries**, often recomputed offline, rarely the system of record. Memorize these five patterns. They are the skeleton of every FAANG-scale data architecture you will design or debug in your career.
