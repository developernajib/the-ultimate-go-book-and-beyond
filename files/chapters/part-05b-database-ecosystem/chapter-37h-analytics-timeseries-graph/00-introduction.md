# Chapter 37H: Analytics, Time-Series & Graph Databases

*"Columnar storage is not a trick. It is a fundamental rethinking of how data should be organized for questions, not for answers."* - Alexey Milovidov, ClickHouse Creator

Most production systems run two fundamentally different workloads on the same data: transactional writes that demand low latency and high concurrency, and analytical reads that demand high throughput across billions of rows. Trying to serve both from a single row-oriented RDBMS is one of the most common and costly architectural mistakes in the industry. Row stores like PostgreSQL and MySQL are optimized for fetching complete records quickly - ideal for OLTP. But when a data analyst runs `SELECT AVG(revenue) FROM orders WHERE region = 'EU'` across three years of data, the database must scan and deserialize every column of every row even though only two columns matter. Columnar databases like ClickHouse invert this layout: each column is stored independently and compressed together, making analytical scans orders of magnitude faster while using a fraction of the I/O.

Time-series data presents its own challenge. Metrics, sensor readings, financial ticks, and application telemetry all share a common characteristic: they are append-heavy, query by time range, and benefit enormously from time-based partitioning and compression. InfluxDB was built specifically for this shape of data, offering a purpose-built query language and automatic retention policies. TimescaleDB takes a different approach - it extends PostgreSQL with hypertables that automatically partition time-series data while preserving full SQL compatibility, giving you the ergonomics of a relational database with the performance of a specialized time-series engine.

Graph databases complete the picture. When your data is fundamentally about relationships - social connections, fraud rings, knowledge graphs, recommendation networks - relational joins across adjacency tables become exponentially expensive as the graph depth increases. Neo4j's Cypher query language and DGraph's GraphQL-native interface let you traverse millions of edges in milliseconds, expressing multi-hop queries like "users within three degrees of separation who also purchased the same product" in a handful of lines. This chapter shows you how to build Go services that read from and write to all three categories, and how to choose the right category when the data model is first being designed.

**What you'll learn in this chapter:**
- Design ClickHouse schemas with MergeTree engines, materialized views, and partition pruning for sub-second OLAP queries
- Build time-series ingestion pipelines using InfluxDB line protocol and TimescaleDB hypertables from Go
- Write Cypher and GraphQL traversal queries against Neo4j and DGraph, integrating them with Go gRPC services
- Apply columnar compression strategies and understand how ClickHouse achieves 10-100x compression ratios
- Implement real-time analytics dashboards by combining Kafka ingestion with ClickHouse materialized views
- Distinguish OLAP, OLTP, and HTAP workload profiles and choose the right engine for each
- Handle schema evolution, backfills, and data retention policies without downtime
- Build polyglot persistence layers in Go that route queries to the appropriate specialized database

**Why this matters at scale:**
Cloudflare's analytics pipeline ingests on the order of ten million-plus events per second into ClickHouse, storing petabytes of HTTP log data that stays queryable at interactive latency. TimescaleDB powers time-series workloads at companies like Maersk (fleet tracking), Walmart (IoT sensor monitoring), and Doctolib (appointment analytics), where PostgreSQL compatibility lets them join time-series data with relational metadata without introducing a second query language. LinkedIn's People You May Know feature is powered by a graph engine that traverses a social graph of over 900 million members in real time. These are not niche workloads. Analytics, time-series, and graph queries represent a large share of total query volume at data-intensive companies.

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 37G (DynamoDB and NewSQL), basic SQL fluency, familiarity with Go database interfaces (`database/sql`, connection pooling), and Chapter 52F (Kafka and streaming concepts recommended but not required).

### Junior → FAANG: where this chapter fits in a first analytics job

If you are arriving here from a CRUD background, the mental gap to close is that analytics engines are not just "faster Postgres". They trade properties a junior engineer assumes are free. Single-row point lookups are slow in ClickHouse. There is no UPDATE that rewrites a row in place. Transactions across statements do not exist. Foreign keys are absent. Those are not bugs, they are the cost of storing one billion rows per column contiguously and compressed. The first worked example every new analytics engineer should run is a side by side. Load one month of web log data into both Postgres and ClickHouse. Run `SELECT count(), avg(latency_ms) FROM logs WHERE status_code >= 500 GROUP BY toStartOfHour(ts)`. On Postgres with a btree index you will read gigabytes and wait minutes. On ClickHouse with a `ORDER BY (toStartOfHour(ts), status_code)` primary key you will read tens of megabytes and wait milliseconds. The difference is not query planner cleverness, it is the physical layout of bytes on disk.

At entry level interviews for data platform teams at FAANG, Snowflake, Databricks, Confluent, or mid-stage analytics startups, the questions you will actually be asked are concrete. What is the difference between OLTP and OLAP. Why does a columnar store compress better than a row store. What is a materialized view and when does it become stale. Why would you pick TimescaleDB over a vanilla Postgres table with a btree on a timestamp column. How does InfluxDB differ from Prometheus. You do not need to have run petabyte clusters, but you need to answer these crisply with one concrete example each. Practice saying "column stores get 10x compression because adjacent values in a column share a dictionary and run length encoding works" without hedging. That is the bar for a new grad or junior SRE rotating into a data team.

The first ticket you will pick up on the job is almost always one of three shapes. A dashboard is slow, find the query and add a skip index or a materialized view. A new event schema is being added, write the migration and backfill script. Retention is being tightened, write the TTL policy and verify it drops the right partitions. Get comfortable with each of those shapes by replicating them in a hobby project before your first on call rotation.

### Senior @ FAANG: the analytics landscape a staff engineer is expected to navigate

At the staff and principal level, the question is never "which database do we use". It is "what is the data platform shape that supports five years of product roadmap, how do we bound cost at petabyte scale, and which engine gets retired first when the org consolidates". You are expected to hold a coherent view of the 2026 analytics ecosystem across at least three axes: storage architecture, compute model, and operational surface.

On storage, the industry has converged on object storage as the source of truth. ClickHouse Cloud, Snowflake, Databricks, BigQuery, and the new ClickHouse 25.x shared nothing to shared storage rewrite all land on S3, GCS, or Azure Blob with local NVMe caches. The Parquet plus Iceberg plus object store triple is the new default cold tier. TimescaleDB 2.14+ added tiered storage to S3 and the OSS project is pushing that harder in 2.17. InfluxDB v3 IOx is Parquet on object storage from day one, written in Rust on Apache Arrow and DataFusion. The staff engineer question is not "should we use object storage", it is "what is our cache hit ratio target, what is our p99 cold read latency, and at what query volume does compute egress from S3 become the dominant cost line item".

On compute, the 2026 comparison matrix you should be able to draw on a whiteboard includes ClickHouse (self managed or Cloud), DuckDB (embedded, single node, Parquet native, zero ops), Databricks Photon (Spark SQL replacement with vectorized C++ execution), Snowflake (virtual warehouses, separation of storage and compute, proprietary file format), BigQuery (Dremel, fully serverless, slot based pricing), Redshift (legacy at this point, RA3 nodes, Spectrum for S3), Trino or Presto (federated query, no storage, great for data lake), StarRocks and Apache Doris (MPP analytical, strong on real time updates, ClickHouse competitor with better joins), and the time series specific lane of InfluxDB v3, Prometheus, VictoriaMetrics, QuestDB, TimescaleDB, Grafana Mimir, and Thanos. A staff engineer picks between them on five dimensions: ingest rate, query latency p99, join capability, update semantics, and operational overhead. ClickHouse wins on ingest and scan latency but loses on joins and updates. Snowflake wins on zero ops and joins but loses on cost per scan. Trino wins on federation but loses on single table speed. DuckDB wins on simplicity and is increasingly a serious answer for anything under a terabyte.

On operational surface, the senior framing is that every analytical database is actually three products stitched together: a storage format, a query engine, and a control plane. When you evaluate ClickHouse Cloud versus self hosted, you are not evaluating ClickHouse, you are evaluating the control plane (autoscaling, backup, replication topology, query routing, noisy neighbor isolation). The same applies to Timescale Cloud versus self hosted Timescale, and InfluxDB Cloud versus self hosted IOx. At FAANG scale the build versus buy math usually favors buy for the control plane and self host for the data plane, because the SRE headcount to run a global control plane is where the real cost hides.

The cross cutting principle is that analytics data platforms compose. A mature stack at a large company looks like Kafka or Kinesis for ingest, a stream processor (Flink, ksqlDB, or Materialize) for normalization, ClickHouse or Druid or Pinot for operational analytics with sub second dashboards, Iceberg on S3 as the cold archive and lakehouse format, Snowflake or BigQuery or Databricks for ad hoc analyst SQL, and a separate time series lane (Prometheus plus VictoriaMetrics or Mimir) for infrastructure metrics. As a staff engineer you will spend more time deciding which workload belongs in which lane, and writing the ADR that justifies it, than you will writing SQL.
