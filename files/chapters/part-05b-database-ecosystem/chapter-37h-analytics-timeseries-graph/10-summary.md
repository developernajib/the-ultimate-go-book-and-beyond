## Summary

Analytics, time-series, and graph databases each excel at specific query patterns that general-purpose databases handle poorly. Core focus areas include:

- **ClickHouse**: MergeTree columnar engine, LowCardinality types, materialized views, ReplicatedMergeTree, funnel analysis with windowFunnel, batch INSERT via native protocol
- **TimescaleDB**: hypertable auto-partitioning, continuous aggregates, compression policies, retention policies, time_bucket queries, anomaly detection with window functions
- **InfluxDB v3**: IOx architecture with Arrow/Parquet, SQL queries, line protocol writes, DATE_BIN aggregation
- **Neo4j**: Cypher pattern matching, MERGE for upserts, variable-length paths, shortestPath, collaborative filtering, fraud detection, GDS algorithms
- **Polyglot persistence**: combining PostgreSQL (OLTP) + Redis (cache) + ClickHouse (analytics) + Elasticsearch (search) + Neo4j (graph) in one application
- **Case studies**: Uber ClickHouse analytics, TimescaleDB production patterns, LinkedIn graph network, Airbnb Druid OLAP

Key insight: **choose your database based on your query patterns**, not familiarity. The right tool dramatically reduces complexity and infrastructure cost.

### Junior → FAANG: Prerequisite gaps, worked decision tree, and the "why did this fail?" reflex

If you are reading this summary and half the terms still feel abstract, that is the prerequisite gap. Fix it in this order before your next interview.

**Step 1: Internalise a one-page decision tree.** Start at the top with the question "what is the dominant access pattern?" If it is "point read by primary key", pick KV (Redis, DynamoDB, or Postgres with a pk lookup). If it is "transactional writes across multiple rows", pick an OLTP relational store (Postgres or MySQL). If it is "aggregate over billions of rows", pick a columnar store (ClickHouse, BigQuery, Snowflake). If it is "append-only measurements at regular intervals", pick a time-series store (TimescaleDB, InfluxDB v3, Prometheus for metrics). If it is "traversal of depth 2 or more through connected entities", pick a graph store (Neo4j, Dgraph). If it is "full-text or fuzzy match", pick a search store (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Meilisearch, Typesense). If it is "approximate nearest neighbor on embeddings", pick a vector store (pgvector, Qdrant, Milvus). Memorize this tree. Recite it in interviews.

**Step 2: Learn to name the failure mode of each system.** ClickHouse fails on tiny inserts and high-cardinality GROUP BYs. TimescaleDB fails when chunk_time_interval is wrong for your ingest rate. InfluxDB v1 failed on cardinality, v3 fails on object-storage latency if you did not warm the cache. Neo4j fails on supernodes (one node with 10M edges). Every bar-raiser tests this. The junior candidate says "it is fast". The FAANG candidate says "here is how it goes slow and what I would do".

**Step 3: The "why did this fail?" reflex, at chapter scope.** Pick a polyglot design from this chapter. Ask: what breaks first under 10x load? Under a bad deploy? Under a regional outage? If your Redis leaderboard is fed from TimescaleDB and TimescaleDB has a long vacuum, the Redis slice goes stale. If your ClickHouse is fed by CDC from Postgres and Debezium crashes, dashboards freeze. Name the coupling, name the SLO, name the runbook. That is what senior means.

**First-job worked example.** You join a team with Postgres and nothing else. Marketing wants cohort retention dashboards. The junior move is "let me introduce Kafka, Debezium, ClickHouse, Redis, and Grafana". The FAANG move is "let me measure the current query, add a materialized view refreshed hourly, and ship a Grafana dashboard today. If we outgrow it in six months, here is the ADR for ClickHouse". Interviewers score the second answer higher because it demonstrates cost awareness and incrementalism.

### Senior @ FAANG: Ecosystem decision trees, platform charter, maturity ladder, and the sunset discipline

At staff and principal level, the summary of this chapter is not a list of databases. It is a written charter that tells the rest of the company which system to use, when to ask for an exception, and how to deprecate what is already running.

**The full ecosystem decision tree, annotated with thresholds.** Start with the workload category from the junior tree, then overlay scale and SLO thresholds. OLTP: Postgres up to roughly 10 TB and 50K writes/s on a well-tuned single writer, then reach for Citus, Aurora, Spanner, or CockroachDB depending on whether the bottleneck is write throughput, read latency, or cross-region consistency. OLAP: DuckDB or Postgres for under 100 GB, ClickHouse or StarRocks for 100 GB to 10 PB, a cloud warehouse (BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift) when you want separation of compute and storage without running the cluster, and a lakehouse (Iceberg + Trino/DataFusion) when you want open formats and multi-engine access. Time-series: Prometheus for metrics with 14-day retention, TimescaleDB when you need SQL joins with relational metadata, InfluxDB v3 when cardinality is unbounded and object storage is cheap, ClickHouse when you need the OLAP surface too. Graph: Neo4j for property graph under 10B edges, Dgraph or JanusGraph when you need horizontal scale-out, a custom RocksDB-backed adjacency store when graph is the whole product. Search: Elasticsearch or OpenSearch for ecosystem breadth, Typesense or Meilisearch for simpler operations at small scale, Vespa for ranking-heavy workloads. Vector: pgvector for under 10M vectors co-located with relational data, Qdrant or Milvus for billions of vectors with hybrid filters, a managed service (Pinecone, Weaviate Cloud) when you do not want to run the cluster. Write this tree on one page. It is the deliverable.

**The data-platform charter.** Mission: serve every internal analytical workload with the smallest sanctioned set of systems. Paved road: one OLTP (Postgres), one OLAP (ClickHouse), one time-series (TimescaleDB or InfluxDB v3), one graph (Neo4j), one search (Elasticsearch), one vector (pgvector or Qdrant), one event bus (Kafka), one query federation (Trino). Exception process: any new database category requires an ADR reviewed by the platform team, with a named owner, an SLO, a cost model, and a sunset plan. Governance: all producers register schemas in a central registry, all datasets declare owners and retention, all queries are attributable to a team for cost allocation. Privacy: column-level tags enforced at query time, GDPR erasure honored within 30 days, PII never lands in analytical stores without tokenization.

**Maturity ladder, applied to this chapter.** L1: Postgres only, one analyst with a read replica. L2: nightly ETL into a warehouse, Looker or Metabase for dashboards, freshness measured in days. L3: CDC from OLTP into ClickHouse, hot dashboards pre-aggregated, freshness in minutes. L4: streaming-first pipeline with Kafka, multiple specialized stores (time-series, graph, search) fed by one event bus, schema registry enforced, freshness in seconds for hot paths. L5: polyglot platform with data contracts, central schema registry, column-level lineage, query federation across all stores, per-dataset SLOs, cost attribution per team, privacy tags enforced at query time, automated deprecation of unused datasets. The staff+ deliverable is a written roadmap from your current level to L5, with named quarterly milestones and named owners.

**The sunset discipline.** Every system in this chapter will be legacy within a decade. The staff engineer plans for that now. For each sanctioned system, maintain a deprecation ADR template: what signals would trigger replacement (cost growth, vendor risk, technology shift), what the replacement candidates are, what the migration pattern looks like (dual-write, CDC fan-out, shadow-read verification), what the rollback trigger is, and what the decommission checklist looks like (data export, consumer migration, backup archival, account closure). Without this discipline, platforms accrete. With it, the cost envelope stays bounded and the team keeps optionality.

**Bar-raiser closing signal.** A principal-grade answer to "summarize this ecosystem" is not a list of databases. It is three artifacts: the decision tree, the charter, and the maturity roadmap. If a candidate produces all three in a 60-minute interview, that is strong-hire signal. If they produce the decision tree only, that is L5. If they produce none and list features, that is L4 at best. The summary of an ecosystem chapter is therefore a summary of organizational practice, not a summary of technology.
