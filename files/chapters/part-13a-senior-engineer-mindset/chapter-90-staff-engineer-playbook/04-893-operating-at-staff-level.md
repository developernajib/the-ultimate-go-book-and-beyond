## 90.3 Operating at Staff Level

### Time Management

Time management is arguably the single most important skill for a staff engineer because your scope is broad and demands on your attention come from every direction. Unlike senior engineers who have relatively predictable schedules, staff engineers must deliberately structure their time or risk spending every day in meetings with no time left for the deep technical work that makes them credible. The allocation below is a starting guideline that you should adjust based on your archetype and current organizational needs.

### The Staff Engineer Weekly Routine

Having a repeatable weekly structure helps you avoid the trap of being purely reactive to whatever lands in your inbox or Slack. The routine below provides a template that batches similar activities together, protecting deep work time on specific days while keeping relationship-building and coordination on others. You should adapt this to your team's rhythms, but the principle of grouping similar work and protecting at least one full day for deep focus is universal.

```
MONDAY:
□ Weekly planning (30 min)
  - Review calendar
  - Identify key priorities
  - Set weekly goals

□ 1:1 with manager (30 min)
□ Team standup/sync

TUESDAY:
□ Design review or architecture meeting
□ Deep work block (afternoon)
□ Write or review design doc

WEDNESDAY:
□ 1:1s with reports or mentees
□ Cross-team collaboration meetings
□ Code review time

THURSDAY:
□ Protected deep work day (limit meetings)
□ Strategic work: research, prototyping
□ Write RFC or documentation

FRIDAY:
□ Weekly wrap-up
  - What did I accomplish?
  - What am I blocked on?
  - What's important for next week?

□ Async catch-up (Slack, email, PRs)
□ 1:1 with peer (optional)
□ Learning time (reading, courses)
```

### Junior → FAANG: Practicing Staff-Level Operating Rhythms Now

The biggest surprise most engineers have when they reach staff level is the volume of writing and coordination work relative to coding. If you have never practiced operating in that rhythm, the shock can stall your effectiveness for six months to a year. Start practicing now: take one coordination or communication problem on your current team and own it fully, including the meeting facilitation, the written follow-up, and the tracking of action items until resolution.

Pay attention to how staff engineers around you manage their time. Ask them directly what they spent their last week on. Most are happy to share. You will find that the answer involves far more "talking to people and writing things down" than you expect, and far less "implementing features." That is not a bug in their operating model. It is the product of operating at a scope where direct implementation is not the most effective activity available.

Interview entry point: if a FAANG interviewer asks "what does a typical week look like for you," describe a week that includes both technical depth and coordination breadth. Engineers who describe only coding signal senior-level scope. Engineers who describe only meetings signal management track. The target is a credible balance that demonstrates you have already begun operating above your current level.

### Senior @ FAANG: Operating Cadence as a Governance Mechanism

At FAANG, a staff engineer's operating cadence is not just personal productivity management. It is a governance mechanism. The weekly 1:1 with an engineering manager is the primary channel through which staff engineers communicate risk, surface organizational impediments, and negotiate scope. Engineers who treat this meeting as a status report are leaving its most valuable function unused. The meeting should primarily answer: what is the most important thing the manager needs to know that they would not find out otherwise?

Time allocation at staff level must be explicitly managed and periodically renegotiated. A common failure pattern at L6: a staff engineer accepts meeting invitations from every team that finds their expertise valuable, until their calendar is 60% fragmented meetings and they have no time for the focused writing and thinking that staff-level work actually requires. The solution is to treat focus time with the same protection as production SLAs: block it, guard it, and escalate when it is consistently violated.

Measurement matters. The best staff engineers at Google and Meta track their own time allocation explicitly, at least quarterly, to detect drift. If deep technical work has dropped below 25% of a week consistently, it is a signal that something has shifted in the org's demands or in the engineer's own boundary-setting. That drift should be diagnosed and corrected, not ignored.

At the principal level, operating cadence shifts further toward "sense and respond" patterns. A principal engineer may spend an entire month on a single written strategy document, then three weeks in back-to-back stakeholder meetings to build alignment, then two weeks heads-down in code to validate the strategy's feasibility. The ability to recognize which phase you are in, and to protect the quality of work in each phase, is a distinguishing characteristic of engineers who operate effectively above the L6 level.
