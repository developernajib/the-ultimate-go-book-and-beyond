## 90.4 Building Influence

### Influence Without Authority

At staff level, the most important work often happens across teams that do not report to you, which means you cannot rely on positional authority to get things done. Instead, you must build influence through a combination of demonstrated competence, consistent reliability, and genuine investment in other people's success. The framework below outlines five pillars of influence that, when practiced consistently over months, make you someone whose opinions carry weight in technical discussions and whose proposals gain traction across the organization.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│             BUILDING ORGANIZATIONAL INFLUENCE                    │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  1. DELIVER RESULTS                                              │
│     ├── Nothing builds credibility like shipping                 │
│     ├── Pick high-visibility, high-impact work                   │
│     └── Make your success measurable                             │
│                                                                  │
│  2. BUILD RELATIONSHIPS                                          │
│     ├── Regular 1:1s with stakeholders                           │
│     ├── Help others succeed                                      │
│     ├── Be genuinely interested in their challenges              │
│     └── Build trust before you need it                           │
│                                                                  │
│  3. COMMUNICATE EFFECTIVELY                                      │
│     ├── Write well (RFCs, docs, updates)                         │
│     ├── Present confidently                                      │
│     ├── Listen actively                                          │
│     └── Adjust style for audience                                │
│                                                                  │
│  4. BE CONSISTENT                                                │
│     ├── Follow through on commitments                            │
│     ├── Show up reliably                                         │
│     ├── Have consistent positions (don't flip-flop)              │
│     └── Admit when you're wrong                                  │
│                                                                  │
│  5. ADD VALUE TO EVERY INTERACTION                               │
│     ├── Bring insights, not just questions                       │
│     ├── Solve problems, don't just raise them                    │
│     ├── Be the person who unblocks others                        │
│     └── Be generous with credit                                  │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Stakeholder Management

Not all stakeholders require the same level of attention, and trying to engage equally with everyone is a fast path to burnout. A stakeholder map helps you categorize the people around you based on their influence over your work and their interest in it, so you can invest your limited relationship-building time where it matters most. The categories below give you a practical framework for deciding how often to meet with different people and what level of detail to share with them.

```
STAKEHOLDER CATEGORIES:

HIGH INFLUENCE, HIGH INTEREST:
"Key Players" - Engage closely
├── Your manager's manager
├── Key product partners
├── Platform/infra leads you depend on
Strategy: Regular 1:1s, involve in decisions

HIGH INFLUENCE, LOW INTEREST:
"Keep Satisfied" - Keep informed
├── Executive leadership
├── Adjacent team leads
├── Security/compliance teams
Strategy: Periodic updates, involve when relevant

LOW INFLUENCE, HIGH INTEREST:
"Keep Informed" - Regular updates
├── Junior engineers on your initiatives
├── Interested parties in other teams
├── Technical program managers
Strategy: Open communication, invite to reviews

LOW INFLUENCE, LOW INTEREST:
"Monitor" - Minimal effort
├── Teams not affected by your work
├── Stakeholders who've delegated
Strategy: General announcements only
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building Influence Before You Have the Title

Influence at staff level is not a switch that flips when you get promoted. It is a reputation built over years of being right about important things, following through on commitments, and helping other engineers solve hard problems without stealing credit. The engineers who reach staff fastest are not the ones who lobbied for the title. They are the ones who were already operating at staff scope before anyone had given them the label.

The most accessible influence-building tactic for junior and mid-level engineers is to become the person your team turns to for a specific type of problem. It does not have to be the most glamorous problem. Being the person who truly understands the deployment pipeline, or the test reliability infrastructure, or the internal observability tooling, gives you a node in the organization's informal problem-solving network. From there, influence grows outward.

Interview entry point: when asked "how do you influence without authority," do not answer abstractly. Name a specific situation where you convinced a skeptical senior engineer to change an approach, what arguments you used, how you handled pushback, and what the outcome was. Concrete stories with real friction are what FAANG interviewers remember.

### Senior @ FAANG: Influence as Organizational Infrastructure

At FAANG, staff engineer influence is most durable when it operates through documentation and standards rather than through personal presence. An engineer who influences through charisma and relationships is a single point of failure: when they leave, the standards they maintained leave with them. An engineer who influences through well-written ADRs, documented design patterns, and codified review processes creates infrastructure that outlasts their tenure.

The most effective staff engineers build what might be called influence multipliers: artifacts and processes that cause their reasoning to propagate through the org without their direct involvement. A well-written Go style guide that new engineers read during onboarding is an influence multiplier. A Slack channel where architectural questions get answered publicly and accumulate searchable history is an influence multiplier. A template for pre-mortems that teams adopt before launching risky changes is an influence multiplier.

Managing the "up" influence channel is a skill most engineers under-invest in until it is too late. Influencing engineering directors and VPs requires a different communication style than influencing peers: less detail, more framing, a clear statement of what decision you need from them, and a concrete recommendation. Engineers who send their VPs the same detailed technical documents they send their peers are losing 80% of the communication because the recipient cannot locate the signal in the noise.

Cross-team influence at FAANG requires understanding that every team has competing priorities and that "this is the right thing to do" is rarely sufficient to win alignment. The effective pattern is to identify what the other team cares most about, show how your proposal advances that goal, and minimize the cost they need to absorb. The staff engineer who has done the work to understand another team's OKRs before asking for their support will win alignment that the engineer who leads with technical correctness will not.

Principal engineers extend influence to the external technical community through conference talks, open-source contributions, and published technical writing. That external reputation creates an inbound channel: engineers who have read your work want to join your org, which compounds internal influence by improving the talent pool around you.
