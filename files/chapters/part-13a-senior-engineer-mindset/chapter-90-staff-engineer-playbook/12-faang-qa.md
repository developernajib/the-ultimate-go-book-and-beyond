## FAANG Interview Q&A: Staff Engineer Playbook

These questions come up both in promotion committees and in external Staff IC loops. They assess whether you operate with Staff-level scope, agency, and judgment.

### 1. What are you working on right now that no one assigned to you?

Staff engineers do not wait for tickets. Identify a current initiative you surfaced, the case you made, the coalition you built, and the measurable outcome you are tracking. Weak answers describe assigned work with a creative framing. Strong answers describe a problem that would not be getting solved if you had not picked it up.

### 2. Tell me about your first 90 days at Staff level.

Expected elements: listening tour with stakeholders, identifying the 2-3 single most useful problems the organization has not yet named, shipping one small visible win in the first 30 days to establish credibility, and committing to one multi-quarter initiative by day 60. Avoid describing the first 90 days as "I kept doing my senior-engineer work but with more scope."

### 3. Describe a time you had to choose between technical excellence and organizational velocity.

Staff engineers make this trade explicitly, not by accident. Identify the specific technical compromise, the organizational constraint that forced it, and the guardrails you put in place so the compromise did not compound. Stripe and Meta interviewers look for evidence that you revisit the trade-off on a scheduled cadence rather than filing it and forgetting.

### 4. How do you allocate your time across coding, reviewing, writing, and meetings?

A calibrated answer names rough percentages (for Staff IC roles at FAANG, coding is typically 30-50%, reviews and writing 30%, meetings and strategy 20-30%) and how those shift with the phase of a project. Explain the signal that tells you your allocation has drifted: if you have not written any production code in a month, you are drifting toward management-track work without the title.

### 5. Design your approach to a technical initiative that will take 4 quarters and span 6 teams.

Cover: the RFC that establishes shared direction, the milestone structure with quarterly go/no-go gates, the embedded engineering lead on each affected team, the metric dashboard that surfaces drift early, and the escalation path when a team falls behind. Discuss how you would handle the quarter where one team's priorities shift and they want to withdraw from the initiative. Reference the Amazon six-pager format or the Google design-review cadence where relevant.

### Junior → FAANG: Using These Questions as a Practice Curriculum

These five questions are not trivia. They are the mental models that distinguish staff-level engineers from senior-level engineers. You do not have to be staff to practice answering them. Find a quiet hour each week and write a one-page answer to one of these questions, grounded in real situations from your current work. Over six months, you will have a library of practiced answers and, more importantly, a set of mental models that you have stress-tested against reality.

The question about measuring engineering quality is particularly important to practice now. Most engineers have an intuitive sense of quality, but cannot articulate measurable criteria. "The code is clean" is not a measurable criterion. "Build time under 3 minutes, test coverage above 80% on business logic, P99 latency under 50ms, and zero unresolved Sev2 incidents older than 48 hours" is measurable. The habit of translating intuitions into measurable criteria is core to staff-level engineering and critical to FAANG interview success.

### Senior @ FAANG: What Bar-Raisers Are Actually Listening For

FAANG bar-raisers at the staff level are not evaluating whether you know the right answer to these questions. They are evaluating the quality of your reasoning process under pressure. A candidate who arrives at a slightly suboptimal answer through rigorous, transparent reasoning is preferred over a candidate who arrives at the optimal answer without explaining how they got there.

The "design your approach to a four-quarter initiative" question is the highest-signal question on this list. Strong answers share three characteristics: they anticipate failure modes before being asked about them, they identify the governance structure (who has decision authority, who is consulted, who is informed) without prompting, and they discuss what would cause them to recommend canceling the initiative rather than continuing to invest. That last point is the bar-raiser signal: engineers who cannot name the conditions under which a project should be killed are engineers who will not recommend killing it when those conditions arrive in production.

The honest self-assessment question is a trap for engineers who treat humility as a performance. The bar-raiser is not looking for false modesty or a carefully crafted weakness that is secretly a strength. They are looking for evidence of genuine self-awareness: a real limitation, a specific situation where it caused a problem, and a concrete change you made as a result. Engineers who have done the actual work of self-reflection produce these stories naturally. Engineers who have prepared a packaged answer produce stories that sound rehearsed and do not survive follow-up questions.
