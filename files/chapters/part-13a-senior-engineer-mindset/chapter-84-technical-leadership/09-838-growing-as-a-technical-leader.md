## 84.8 Growing as a Technical Leader

### Self-Assessment

Growth as a technical leader requires deliberate self-reflection. Without regular assessment, it is easy to default to what feels comfortable (usually writing code) and neglect the leadership activities that actually increase your impact. The quarterly self-review below covers four dimensions: technical impact, leadership impact, communication, and areas for growth. Set a calendar reminder to complete this assessment every quarter and share the results with your manager or a trusted peer.

```
QUARTERLY SELF-REVIEW:

TECHNICAL IMPACT
□ What technical problems did I solve?
□ What systems did I improve?
□ What technical debt did I address?
□ What did I learn technically?

LEADERSHIP IMPACT
□ What decisions did I influence?
□ Who did I help grow?
□ What initiatives did I lead?
□ How did I improve team processes?

COMMUNICATION
□ How clearly did I communicate decisions?
□ How well did I handle disagreements?
□ What written artifacts did I produce?
□ How visible was my work?

AREAS FOR GROWTH
□ What feedback did I receive?
□ Where did I struggle?
□ What would I do differently?
□ What skills do I need to develop?
```

### Building Your Leadership Brand

Your technical brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room. It determines which projects you get pulled into, whose opinion you are asked for, and ultimately how your impact is perceived at promotion time. Building a brand requires intentional investment in both depth (becoming the go-to person for a specific area) and breadth (being known across the organization). Critically, you also need to make your work visible, great work that nobody knows about does not help your career or your team.

```
YOUR TECHNICAL BRAND = What you're known for

DEVELOP DEPTH:
- Become the go-to person for something
- Write about it, speak about it
- Help others in that area

DEVELOP BREADTH:
- Take on cross-functional projects
- Learn adjacent domains
- Build relationships across org

MAKE YOUR WORK VISIBLE:
- Write design docs and RFCs
- Present at tech talks
- Publish blog posts or talks
- Contribute to open source

BUILD RELATIONSHIPS:
- 1:1s with people across the org
- Help others without expecting return
- Be reliable and trustworthy
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building a Deliberate Growth Practice

Most engineers who plateau at mid-level do so because their growth was opportunistic rather than deliberate. They took on whatever came next rather than identifying the specific leadership capabilities they were missing and creating conditions to practice them.

Before your FAANG interview, do a genuine self-assessment against the growth dimensions in this section. Where are you strong? Where are you genuinely weak? Then look at your recent experience and ask: have I been building toward those weaknesses, or avoiding them? The engineers who grow fastest are the ones who actively seek out the uncomfortable assignment, the cross-team coordination role, the design review they feel under-qualified to lead.

In the interview itself, growth self-awareness is a positive signal. "Here is what I am working on to get better" is a more compelling answer than "here is how good I already am." FAANG calibration is done against bar, not against self-assessment, so interviewers are partly looking for the ability to accurately locate yourself on the capability spectrum.

### Senior @ FAANG: Designing Growth Systems for Your Org

At staff and principal levels, your own growth is one input. The growth of the engineers around you is a primary output. The section's list of growth activities (seeking feedback, stretching assignments, teaching, writing) should be things you are actively designing into the team's environment, not just practicing personally.

One governance lever that staff engineers consistently underuse: the growth conversation. Not the performance review, which is backward-looking and high-stakes. The growth conversation is forward-looking, lower-stakes, and more useful: "What is the most important capability you want to build in the next six months? What are the two or three concrete opportunities in our current roadmap where you can practice it? How will you know you are improving?" Running this conversation for every engineer on your team, and documenting the answers, creates a growth system rather than ad-hoc mentoring.

The bar-raiser signal at L7 is not just that you have grown as a leader. It is that engineers who worked with you grew measurably faster than they would have otherwise. Be prepared to name specific engineers, the specific capabilities they developed, and the specific environment or conditions you created that made that growth possible.
