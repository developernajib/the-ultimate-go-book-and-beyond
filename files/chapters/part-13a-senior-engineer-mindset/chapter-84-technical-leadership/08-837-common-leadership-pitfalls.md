## 84.7 Common Leadership Pitfalls

### Anti-Patterns

Even well-intentioned engineers can fall into leadership anti-patterns that undermine their effectiveness. These patterns are common because each one starts from a strength taken too far: the Lone Wolf is a capable engineer who forgot to delegate, the Perfectionist is a quality-minded engineer who lost sight of delivery timelines. Recognizing these patterns in yourself is the first step to correcting them. Review this list honestly and ask yourself which ones you tend toward.

```

│              TECHNICAL LEADERSHIP ANTI-PATTERNS                  │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  THE LONE WOLF                                                   │
│  Problem: Does everything themselves, doesn't delegate           │
│  Impact: Bottleneck, team doesn't grow, burnout                  │
│  Fix: Identify work others can own, let go of control            │
│                                                                  │
│  THE IVORY TOWER                                                 │
│  Problem: Designs without understanding constraints              │
│  Impact: Impractical solutions, frustrated teams                 │
│  Fix: Stay close to implementation, pair with ICs                │
│                                                                  │
│  THE PEOPLE PLEASER                                              │
│  Problem: Avoids conflict, agrees with everyone                  │
│  Impact: No decisions made, inconsistent direction               │

│                                                                  │
│  THE BULLDOZER                                                   │
│  Problem: Pushes ideas without listening                         │
│  Impact: Alienates team, misses important input                  │
```

### Junior → FAANG: Recognizing Pitfalls Before They Become Patterns

Most junior engineers who fail to advance to senior do so not because of technical deficiencies but because of one or two persistent behavioral anti-patterns that go unexamined. The most common: hero mode (solving everything themselves instead of coordinating), over-communication (flooding channels with updates that nobody needs), or under-communication (assuming silence means alignment).

For FAANG interviews, being able to identify and name a leadership anti-pattern you fell into and what you did to correct it is a strong signal. It demonstrates self-awareness, which is a prerequisite for growth at every level above L4. The story structure: "I noticed I was doing X, I realized it was causing Y consequence, I changed my approach to Z, and the result was W."

Avoid the trap of choosing a fake weakness: "I care too much" or "I work too hard." Choose a real pattern you genuinely struggled with and describe the actual mechanism by which you corrected it. That specificity is what distinguishes high-signal answers from rehearsed ones.

### Senior @ FAANG: Building Organizational Defenses Against Pitfalls

At staff and principal levels, you have typically experienced all of the pitfalls listed in this section and have enough perspective to recognize them in others. The question is not whether you personally avoid them. It is whether you have established organizational defenses that make them less likely to recur in your team or org.

The "hero engineer" pitfall is particularly important at scale. Organizations develop structural dependencies on hero behavior when they fail to build reliable systems, adequate documentation, and cross-trained ownership. The staff engineer who identifies this pattern and advocates for a runbook requirement, an on-call rotation, and documented architecture rather than just being the hero themselves is creating lasting organizational health.

The "ghost leader" pitfall (making decisions without visibility or accountability) is a governance failure that senior engineers are uniquely positioned to address. If important technical decisions are being made in Slack threads with no documentation, the org is accumulating institutional debt. Introducing a lightweight ADR process, even informally at first, converts invisible decisions into visible ones and creates the audit trail that makes governance possible.

In bar-raiser interviews, be prepared to describe not just your personal avoidance of these pitfalls but the structural changes you made to reduce their occurrence org-wide. That is the scope difference between L5 and L6 answers on leadership maturity.
