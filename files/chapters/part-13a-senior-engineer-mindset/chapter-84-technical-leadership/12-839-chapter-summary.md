## 84.11 Core Concepts Recap

### Key Takeaways

1. **Leadership is influence, not authority** - Build it through expertise, relationships, and communication
2. **Frame decisions carefully** - Distinguish reversible from irreversible
3. **Build consensus strategically** - Know when to inform vs consult vs collaborate
4. **Mentor through coaching** - Develop others rather than doing it yourself
5. **Lead initiatives, not just tasks** - Own outcomes across the lifecycle
6. **Avoid common pitfalls** - Lone wolf, ivory tower, perfectionist

### Leadership Checklist

- [ ] Building expertise in a domain
- [ ] Writing design docs and RFCs
- [ ] Mentoring at least one engineer
- [ ] Leading a cross-team initiative
- [ ] Making and documenting decisions
- [ ] Building relationships across teams
- [ ] Giving and receiving feedback effectively
- [ ] Making your work visible

### Core Practice Exercises

### Exercise 1: Write an RFC
Choose a technical improvement you've been thinking about. Write a complete RFC including problem statement, proposed solution, alternatives considered, and trade-offs.

### Exercise 2: Map Your Influence
Draw a map of people you need to influence to succeed in your current role. Identify where your influence is strong and where you need to build it.

### Exercise 3: Mentoring Practice
Identify one engineer you could mentor. Schedule an initial conversation using the GROW model and create a mentoring plan.

### Junior → FAANG: Using Chapter Summaries as Interview Checklists

A chapter summary is not a replacement for reading the chapter, but it is a useful pre-interview audit tool. Go through each key takeaway and ask yourself: can I give a concrete, specific example from my own experience that demonstrates this? If yes, add it to your behavioral story bank. If no, identify the gap and try to create an opportunity to fill it before your interview date.

The most common gap for L4/L5 candidates is around cross-team influence. Most junior engineers have strong examples within their own team but weak examples of driving decisions or change beyond it. This is the gap to prioritize closing.

### Senior @ FAANG: Using Chapter Summaries as Org Health Audits

At staff and principal level, re-reading a chapter summary becomes an opportunity to audit your organization rather than yourself. For each takeaway, ask: does my team have a system or practice that embodies this? If not, what is the cost of that gap and is it worth addressing? This converts passive reading into actionable organizational improvement.

The summaries are also useful for onboarding new senior engineers. A staff engineer who asks a new L6 hire "which of these takeaways would you say is a gap for our team right now?" gets an immediate high-quality signal about how that engineer thinks about organizational health and where they might contribute most during onboarding.
