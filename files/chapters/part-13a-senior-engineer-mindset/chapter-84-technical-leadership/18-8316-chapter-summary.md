## Summary

### Key Takeaways

1. **Leadership is influence, not authority** - Build it through expertise, relationships, and communication
2. **Frame decisions carefully** - Distinguish reversible from irreversible
3. **Build consensus strategically** - Know when to inform vs consult vs collaborate
4. **Mentor through coaching** - Develop others rather than doing it yourself
5. **Lead initiatives, not just tasks** - Own outcomes across the lifecycle
6. **Avoid common pitfalls** - Lone wolf, ivory tower, perfectionist
7. **Make your work visible** - Document and communicate leadership impact
8. **Work cross-functionally** - Build relationships beyond engineering

### Leadership Checklist

- [ ] Building expertise in a domain
- [ ] Writing design docs and RFCs
- [ ] Mentoring at least one engineer
- [ ] Leading a cross-team initiative
- [ ] Making and documenting decisions
- [ ] Building relationships across teams
- [ ] Giving and receiving feedback effectively
- [ ] Making your work visible
- [ ] Running or participating in architecture reviews
- [ ] Influencing without direct authority

### Junior → FAANG: Consolidating Technical Leadership Foundations

This chapter has given you the foundational framework for technical leadership at every level. Before moving to the next chapter, do a personal inventory: of the eight core sections covered, which two feel most natural to you and which two feel most underdeveloped? The two underdeveloped areas are your interview preparation priorities.

Technical leadership is cumulative. The skills in this chapter compound with the system thinking, RFC writing, and career strategy skills in subsequent chapters. An engineer who can lead without authority AND write clear design documents AND think in feedback loops is operating at a fundamentally different level than one who has mastered any single skill in isolation.

### Senior @ FAANG: From Chapter to Operating Model

At staff and principal level, a chapter summary is the beginning of an operating model, not the end of a learning exercise. Take the key skills from this chapter and evaluate them as organizational capabilities: which of these does your team have strong institutional support for, and which depend entirely on individuals? The answer tells you where to invest your organizational design energy in the next quarter.

The most valuable investment for most senior engineers is the one that creates the most durable organizational capability per unit of effort. Usually that is documentation and process design: write the RFC template, define the decision record standard, create the mentoring framework. These artifacts outlast individual tenure and scale with the organization in ways that personal mentoring and direct influence cannot.
