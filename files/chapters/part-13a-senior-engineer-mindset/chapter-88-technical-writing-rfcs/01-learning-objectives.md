## Learning Objectives

This chapter covers the full spectrum of technical writing skills that senior engineers use daily. Whether you are writing your first RFC or refining your approach to design documents, these objectives will guide your learning and give you concrete skills to practice immediately.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
- Write clear, effective technical documents
- Create RFCs that drive decisions and build consensus
- Structure design documents for different audiences
- Navigate the RFC process from proposal to approval
- Write documentation that people actually read
- Use technical writing as a leadership tool

### Junior → FAANG: Each Objective as an Interview Question Category

"Write clear, effective technical documents" maps to: "Show me a design doc you wrote." Every FAANG loop for L5 and above expects you to have written at least one design doc that you can describe in detail. If you do not have one, write one before you apply.

"Navigate the RFC process from proposal to approval" maps to: "Tell me about a time you drove alignment on a controversial technical decision." The word "navigate" is key: the question is not just "did you write an RFC" but "did you understand how to move it through the organization, manage the feedback, and close it?"

"Use technical writing as a leadership tool" is the most senior objective in this list and the one most candidates skip. Before your interview, identify one case where a document you wrote changed how people thought about a problem, not just documented a decision. That story, with the influence mechanism explained explicitly, is L5-to-L6 interview material.

### Senior @ FAANG: These Objectives as an Engineering Culture Scorecard

At L6 and above, these objectives describe the writing culture you should be building in your team, not just your personal skills.

"Create RFCs that drive decisions and build consensus" at team scale means your team has a working RFC process: a template, a review period convention, a definition of what requires an RFC versus what can be decided in a meeting, and a searchable archive of past RFCs that engineers can reference. Organizations that have this infrastructure make better decisions faster than organizations that treat every technical debate as a fresh argument.

"Write documentation that people actually read" is an objective that requires actively measuring documentation consumption, not just producing documentation. Staff engineers who track page views, search queries, and time-to-onboard for new engineers can demonstrate that their documentation investment is producing measurable returns. Documentation that no one reads is a liability, not an asset: it creates a false sense of coverage while consuming maintenance time.
