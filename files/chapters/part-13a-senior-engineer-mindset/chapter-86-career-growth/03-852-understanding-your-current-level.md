## 86.2 Understanding Your Current Level

### Self-Assessment Framework

Before you can plan your career growth, you need an honest picture of where you stand today. This self-assessment checklist covers the four key dimensions that companies evaluate: technical skills, impact, leadership, and communication. Go through each question honestly. The areas where you check the fewest boxes are likely your biggest growth opportunities, and the ones you should prioritize in your development plan.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                  LEVEL SELF-ASSESSMENT                           │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  TECHNICAL SKILLS                                                │
│  □ Can I solve any problem in my domain?                         │
│  □ Am I the go-to person for something?                          │
│  □ Do I understand systems beyond my immediate work?             │
│  □ Can I design complex systems independently?                   │
│  □ Do I influence technical standards?                           │
│                                                                  │
│  IMPACT                                                          │
│  □ What was my biggest impact in the last year?                  │
│  □ Who benefited from my work? (team/org/company)                │
│  □ Did I enable others to be more effective?                     │
│  □ Did I solve problems that weren't assigned to me?             │
│  □ Did I prevent problems before they happened?                  │
│                                                                  │
│  LEADERSHIP                                                      │
│  □ Do people seek my opinion on technical decisions?             │
│  □ Have I mentored others successfully?                          │
│  □ Have I led cross-team initiatives?                            │
│  □ Can I influence without authority?                            │
│  □ Do I create clarity in ambiguous situations?                  │
│                                                                  │
│  COMMUNICATION                                                   │
│  □ Are my design docs clear and thorough?                        │
│  □ Can I explain complex topics to non-technical people?         │
│  □ Do I communicate proactively and effectively?                 │
│  □ Am I effective in meetings?                                   │
│  □ Do stakeholders trust my estimates and plans?                 │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Gap Analysis

Once you have completed the self-assessment, the next step is to compare where you are now with where your target level expects you to be. A gap analysis makes this concrete by listing each dimension, describing your current state, defining the target state, and outlining a specific plan to close the gap. This is not a one-time exercise. Revisit it quarterly with your manager so you can track progress and adjust your plan as you grow.

```
CURRENT: Senior Engineer
TARGET: Staff Engineer

GAPS IDENTIFIED:

1. SCOPE
   Current: Own features within team
   Target: Own systems across teams
   Gap: Need cross-team project experience
   Plan: Volunteer for cross-team initiatives

2. AMBIGUITY
   Current: Solve well-defined problems
   Target: Identify and define problems
   Gap: Need to be more proactive
   Plan: Start proposing solutions, not just finding issues

3. INFLUENCE
   Current: Influence within team
   Target: Influence across org
   Gap: Limited visibility outside team
   Plan: Write blog posts, present at tech talks

4. MENTORING
   Current: Help juniors with code
   Target: Develop senior engineers
   Gap: Haven't mentored seniors
   Plan: Take on senior mentee, focus on leadership skills
```

### Junior → FAANG: Reading the Level Map Before You Apply

The self-assessment framework in this section directly mirrors how FAANG recruiters and hiring managers calibrate candidates before an offer. If you cannot articulate concrete examples for "cross-team impact" and "influence without authority," you will be leveled lower regardless of your technical performance in the coding rounds.

Before your interview, complete the gap analysis for yourself honestly. If the "cross-team impact" row is empty, that is the story gap that will cost you an L5 offer. Create that experience before you apply: volunteer to write the RFC no one else wants to write, pair with an engineer from another team on a shared problem, or run a design review that spans two services.

The self-assessment also matters for comp negotiation. Candidates who can clearly articulate why they belong at the top of an offer band, citing specific scope and impact examples, consistently land better initial offers.

### Senior @ FAANG: Using Self-Assessment as a Promotion Readiness Signal

At L6 and above, the self-assessment framework shifts from personal development tool to organizational infrastructure. Staff engineers are expected to help others use it, not just complete it themselves.

A bar-raising senior engineer does not just know their own gaps. They have a working mental model of the gap distribution across their team: which engineers are close to the next level, which are blocked and why, and which ones need different opportunities rather than more time. This systemic view of level readiness is what distinguishes a L6 from a L5 who reads the same self-assessment questions.

At the L7 bar, interviewers ask: "Tell me about someone you helped get promoted." The expected answer is not "I mentored them in code reviews." It is: "I identified that their gap was cross-team scope, so I deliberately pulled them into my initiative with ownership of a specific milestone. I structured the work so that their contribution was visible to the broader org, and I coached them on how to document it in their promo packet." That is what self-assessment at staff level actually looks like, applied to the people around you.

The gap analysis template also becomes a governance artifact at senior levels. Running a quarterly calibration session where your team's L5 engineers self-assess against the L6 rubric, and then comparing self-ratings to manager and skip-level ratings, surfaces systematic disagreements in level expectations before they become attrition risks.
