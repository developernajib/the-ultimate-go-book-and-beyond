## 86.3 Building Staff-Level Skills

### The Staff Engineer Skillset

Becoming a Staff Engineer requires developing skills that most Senior Engineers have never been explicitly taught. While technical depth remains important, the role demands an entirely new set of capabilities around breadth, strategy, organizational navigation, and communication. Think of it as shifting from "best player on the team" to "coach who also plays." The diagram below maps out the five skill categories that define Staff-level effectiveness.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 STAFF ENGINEER SKILLS                            │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  TECHNICAL DEPTH (Still important!)                              │
│  ├── Expert in your domain                                       │
│  ├── Understanding of adjacent domains                           │
│  ├── Can make complex trade-offs                                 │
│  └── Stay current with technology                                │
│                                                                  │
│  TECHNICAL BREADTH (More important at staff+)                    │
│  ├── Understanding of entire system                              │
│  ├── Can evaluate new technologies                               │

│  STRATEGIC THINKING                                              │
```

### Growth Activities

Knowing which skills to build is only half the battle. You also need concrete activities that develop those skills in your day-to-day work. The checklist below organizes growth activities by category so you can pick a few from each area and integrate them into your quarterly goals. You do not need to do all of these at once. Start with one or two per category and increase over time as they become habits.

```
TECHNICAL DEPTH:
□ Read source code of technologies you use
□ Contribute to open source projects
□ Build side projects exploring new areas
□ Attend/speak at conferences
□ Read papers and books in your domain

TECHNICAL BREADTH:
□ Shadow other teams (observability, security, infra)
□ Join incident responses across the org
□ Participate in architecture reviews
□ Study adjacent domains (databases, networking)
□ Learn a new programming paradigm

STRATEGIC THINKING:
□ Understand company business model
□ Attend product planning meetings
□ Read company strategy documents
□ Propose technical investments
□ Map technology to business outcomes

ORGANIZATIONAL SKILLS:
□ Lead a cross-team project
□ Write and socialize an RFC
□ Mentor an engineer in another team
□ Facilitate a technical decision process
□ Build relationships across the org

COMMUNICATION:
□ Write internal blog posts
□ Give tech talks
□ Document systems you work with
□ Lead meetings and retrospectives
□ Present to non-technical stakeholders
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building the Foundation That Makes Staff Skills Possible

The five skill categories in this section reveal why FAANG L4 and L5 interviews include behavioral questions that feel unrelated to technical ability. "Tell me about a time you influenced a decision you didn't own" is testing the organizational skills dimension. "Describe a time you identified a problem before it became critical" is testing strategic thinking.

At first-job and mid-level, the growth activities list should be treated as a deliberate practice queue, not an aspiration. Pick one item per category and execute it over a quarter. Presenting at a team meeting, writing one internal blog post, and participating in a single architecture review creates three concrete examples for behavioral interviews that most L4 candidates cannot produce.

Strategic thinking is the most neglected skill at this level because it feels like it belongs to "someone else's job." But every FAANG interview for L5 includes at least one question that probes strategic awareness: "How does this system fit into the broader product strategy?" Candidates who can answer this question, even roughly, differentiate themselves from candidates who only know their own service.

### Senior @ FAANG: Designing Skill-Building Infrastructure for Your Team

At L6 and above, the staff engineer skillset is not just a personal development goal. It becomes something you systematically build into your team's growth structure.

The most effective staff engineers create deliberate skill-building pathways for the engineers around them. This means constructing projects where a mid-level engineer can practice strategic thinking, not just execution. It means deliberately pulling L5 engineers into architecture reviews so they develop breadth, even when it would be faster to run the review yourself. It means creating "technical breadth tours" where engineers shadow incidents outside their domain.

At the L7 and L8 bar, interviewers evaluate whether you have shaped a team where staff-level skill development is systematic rather than accidental. Questions like "How has your team gotten better as a whole over the past year?" probe this. The expected answer names specific mechanisms: the RFC process you introduced, the architecture review rotation you set up, the internal talk series you launched. These are org-level artifacts of a staff engineer's skill-building work, not individual achievements.

Maturity models for engineering organizations explicitly track whether staff-level skills are concentrated in a few individuals or distributed broadly. A healthy team at L7 scope has multiple engineers who can operate at staff level when needed, not just one. If you are the only person who can run a design review or write a credible technical strategy document, you are a single point of failure, not a staff engineer.
