## 86.15 Interview Questions: Career Growth

### Question 1: "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"

**What They're Really Asking**: Do you have direction? Are you thinking strategically about your career? Will you grow with us?

**Framework for Answering**:

```markdown
# Answer Structure

## 1. Show thoughtful direction (not a rigid plan)
"I've thought about this a lot, and while I know plans evolve..."

## 2. Describe growth trajectory, not specific titles
"I see myself taking on increasing scope and impact..."

## 3. Connect to skills and contributions
"I want to develop expertise in X and be known for Y..."

## 4. Tie to the company/role
"This role excites me because it would help me..."

## 5. Show flexibility
"I'm open to how this evolves based on opportunities..."
```

**Sample Answer**:
> "I've been thinking about this as I've progressed from mid-level to senior. In the next five years, I want to move from owning individual features to owning entire technical domains. I'm particularly interested in developing deeper expertise in distributed systems and being someone teams come to for architectural guidance.
>
> I see my path as continuing on the IC track toward Staff-level work - taking on problems with more ambiguity, having cross-team impact, and mentoring other engineers. This role excites me because the scale of your systems would give me exactly those growth opportunities.
>
> I'm also open to how this evolves. I've seen that sometimes the best opportunities come from directions you didn't expect. What's most important to me is continuing to grow technically and increasing my impact."

### Question 2: "Tell me about a time you developed or mentored someone."

**What They're Really Asking**: Can you grow others? Do you invest in team success? Are you ready for senior-level expectations?

**Sample Answer (STAR Format)**:
> "When I was at my previous company, a junior engineer, Maya, joined my team. She had strong fundamentals but struggled with our complex codebase and was getting discouraged by code review feedback.
>
> I volunteered to be her primary mentor. Instead of just reviewing her code, I started pair programming with her on features, explaining my thought process as we worked. I created a 'system overview' document that didn't exist before, which helped her and subsequent new hires.
>
> I also changed how I gave feedback. Instead of just pointing out issues, I'd explain the 'why' and link to relevant documentation. We had weekly 1:1s where I'd ask about her challenges and help her prioritize learning.
>
> Within four months, Maya was contributing independently. Her code review approval rate went from about 30% to over 90%. She later told me she'd been close to leaving but the mentorship changed her trajectory. She was promoted to mid-level within a year, which was actually faster than typical.
>
> This experience taught me that investing in others multiplies your impact. Maya later helped onboard two other engineers using the patterns we'd established."

### Question 3: "Why do you want to be a Staff/Senior/Principal engineer?"

**What They're Really Asking**: Are your motivations aligned with what the level requires? Do you understand what it actually involves?

**Sample Answer**:
> "I want to move to Staff level because I've realized my greatest impact comes from solving problems that span teams, not just implementing features. At my current level, I'm often constrained to my team's scope even when I can see opportunities that cross boundaries.
>
> I'm drawn to the ambiguity that comes with Staff-level work. I enjoy taking a vague problem like 'our systems don't scale well' and turning it into a concrete plan that multiple teams can execute on. I've done this successfully a few times - the most recent was a cross-service caching initiative - and I want to do more of it.
>
> I'm also realistic that Staff means less coding and more influence work. I've tested this by leading an architecture working group, and I found I enjoyed the discussions and decision-making even more than I expected.
>
> Most importantly, I want to have the positional authority to drive changes I believe in. I have opinions about how we should build systems, and at Staff level I'd have a stronger platform to advocate for those approaches and help shape our technical direction."

### Question 4: "How do you decide when to stay vs. leave a company?"

**What They're Really Asking**: Are you thoughtful about career decisions? What matters to you? (Also possibly: Will you leave us quickly?)

**Sample Answer**:
> "I think about this in terms of learning, impact, and relationships.
>
> Learning is my primary driver early in roles. Am I developing new skills? Am I being challenged? If I've stopped growing and the company can't offer new challenges, that's a signal to explore.
>
> Impact matters more as I've become more senior. Can I have meaningful impact here? Are there problems worth solving? Sometimes companies grow past what I can influence, or contract to where there aren't interesting problems left.
>
> Relationships also matter. Working with people I respect and who push me to be better is valuable. Toxic environments or values misalignment are reasons to leave even if other factors are good.
>
> What I try to avoid is leaving prematurely - jumping ship for a small compensation bump or before I've really delivered impact. I aim for 2-4 years at each company, long enough to learn deeply and deliver substantial work, but not so long that I'm staying out of inertia.
>
> For this role specifically, I'm excited because the problems align with where I want to grow, and the company stage means I could have meaningful impact."

### Junior → FAANG: Practicing These Answers Until They Feel Natural

The four questions in this section appear in almost every FAANG behavioral loop, and they appear because they have high signal-to-noise ratio for level calibration. "Where do you see yourself in five years?" at a FAANG company is not small talk. It is a scope probe. The expected answer at L5 describes operating at Staff level. The expected answer at L4 describes operating at Senior level. Candidates who answer with role ambiguity, "maybe management, maybe not," are harder to level and often get passed over.

The mentorship answer is particularly important for L5 targeting. If your best mentorship story is "I answered questions in Slack," you are presenting L3 evidence. Upgrade it to show changed trajectory: "I restructured how Maya approached code reviews, which reduced her revision cycles from four to one on average, and she was promoted six months later." Outcome stories beat activity stories every time.

Practice these answers out loud with a peer who will give honest feedback. Written preparation does not catch the filler words, the vague scope descriptions, and the missing metrics that live performance reveals.

### Senior @ FAANG: Using These Questions to Calibrate Others

At L6 and above, your relationship to these interview questions flips: you use them to calibrate engineers in promotional discussions and bar-raiser interviews, not to answer them for yourself.

"Why do you want to be a Staff/Senior/Principal engineer?" is one of the highest-signal questions in a staff engineer assessment because the wrong motivations, more control, less coding, the title, predict future performance problems. Candidates who articulate specific technical problems they want to tackle, specific organizational improvements they want to drive, and specific impact they want to have at broader scope are operating from a foundation that sustains performance. Candidates who describe the destination rather than the journey are likely to be disappointed when they discover that the work is harder and less glamorous than the title suggested.

"How do you decide when to stay vs leave?" is a bar-raiser question that surfaces whether a candidate has strategic self-awareness or is reactive to external triggers. Candidates at the senior bar should be able to articulate their decision framework in terms of impact ceiling, learning velocity, and organizational fit, not just compensation and excitement. If a candidate describes leaving primarily because a competing offer arrived, that signals reactive decision-making that does not predict well for the kind of long-term, high-stakes technical investment a staff engineer is expected to make.
