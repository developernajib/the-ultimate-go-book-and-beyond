## 86.5 IC vs Management Decision

### Comparing Paths

The IC and management tracks are genuinely different careers that share a common starting point. Many engineers assume management is "the next step" after senior, but it is actually a lateral move into a different discipline. Both paths can lead to very senior, well-compensated roles. The comparison below highlights how the two tracks differ across the dimensions that matter most for day-to-day satisfaction and long-term career trajectory.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│            IC vs MANAGEMENT COMPARISON                           │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  ASPECT          INDIVIDUAL CONTRIBUTOR     MANAGER              │
│                                                                  │
│  IMPACT          Through technology         Through people       │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  SATISFACTION    Solving hard problems      Growing others       │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  DAILY WORK      Design, code, review       Meetings, 1:1s       │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  SKILLS          Technical + influence      People + org         │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  METRICS         Technical outcomes         Team outcomes        │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  STRESS          Technical complexity       People complexity    │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  CAREER          Staff → Principal          Manager → Director   │
│  CEILING         → Distinguished            → VP                 │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  SWITCHING       Easier to go to mgmt       Harder to go back    │
│  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────   │
│  COMP            Similar at most levels     Slightly higher      │
│                                             at senior levels     │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Questions to Ask Yourself

Choosing between the IC and management tracks is one of the most consequential career decisions you will make, so it deserves careful reflection. The checklist below helps you identify which path aligns with what genuinely energizes you. Pay special attention to the red flags section. If your primary motivation for management matches any of those reasons, the role is likely to disappoint you. The best approach is to test management responsibilities in low-risk ways before committing.

```
CONSIDER IC PATH IF:
□ You love solving technical problems
□ You want to stay close to code
□ You prefer deep work over meetings
□ You're energized by learning new technologies
□ You measure success by technical outcomes
□ You'd rather influence than direct

CONSIDER MANAGEMENT IF:
□ You love growing and developing people
□ You're energized by helping others succeed
□ You're comfortable with ambiguity and conflict
□ You enjoy organizational challenges
□ You measure success by team outcomes
□ You want to shape team culture

RED FLAGS FOR MANAGEMENT:
✗ "I'll have more control"
✗ "It's the only way to advance"
✗ "I'm tired of coding"
✗ "I want more money"
✗ "I don't like my current manager"

TEST IT FIRST:
- Mentor someone formally
- Lead a project with dotted-line reports
- Volunteer for team process improvements
- Ask to shadow your manager for a week
```

### Junior → FAANG: Making the Decision Before the Offer Forces You

FAANG interviewers rarely ask "do you want to be a manager?" at the L4/L5 level, but they do ask questions that reveal your preference indirectly. "Describe a time you helped someone else grow" and "how do you handle disagreements on technical direction" both probe toward the IC-vs-management axis.

The right time to decide is before you are under offer pressure. Accepting a management role because the offer came first, or because it seemed like the only path to senior levels, is the pattern behind most "reluctant manager" situations. Use the questions in this section now, not during a salary negotiation.

If you are uncertain, the lowest-risk test is to take on tech lead responsibilities for a quarter. Run the sprint, own the design reviews, unblock the team. If you miss coding more than you expected, stay IC. If you found the team coordination more satisfying than you expected, management is worth exploring.

### Senior @ FAANG: Managing the IC-vs-Management Distribution on Your Team

At staff and principal level, the IC-vs-management decision stops being only a personal career question and becomes a team composition and succession planning problem.

A healthy engineering organization needs both strong IC leads and strong managers at appropriate ratios. When a team loses its primary Staff IC to management, the technical direction often suffers for 12 to 18 months while a replacement develops. Conversely, when a team has too many ICs chasing the same staff-level scope, political friction emerges around who owns which decision.

Senior engineers who think at the L7 level recognize when they should actively steer talented engineers toward management rather than IC, not because management is better, but because the organization needs a strong manager in that role more than another staff IC. This is an uncomfortable conversation to have with a technically excellent engineer who is set on the IC track. Having it anyway, and making the case well, is a bar-raiser signal.

Succession planning explicitly involves the IC-vs-management decision. If you leave tomorrow, can the technical direction for your area be maintained? If the answer is "my manager would cover it," you have not built an IC succession path. The ADR equivalent here is a written technical succession plan that names two or three engineers who could own specific domains, not just "the team will figure it out."
