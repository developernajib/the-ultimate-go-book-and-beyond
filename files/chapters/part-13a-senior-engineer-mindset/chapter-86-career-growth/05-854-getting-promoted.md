## 86.4 Getting Promoted

### The Promotion Process

Promotions at most technology companies do not happen automatically based on tenure or performance reviews alone. Understanding how the process actually works gives you a significant advantage, because you can align your efforts with what decision-makers are looking for. The following breakdown covers the five realities of how promotions happen in practice, based on patterns observed across Google, Meta, Amazon, and similar organizations.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   PROMOTION REALITIES                            │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  How promotions actually work:                                   │
│                                                                  │
│  1. PROVE IT FIRST                                               │
│     You must demonstrate next-level work before promotion        │
│     Not: "Promote me and I'll perform at that level"             │
│                                                                  │
│  2. NEED A SPONSOR                                               │
│     Someone senior who advocates for you                         │
│     Usually your manager or a senior leader                      │
│                                                                  │
│  3. CALIBRATED WITH PEERS                                        │
│     You're compared to others at target level                    │
│     Consistency of performance matters                           │
│                                                                  │
│  4. VISIBILITY MATTERS                                           │
│     Decision-makers need to know your work                       │
│     Great work that's invisible won't get promoted               │
│                                                                  │
│  5. TIMING AND HEADCOUNT                                         │
│     Some levels have limited slots                               │
│     Business conditions affect promotion rates                   │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Building Your Promotion Case

Your promotion case is the collection of evidence that proves you are already operating at the next level. At most companies, this takes the form of a written document (often called a "promo packet" or "impact summary") that your manager or a promotion committee reviews. The strongest packets tell a clear story connecting your work to measurable business outcomes. Start building this document months before you expect to be considered, updating it as you complete impactful work.

```
THE PROMOTION PACKET (What you should document)

1. IMPACT SUMMARY
   "In the past year, I led the redesign of our payment
   processing system, reducing latency by 70% and saving
   $200K annually in infrastructure costs."

2. SCOPE DEMONSTRATION
   "I owned the complete payments domain (3 services,
   5 team members), while also influencing API standards
   across 4 other teams."

3. KEY ACCOMPLISHMENTS
   - Led migration to new database (300M records, zero downtime)
   - Created observability framework adopted by 6 teams
   - Mentored 2 engineers to promotion
   - Authored 5 RFCs, all approved and implemented

4. CROSS-TEAM IMPACT
   "Collaborated with Security team on audit requirements,
   reducing compliance burden by 40% across all teams."

5. NEXT-LEVEL WORK EVIDENCE
   "Currently operating at Staff level by:
   - Setting technical direction for payments domain
   - Representing engineering in product discussions
   - Driving multi-quarter technical roadmap"

6. PEER FEEDBACK (Collect these)
   "Working with [Name] on Project X, they demonstrated..."
```

### The Promotion Conversation

Having regular, structured career conversations with your manager is one of the most effective things you can do for your growth. Many engineers feel uncomfortable bringing up promotions, but good managers expect and welcome these discussions. The key is to frame the conversation around growth and gaps rather than entitlement. Below is a template for productive career conversations, along with common phrases that will undermine your case.

```
WITH YOUR MANAGER (Quarterly):

1. OPEN THE DISCUSSION
   "I'd like to discuss my path to [target level].
   Can we talk about where I am and what I need to do?"

2. UNDERSTAND EXPECTATIONS
   "What does success at [target level] look like here?
   Who is a good example I could learn from?"

3. IDENTIFY GAPS
   "What are the main gaps between where I am now
   and [target level]?"

4. CREATE A PLAN
   "Can we create a development plan with specific
   milestones? I'd like to check in quarterly."

5. GET FEEDBACK
   "Can you give me specific feedback on where I'm
   doing well and where I need to improve?"

WHAT NOT TO SAY:
✗ "I've been here X years, I deserve a promotion"
✗ "Person Y got promoted, why not me?"
✗ "If I don't get promoted, I'll leave"
✗ "I'm already doing the job, just make it official"
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building Your Promo Packet Before You Need It

The most common mistake junior and mid-level engineers make is waiting until promotion season to start documenting their impact. By then, the strongest examples are months old and the details have faded. FAANG promotion packets are evaluated on specificity: vague impact descriptions consistently lose to concrete ones with numbers.

Start a running document today titled "Impact Log." Add an entry every time you ship something, resolve a significant incident, mentor someone, or influence a technical decision. Record the metrics before and after. Six months of this practice produces a promotion packet that writes itself.

The "prove it first" reality is particularly important to internalize early. First-job engineers who understand this stop asking "when will I get promoted?" and start asking "what would evidence of next-level work actually look like for my specific situation?" That question, asked to a good manager, unlocks a much more useful conversation.

### Senior @ FAANG: Running Promotions as an Org-Level Process

At L6 and above, your relationship to the promotion process inverts. You are no longer primarily building your own packet. You are contributing to the calibration process that determines other engineers' promotions.

Staff engineers at FAANG are regularly asked to participate in promotion committees, write cross-functional peer reviews, and calibrate candidates against the level bar. This is not a soft leadership task. It is a high-stakes governance function that directly affects compensation, retention, and team composition. Staff engineers who engage with this poorly, writing generic praise or failing to distinguish between L5 and L6 evidence, create miscalibration that propagates through the organization for years.

The ADR-equivalent for promotions is the promotion rationale document. A well-written rationale names specific evidence: the RFC title, the metric improvement percentage, the cross-team initiative name, the engineer's name who was mentored and what level they are now. Vague rationales produce contested promotions. Specific rationales produce fast, clean decisions.

At the L7 bar, interviewers sometimes ask directly: "Tell me about the last time you blocked a promotion and why." The expected answer demonstrates that you hold a real bar rather than optimizing for team happiness. Engineers who can articulate what was missing, not just what was present, signal that they understand the level expectations well enough to defend them under pressure. That is a governance maturity signal that distinguishes L7 from L6 thinking.
