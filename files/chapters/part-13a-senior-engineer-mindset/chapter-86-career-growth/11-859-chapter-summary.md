## 86.10 Core Concepts Recap

### Key Takeaways

The following points distill the most important lessons from this chapter so far. If you remember nothing else, remember these six principles. They represent the consensus wisdom from engineers who have successfully navigated the path from Senior to Staff and beyond at top technology companies.

1. **Understand level expectations** - Know what's required at your target level
2. **Build a growth plan** - Specific actions, timelines, and success measures
3. **Prove it before promotion** - Demonstrate next-level work first
4. **Make your work visible** - Great work in the dark doesn't get promoted
5. **Have career conversations** - Regular check-ins with your manager
6. **Build relationships** - Your network enables opportunities

### Career Growth Checklist

Use this checklist as a periodic health check on your career development efforts. If you cannot check off most of these items, revisit the relevant sections of this chapter and take action. Career growth is not passive. It requires deliberate, sustained effort across multiple dimensions.

- [ ] Know what's expected at your target level
- [ ] Identify your specific gaps
- [ ] Have a written development plan
- [ ] Have a sponsor who advocates for you
- [ ] Regular career conversations with manager
- [ ] Building visibility for your work
- [ ] Tracking your accomplishments

### Junior → FAANG: Turning the Checklist into an Interview Story Map

Each item on the career growth checklist maps to a behavioral question in a FAANG loop. "Know what's expected at your target level" maps to: "What does it mean to operate at Senior level?" "Building visibility for your work" maps to: "How do you ensure your impact is recognized beyond your immediate team?"

Before submitting a FAANG application, go through this checklist and verify that you have a concrete example for each item. Gaps on the checklist are gaps in your interview story bank. Fill them before the recruiter call, not after.

The checklist also helps with the "where are you in your career growth?" question that bar-raisers use to open behavioral rounds. A candidate who can say "I have a written development plan, I have quarterly career conversations with my manager, I have an identified sponsor, and here is the specific gap I am actively closing right now" signals readiness for the next level more convincingly than any technical answer.

### Senior @ FAANG: The Checklist as an Engineering Culture Diagnostic

At staff and principal level, this checklist is a team health instrument, not just a personal one. Running a quarterly "career growth health check" across your team, asking each engineer whether they can check these items, surfaces risks before they become resignations.

A team where fewer than half the engineers have a written development plan has a career growth infrastructure problem. A team where no engineer can name a sponsor outside their direct management chain has a visibility and advocacy gap. These are organizational symptoms, not individual failures, and they require organizational interventions: structured career conversations, explicit sponsorship programs, regular skip-level 1:1s, and public recognition mechanisms.

The most senior bar, the L7 and above consideration, is whether the career growth culture you established persists when you leave. If the development plans, sponsor relationships, and promotion cadence on your team depend on your personal involvement, you have not built infrastructure. You have created dependency. The test of senior-level career growth leadership is that your team's engineers continue to grow at the same rate when you move to a new area or take a sabbatical.
