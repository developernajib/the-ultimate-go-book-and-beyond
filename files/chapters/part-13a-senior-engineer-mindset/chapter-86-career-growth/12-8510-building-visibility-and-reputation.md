## 86.11 Building Visibility and Reputation

Great work that nobody knows about doesn't advance your career. Building visibility is not about self-promotion - it's about ensuring your impact is recognized.

### Visibility Strategies

There are two broad categories of visibility: internal (within your company) and external (in the broader industry). For promotion purposes, internal visibility matters far more in the short term. The framework below organizes specific activities you can do to make your work known to the people who influence career decisions. You do not need to do everything listed here. Choose the activities that feel natural and sustainable for you.

### Visibility Without Bragging

One of the biggest concerns engineers have about self-promotion is coming across as arrogant or taking credit for team work. The good news is that effective visibility does not require bragging. The key is to frame your communication around sharing learnings and acknowledging collaborators rather than listing personal achievements. The examples below show the difference between self-promotion that alienates people and impact-focused sharing that builds your reputation.

```markdown
# The Art of Visible Humility

## BAD: Self-promotion
"I single-handedly saved the company $1M by fixing the database issue."

## GOOD: Impact-focused sharing
"Our team reduced database costs by $1M. I'm excited to share the
approach we took in case it helps other teams facing similar issues."

## BAD: Taking credit
"I designed and implemented the new payment system."

## GOOD: Giving credit while participating
"I worked with Sarah on the payment system design. She had the initial
idea, and I helped validate the approach and led the implementation.
Here's what we learned..."

## BAD: Humble bragging
"I was SO surprised when the VP asked me to present to the board..."

## GOOD: Graceful acknowledgment
"I had the opportunity to present to the board on our API strategy.
Here are my takeaways on communicating with executives..."

## Principles:
1. Share learning, not achievement
2. Acknowledge collaborators
3. Focus on "what we learned" not "what I did"
4. Make it useful for others
5. Be matter-of-fact, not boastful
```

### Building Technical Reputation

Building a strong technical reputation is an investment that compounds over time. The activities below are organized by effort level so you can start small and increase your investment as you see results. Even the low-effort activities, done consistently over months, can significantly change how people in your organization perceive your expertise and value.

```
REPUTATION BUILDING ACTIVITIES BY TIME INVESTMENT:

LOW EFFORT (1-2 hours/week):
├── Answer questions in Slack/forums
├── Write thorough code review comments
├── Share interesting articles with commentary
├── Update team documentation
└── Help with on-call issues

MEDIUM EFFORT (3-5 hours/week):
├── Write internal blog posts
├── Give team tech talks
├── Mentor a junior engineer
├── Create reusable tools/scripts
└── Lead small cross-team initiatives

HIGH EFFORT (5-10 hours/week):
├── Write external blog posts
├── Contribute to open source
├── Speak at meetups/conferences
├── Lead major architectural initiatives
└── Create educational content

CHOOSE BASED ON YOUR GOALS:
- Want internal promotion? Focus on internal visibility
- Want industry recognition? Focus on external visibility
- Want Staff+ role? Focus on cross-team impact visibility
- Want to change companies? Balance internal and external
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building Visibility Before You Need It

FAANG interviews require social proof that goes beyond your resume. Hiring committees look at your GitHub activity, internal design docs, tech talks, and peer review feedback to triangulate whether your claims about impact are credible. Engineers who have been publishing and contributing visibly before they apply are far easier to level correctly.

The "visible humility" framework in this section is especially important for behavioral interviews. Candidates who say "I built X" consistently score lower than candidates who say "I led the team in building X, with Sarah owning the API design and Marcus handling the infrastructure." The second framing demonstrates leadership and gives credit, which is exactly what FAANG evaluators are looking for.

Start building internal visibility now, regardless of your current company. Write the postmortem. Present at the team meeting. Document the system no one has documented. These artifacts travel with your reputation even when your name does not appear in the byline.

### Senior @ FAANG: Engineering Visibility as a Team Infrastructure Investment

At L6 and above, individual visibility becomes less important than building the mechanisms that give your team's engineers visibility. Staff engineers who publish their own blog posts but do not create structures for others to publish are building personal brands, not engineering cultures.

The highest-leverage visibility infrastructure at staff level includes: a team engineering blog with regular cadence, a rotation for presenting at org-wide all-hands, a process for nominating strong work for company-wide recognition programs, and a habit of explicit attribution when citing other teams' work in cross-functional discussions.

Reputation at senior levels also has an organizational dimension that goes beyond individual recognition. Your team's reputation, its known strengths, its reliability, its technical judgment, determines how much authority you are given on cross-team decisions, which platform investments you are consulted on, and whether your team is seen as the place strong engineers want to transfer into. Staff engineers who manage team reputation explicitly, through consistent delivery, public postmortems, and high-quality public artifacts, accumulate organizational capital that individual visibility cannot match.

ADR-level thinking applies here: when a team's reputation is poor, there is usually a traceable root cause. An unreliable on-call, a poorly written design doc that failed publicly, or an incident where the team was slow to communicate. Staff engineers who treat reputation as a managed asset, tracking incidents in terms of organizational perception as well as technical resolution, operate at a distinctly higher maturity level.
