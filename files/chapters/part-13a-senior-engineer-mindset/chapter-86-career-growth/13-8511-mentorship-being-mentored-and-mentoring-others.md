## 86.12 Mentorship: Being Mentored and Mentoring Others

### Finding and Working with Mentors

Mentorship comes in many forms, and understanding the different types helps you seek out the right kind of support for your specific needs. Not every mentor needs to be a formal, long-term relationship. Some of the most valuable mentoring happens through short, focused engagements around a specific skill or decision. The framework below categorizes the five types of mentors and explains where to find them.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    MENTORSHIP FRAMEWORK                          │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  TYPES OF MENTORS:                                               │
│                                                                  │
│  1. SKILL MENTOR                                                 │
│     Focus: Specific technical skill development                  │
│     Example: "Help me become better at system design"            │
│     Duration: Usually 3-6 months for a skill                     │
│                                                                  │
│  2. CAREER MENTOR                                                │
│     Focus: Career strategy and navigation                        │
│     Example: "How do I grow to Staff level?"                     │
│     Duration: Often years, evolving relationship                 │
│                                                                  │
│  3. SPONSOR (Different from mentor!)                             │
│     Focus: Advocates for you in rooms you're not in              │
│     Example: Recommends you for opportunities                    │
│     Duration: Usually while at same company                      │
│                                                                  │
│  4. PEER MENTOR                                                  │
│     Focus: Mutual growth and accountability                      │
│     Example: Two Senior engineers studying for Staff             │
│     Duration: Varies, often informal                             │
│                                                                  │
│  5. REVERSE MENTOR                                               │
│     Focus: Learning from someone junior                          │
│     Example: New grad teaching you about new tech                │
│     Duration: Topic-specific                                     │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

WHERE TO FIND MENTORS:
├── Current company: Skip-level manager, senior ICs, other teams
├── Former company: Ex-colleagues who've advanced
├── Professional networks: Meetups, conferences, alumni groups
├── Online: Plato, MentorCruise, ADPList
├── Open source: Maintainers of projects you contribute to
└── Social media: Reach out to people whose content you value
```

### How to Be a Good Mentee

The quality of mentorship you receive depends heavily on how you show up as a mentee. Mentors are volunteering their time and experience, so the more prepared and engaged you are, the more they will invest in your growth. Engineers who come to meetings with specific questions, follow through on advice, and report back on results consistently get more value from their mentoring relationships.

```markdown
# Effective Mentee Behaviors

## Before Each Meeting:
- Come with specific questions or topics
- Share what you've tried and learned since last time
- Prepare context so mentor can jump in quickly

## During the Meeting:
- Take notes (shows you value their time)
- Ask follow-up questions
- Be honest about struggles and mistakes
- Ask "how" and "why" not just "what"

## After the Meeting:
- Send a thank-you note
- Implement at least one piece of advice
- Follow up on results ("I tried X and here's what happened...")

## Sample Meeting Structure (30 min):
- 5 min: Update on progress since last time
- 15 min: Main topic/question for today
- 5 min: Mentor shares related experience
- 5 min: Action items and next meeting topics

## What NOT to Do:
✗ Show up without preparation
✗ Only reach out when you need something
✗ Expect mentor to solve your problems
✗ Ignore advice and keep asking the same questions
✗ Ghost or flake on meetings
```

### Being an Effective Mentor

Mentoring others is not just good for the mentee. It is one of the strongest signals of senior-level leadership that promotion committees look for. Effective mentoring is a skill in itself, and it requires a different approach than simply giving answers. The best mentors ask questions that help the mentee develop their own judgment, share experiences rather than prescriptions, and know when to step back and let the mentee struggle productively.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                EFFECTIVE MENTORING PRACTICES                     │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  INITIAL SETUP:                                                  │
│  ├── Set clear expectations and cadence                          │
│  ├── Understand mentee's goals and current level                 │
│  ├── Define what success looks like                              │
│  └── Agree on communication preferences                          │
│                                                                  │
│  EFFECTIVE TECHNIQUES:                                           │
│  ├── Ask questions more than give answers                        │
│  │   "What have you tried?" "What's your hypothesis?"            │
│  │                                                               │
│  ├── Share experiences, not prescriptions                        │
│  │   "When I faced X, I tried Y because Z..."                    │
│  │                                                               │
│  ├── Help them think, don't think for them                       │
│  │   "What are the trade-offs you're considering?"               │
│  │                                                               │
│  ├── Give specific, actionable feedback                          │
│  │   "In your design doc, section 3 could be clearer if..."      │
│  │                                                               │
│  └── Connect them with others                                    │
│      "You should talk to Sarah about this, she faced similar"    │
│                                                                  │
│  WHAT TO AVOID:                                                  │
│  ├── Solving problems for them                                   │
│  ├── Being too prescriptive                                      │
│  ├── Projecting your path onto them                              │
│  ├── Canceling meetings frequently                               │
│  └── Only focusing on technical skills                           │
│                                                                  │
│  TRACKING PROGRESS:                                              │
│  ├── Keep notes on goals and progress                            │
│  ├── Celebrate wins explicitly                                   │
│  ├── Adjust approach based on what's working                     │
│  └── Know when to conclude the formal relationship               │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Junior → FAANG: Being a Mentee Who Creates Good Stories

Every mentoring relationship you have as a junior or mid-level engineer generates potential interview stories, but only if you are a deliberate mentee. Following the "effective mentee behaviors" framework ensures that you extract concrete lessons and outcomes, not just general guidance. "My mentor helped me understand system design better" is not an interview answer. "My mentor challenged me to write a design doc for our caching layer, which led to a team-wide adoption and became the template for three subsequent projects" is.

When seeking mentors before a FAANG application, prioritize engineers at your target company who will give you calibrated feedback on whether your examples map to the level you are targeting. A mentor who has interviewed at your target company is worth more than a mentor who is technically senior but has no FAANG context.

The reverse mentor concept is underutilized for interview preparation. If you have mentored someone on a newer technology or process, that story demonstrates both teaching ability and willingness to learn from all directions, both of which are explicit FAANG cultural values.

### Senior @ FAANG: Mentorship as Organizational Infrastructure

At L6 and above, mentoring individuals is necessary but insufficient. The expectation is that you build mentorship infrastructure that scales beyond your direct relationships.

Google's readability reviewer system is the canonical example: a distributed mentorship mechanism where language expertise is shared through the code review process itself, not one-on-one conversations. Staff engineers at FAANG who have built equivalent infrastructure, a shared runbook culture, a peer RFC review process, a technical seminar series, achieve mentorship at 10x the throughput of individual mentoring relationships.

The distinction between mentoring and sponsoring, covered in the next section, is particularly sharp at senior levels. Mentoring without sponsoring is lower-leverage than the combination. A bar-raising staff engineer does both: develops engineers through direct engagement and then advocates for those engineers in calibration and promotion discussions. Engineers who only mentor but never sponsor produce development experiences that do not convert into career advancement, which eventually destroys trust in the mentoring relationship.

Measuring mentorship effectiveness at team scale requires tracking outcomes, not activities. Monthly 1:1 count is an activity metric. Engineers promoted per year, time-to-productivity for new hires, and retention rate for engineers with formal mentors are outcome metrics. Staff engineers who report on mentoring using outcome metrics rather than activity metrics signal that they think about mentoring as an investment with measurable returns.
