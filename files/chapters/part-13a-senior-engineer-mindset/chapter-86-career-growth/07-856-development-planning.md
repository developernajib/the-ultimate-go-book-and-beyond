## 86.6 Development Planning

### Personal Development Plan Template

A personal development plan (PDP) turns vague career aspirations into actionable steps with deadlines. Without one, it is easy to spend years "planning to grow" without making measurable progress. The template below gives you a structured format to fill in with your manager. The most important parts are the specific actions, the deadlines, and the success measures. A good PDP should fit on one page and be reviewed quarterly.

### Example Development Plan

To make the template above more concrete, here is a filled-in example for a Senior Engineer targeting a Staff promotion within 12 to 18 months. Notice how each gap has specific, time-bound actions rather than vague goals like "get better at leadership." The example also includes a named sponsor and mentor, because career growth rarely happens in isolation. Use this as a model when filling in your own plan.

```
NAME: Alex Chen              DATE: 2025-01-15
CURRENT LEVEL: Senior (L5)   TARGET LEVEL: Staff (L6)
TARGET TIMELINE: 12-18 months

GAP 1: Cross-team impact (currently team-scope only)
Actions:
□ Lead API standardization initiative across platform by Q2
□ Present at engineering all-hands on payment system by Q1
□ Mentor engineer from Auth team on distributed systems by Q3
Success measure: Recognized contribution to 2+ teams

GAP 2: Technical vision and strategy
Actions:
□ Write 2-year technical roadmap for payments domain by Q1
□ Author RFC for event sourcing migration by Q2
□ Lead architecture review for 3 proposals by Q2
Success measure: Roadmap approved, RFC accepted

GAP 3: Visibility and influence
Actions:
□ Publish 4 internal blog posts on distributed systems by Q4
□ Lead 2 brownbag sessions by Q2
□ Get skip-level feedback quarterly
Success measure: Recognized by leaders outside my team

QUARTERLY CHECK-IN DATES:
Q1: Apr 15      Q2: Jul 15      Q3: Oct 15

SPONSOR: Sarah (Director)    MENTOR: Mike (Staff at another company)
```

### Junior → FAANG: Treating Your PDP as an Interview Preparation Tool

The personal development plan template in this section doubles as an interview preparation framework. Every gap you identify, and every action you take to close it, becomes a behavioral interview story. The quarterly check-in cadence ensures you have fresh examples when the recruiter calls.

The most common PDP failure mode for junior and mid-level engineers is choosing actions that are too passive: "read about distributed systems" instead of "propose and run a design review for the caching layer." Reading does not produce interview examples. Doing does. Make every PDP action something that will generate a concrete, nameable artifact or outcome.

Sharing your PDP with your manager and explicitly asking "what am I missing?" produces better input than any amount of self-reflection alone. Managers have visibility into which opportunities are coming up and can route them to engineers who have expressed interest. Engineers who do not share their development goals rarely get the stretch assignments.

### Senior @ FAANG: Development Planning at Organizational Scale

At L6 and above, your own PDP becomes a secondary concern relative to the development planning infrastructure you build for others. Staff engineers who have shipped significant growth infrastructure include: onboarding programs that accelerated time-to-productivity for new hires, technical mentorship programs with explicit rubrics and paired pairings, and internal mobility processes that gave engineers access to the cross-team experience they needed for promotion.

The highest-leverage development planning at staff level is identifying and removing structural barriers that prevent engineers from closing their promotion gaps. If every engineer on the team is blocked on "cross-team impact" because the team's projects are all self-contained, the development plan fix is not to tell each engineer to network harder. It is to restructure projects so that cross-team collaboration is built into the work. That is a systems-level intervention, not an individual-level one.

When participating in executive conversations about engineering org health, staff engineers should be able to describe the development planning culture on their team quantitatively: average time-to-promotion at each level, distribution of engineers across levels, and the career trajectory of the last five engineers who left. These metrics reveal whether development planning is working or just being performed. A team where every departure is a surprise, or where no one has been promoted in 18 months, has a development planning failure regardless of whether PDPs exist on paper.
