## 86.14 Career Pivots and Transitions

### Common Career Transitions

Career transitions are a normal and healthy part of professional growth, but they require planning and patience. Each type of transition has its own timeline, skill gaps, and challenges. The table below maps out the six most common career transitions for software engineers, so you can understand what to expect and prepare accordingly before making a move.

```

│                COMMON CAREER TRANSITIONS                         │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  IC → MANAGEMENT:                                                │
│  ├── Skills to develop: People management, delegation            │
│  ├── Time to transition: 6-12 months to feel competent           │
│  ├── Challenge: Less coding, more meetings                       │
│  └── Tip: Test it before committing (tech lead first)            │
│                                                                  │
```

### Transition Planning

A successful career transition follows a predictable four-phase process: exploration, skill building, making the move, and establishing yourself in the new role. Rushing through the early phases is the most common reason transitions fail. Taking three to six months to explore and build skills before committing reduces your risk significantly and helps you avoid the regret of discovering too late that the new role is not what you expected.

```markdown
# Career Transition Framework

## Phase 1: Exploration (1-3 months)
- Understand what the new role actually involves
- Talk to people who've made similar transitions
- Shadow someone in the target role if possible
- Be honest: Is this what you really want?

## Phase 2: Skill Building (3-12 months)
- Identify skill gaps between current and target role
- Find ways to build those skills in current role
  - Side projects within your company
  - Volunteer for cross-functional work
  - Take on responsibilities that bridge roles
- Consider formal learning if needed

## Phase 3: Making the Move (1-3 months)
Options:
1. **Internal transfer**: Lowest risk, use internal mobility
2. **Internal new role**: If company creates new position
3. **External move**: Join new company in target role
4. **Gradual transition**: Hybrid role that evolves

## Phase 4: Establishing Yourself (6-12 months)
- Accept that you're learning again
- Use your unique background
- Build new network in new domain
- Document early wins

## Questions Before Any Transition:
□ Why do I want this change? (Push vs pull)
□ What will I miss about current role?
□ What's my competitive advantage in new role?
□ What's my backup if it doesn't work out?
□ Who can support me through this transition?
```

### Junior → FAANG: Using Transitions as a Level Reset Strategy

Career transitions are high-risk and high-reward from a leveling perspective. A frontend engineer with two years of experience who transitions to backend development at a startup may come out of that transition with significantly broader ownership experience than a same-tenure engineer who stayed in their lane. That breadth can justify a higher level offer at a FAANG company.

Before executing any transition, research how the target company treats your background. A strong backend engineer who has never touched distributed systems is leveled differently at Amazon than at a startup. Identify the specific technical gaps between your current background and the company's expectations for your target level, then use the transition planning framework to close them deliberately before the interview.

The "establish yourself" phase described in this section is underestimated for interview preparation. Joining a company in a new role and building early wins within 90 days produces strong recent examples for behavioral interviews. Candidates who are 6 to 12 months into a successful transition often have better stories than candidates who have been at the same company doing the same work for four years.

### Senior @ FAANG: Managing Transitions on Your Team and Across Your Org

At L6 and above, career transitions on your team require organizational management, not just individual support. When an engineer moves from IC to management, or from one technical domain to another, the team absorbs the cost of their ramp-up period. Staff engineers who plan for this explicitly, building coverage and succession before the transition, minimize disruption and enable the engineer to take the risk without feeling guilty about the burden they are creating.

The "big company to startup" transition is particularly relevant as a staff-level retention and attrition signal. Engineers who are considering this transition are often under-challenged or over-structured. Staff engineers who can create startup-like conditions within a large organization, high ownership, fast iteration, real ambiguity, retain engineers who would otherwise leave for smaller companies. Understanding the appeal is prerequisite to addressing it.

At the organizational governance level, career transitions require explicit policy: whether the company supports role changes through internal mobility programs, how leveling is handled when an engineer switches domains, and what career protection exists for transitions that do not work out. Staff engineers who advocate for clear, written transition policies, rather than leaving them to case-by-case manager discretion, create more equitable and predictable career development environments. These policies are ADR-equivalent artifacts: they capture the organization's decisions about career transitions with the reasoning documented, so future managers are not reinventing these policies under pressure.
