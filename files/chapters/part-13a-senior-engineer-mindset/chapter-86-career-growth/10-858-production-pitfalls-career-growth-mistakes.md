## 86.9 Production Pitfalls: Career Growth Mistakes

### Pitfall 1: Waiting to Be Promoted Before Doing the Job

**Real Incident Report**: An L5 engineer waited three years for a promotion to L6 (Staff). When they asked why they weren't advancing, their manager said: "You're doing L5 work excellently. But I haven't seen evidence you can operate at L6." The engineer had been waiting for the title to do the work, but the title comes after demonstrating the work.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Expecting promotion before responsibility
- Doing current-level work excellently but not next-level work
- Waiting for permission to take on bigger scope
- Thinking "that's not my job" about stretch opportunities

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# The Promotion Paradox

WRONG mental model:
Promotion → New responsibilities → Prove yourself

RIGHT mental model:
Take on responsibilities → Demonstrate capability → Promotion confirms it

## Practical approach:
1. Study the level guide for your target level
2. Identify 2-3 things you're NOT currently doing
3. Find opportunities to demonstrate those things
4. Document evidence as you go
5. After 6-12 months of consistent next-level work,
   discuss promotion with manager
```

**Key Insight**: At Google, the promotion committee explicitly asks: "Has this person already been operating at the next level for a sustained period?" The answer must be yes before promotion.

### Pitfall 2: Optimizing for the Wrong Metrics

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer optimized for lines of code, PRs merged, and story points completed - all measurable metrics. They shipped more code than anyone on the team. At review time, feedback was: "High output but low impact. Many features were unnecessary or poorly thought through." Quantity had been optimized at the expense of value.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Chasing vanity metrics (lines of code, PRs, story points)
- Confusing activity with impact
- Not connecting work to business outcomes
- Taking on easy/visible work over important work

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Impact vs Activity

## Low-Value Metrics (Activity):
- Lines of code written
- Number of PRs merged
- Story points completed
- Meetings attended
- Hours worked

## High-Value Metrics (Impact):
- Business outcomes improved (revenue, reliability, efficiency)
- User problems solved
- Teams unblocked
- Technical debt reduced (measured by incident rate, velocity)
- Engineers made more productive

## Reframing your work:
WEAK: "Shipped 15 features this quarter"
STRONG: "Shipped checkout redesign that increased conversion 12%"

WEAK: "Completed 150 story points"
STRONG: "Reduced p99 latency by 40%, enabling $2M new enterprise deal"
```

**Key Insight**: Amazon's "working backwards" starts with customer impact, not feature output. Your career narrative should do the same.

### Pitfall 3: Building Depth Without Breadth (or Vice Versa)

**Real Incident Report**: A deeply specialized distributed systems engineer applied for Staff roles but was consistently passed over for engineers with "less technical depth." The feedback: "Exceptional in one area, but Staff engineers need to contribute across the technical landscape. Can't effectively review code or mentor outside their specialty."

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Extreme specialization without foundation
- Knowing everything about one thing but nothing about adjacent areas
- OR: Being a generalist with no area of deep expertise
- Not adapting learning focus as career progresses

**The Better Approach**:
```
T-SHAPED ENGINEER EVOLUTION:

Junior/Mid:
├── Build broad foundation
└── Start developing one deep area

Senior:
├── Solid breadth across the stack
└── Deep expertise in 1-2 areas

Staff:
├── Deep expertise in 2-3 areas
├── Working knowledge across entire stack
└── Can learn new areas quickly

Principal:
├── Industry-leading depth in 1-2 areas
├── Strong depth in 3-4 adjacent areas
└── Strategic knowledge of entire technology landscape

KEY: Alternate between depth and breadth as you grow
```

**Key Insight**: Netflix's "stunning colleagues" are T-shaped - broad enough to collaborate effectively, deep enough to be domain experts in their area.

### Pitfall 4: Not Building Relationships Until You Need Them

**Real Incident Report**: A talented engineer worked in isolation for years, consistently delivering good work. When a Staff role opened up, they had no sponsors to advocate for them. Meanwhile, a less technically skilled engineer with strong relationships across the organization was promoted. "Technical skill was similar, but one had organizational support."

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Viewing networking as "politics" to avoid
- Only reaching out when you need something
- Not building relationships with leadership
- Staying in your team silo

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Relationship Building Cadence

## Weekly:
- Coffee/lunch with someone outside your team
- Help someone in public channels (Slack, code review)
- Share knowledge (snippets, learnings, write-ups)

## Monthly:
- 1:1 with your skip-level manager
- Connect with engineers on teams you interact with
- Present at a team meeting or brown bag

## Quarterly:
- 1:1 with someone in leadership (director+)
- Cross-team project collaboration
- External visibility (blog, conference, open source)

## Key Relationships to Build:
- Your manager and their peers
- Skip-level (your manager's manager)
- Senior ICs across the org
- Product and design partners
- Future potential sponsors
```

**Key Insight**: At Meta, sponsorship is explicitly required for senior promotions. A sponsor is someone senior who will advocate for you in rooms you're not in. You can't build that relationship the week before your promotion packet is due.

### Pitfall 5: Changing Jobs at the Wrong Time

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer was 6 months from a likely Staff promotion at their current company. They got a Senior offer from a "hot" startup paying 20% more. They took it, resetting their career trajectory. The startup's growth slowed, and three years later they were still Senior - further from Staff than when they left.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Leaving right before a likely promotion
- Chasing compensation without career strategy
- Joining startups without understanding growth trajectory
- Not considering level trajectory in job changes

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Job Change Decision Framework

## Good times to leave:
- After a promotion (solidified level)
- If promotion is clearly blocked (org structure, bias)
- For a significant level-up opportunity
- If learning has stagnated
- Toxic environment affecting health

## Risky times to leave:
- 6-12 months before expected promotion
- Right after a reorg (opportunities emerge)
- During a project that will be career-defining
- Before vesting cliffs

## Questions to ask about new role:
- What level will I come in at? (Get it in writing)
- What's the path to next level? How long typically?
- What % of [level]s get promoted in a year?
- What would I be working on first 6 months?
- Can I talk to someone at my target level?
```

**Key Insight**: Neither path is uniformly faster. Internally you already have relationships, context, and demonstrated impact working for you. An external move resets that trust but reprices you at market rates, which is why job changes tend to win on compensation while internal promotion tends to win on scope continuity. Choose by which constraint is actually binding you, not by a rule of thumb.

### Quick Reference: Career Growth Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|--------------|---------|-----|
| Wait for title | "When I'm Staff, I'll do Staff work" | Demonstrate before promotion |
| Vanity metrics | High output, low impact | Connect work to business outcomes |
| Unbalanced shape | Too deep OR too broad | Alternate depth and breadth |
| Isolation | No sponsors or advocates | Build relationships before you need them |
| Bad timing | Leave before promotion, reset level | Strategic job change timing |
| Invisible impact | Great work, no recognition | Document and communicate consistently |

### Junior → FAANG: Using These Pitfalls as a Pre-Interview Diagnostic

Each pitfall in this section corresponds to a behavioral question that FAANG interviewers use to screen for it. "Waiting to be promoted before doing the job" is probed by: "Tell me about a time you took on responsibility beyond your formal role." "Optimizing for wrong metrics" is probed by: "What impact are you most proud of and how do you know it mattered?"

Run through each pitfall honestly and ask: does my interview story bank contain evidence that I have avoided this pattern? If you have no story for "finding and solving a problem that wasn't assigned," that is the anti-pattern you are currently living. Create the evidence before the interview, not in the interview room.

The job-change timing pitfall is worth studying carefully for external FAANG applications specifically. Leaving your current company 6 months before a likely promotion to chase a FAANG offer at the same level produces the worst of both worlds: you reset your promotion clock and enter a company where your track record is unknown. If a FAANG offer is genuinely superior in scope and compensation, taking it may still be correct. But do the math explicitly rather than reacting to excitement.

### Senior @ FAANG: Recognizing These Pitfalls in the Engineers You Are Growing

At L6 and above, the career pitfalls in this section are patterns you need to detect in others, not just avoid yourself. This is the organizational dimension of career growth leadership.

"Waiting for title" is the most common L5 stall pattern and the hardest to address diplomatically. The engineer is doing good L5 work and frustrated that they have not been promoted. The fix is not to encourage them to wait. It is to give them a specific L6-scoped problem with real ownership, and then be explicit: "This is the kind of work that would justify a promotion case. I want to see you operate at this level for two quarters." That conversation, had clearly and early, prevents the 36-month spiral where the engineer is eventually managed out rather than promoted.

"Vanity metrics" at team scale is a red flag for the engineering culture. When engineers are talking about "shipped features" and "story points" rather than "user retention" and "error rate reduction" in team discussions, the incentive structure is misaligned. Staff engineers correct this through how they write up achievements in all-hands presentations and team newsletters, always pairing output metrics with outcome metrics. The culture follows the exemplar.

"Not building relationships until needed" shows up at team scale as engineers who make strong technical proposals that die because they have no organizational backing. Staff engineers who have internalized this pattern invest in relationship infrastructure before it is needed: regular cross-team syncs, participation in working groups, and deliberate introductions of strong engineers to senior leaders before the promotion cycle opens. Building relationships for an engineer who needs them right now is harder and less effective than having built them six months earlier.
