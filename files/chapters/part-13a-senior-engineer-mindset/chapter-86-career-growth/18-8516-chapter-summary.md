## Summary

### Key Takeaways

These seven principles represent the complete distillation of everything covered in this chapter. They apply regardless of which company you work at, which level you are targeting, or whether you choose the IC or management track. Return to this list periodically as a compass for your career development efforts.

1. **Understand level expectations** - Know what's required at your target level
2. **Build a growth plan** - Specific actions, timelines, and success measures
3. **Prove it before promotion** - Demonstrate next-level work first
4. **Make your work visible** - Great work in the dark doesn't get promoted
5. **Have career conversations** - Regular check-ins with your manager
6. **Build relationships** - Your network enables opportunities
7. **Find sponsors, not just mentors** - Sponsors advocate for you in your absence

### Career Growth Checklist

Use this expanded checklist as a thorough health check on your career development. Review it monthly and focus on the unchecked items that will have the highest impact on your next promotion. If you can check off most of these consistently, you are well-positioned for advancement.

- [ ] Know what's expected at your target level
- [ ] Identify your specific gaps
- [ ] Have a written development plan
- [ ] Have a sponsor who advocates for you
- [ ] Regular career conversations with manager
- [ ] Building visibility for your work
- [ ] Tracking your accomplishments
- [ ] Mentoring others (shows leadership)
- [ ] Contributing to hiring and interviews

### Junior → FAANG: Applying the Seven Principles Before Your Next Application

The seven key takeaways in this section represent the exact criteria that FAANG promotion committees and bar-raising interviewers use to evaluate candidates. Map each principle to a concrete action you will take in the next 90 days.

"Make your work visible" is the principle most commonly violated by technically strong engineers. If you cannot describe in one sentence what your skip-level manager knows about your last major contribution, your work is not visible enough. Send an update. Write a one-pager. Present at the team meeting. Do it this week.

The career growth checklist is most valuable when shared with your manager and treated as a joint accountability document. Engineers who hide their checklist and optimize privately get less manager support than engineers who share it openly and ask for help closing specific gaps. FAANG managers have seen hundreds of engineers at your level and can often point directly to the opportunity you are missing.

### Senior @ FAANG: Institutionalizing the Seven Principles for Your Org

At L6 and above, the seven principles need to become embedded in how your organization operates, not just internalized by individual engineers.

"Understand level expectations" at org scale means that level rubrics are written, published, applied consistently, and reviewed annually. Organizations where level expectations are implicit, known to managers but not documented, produce chronic calibration problems. Staff engineers who push for explicit, public level rubrics are investing in long-term org health even when it creates short-term friction.

"Find sponsors, not just mentors" at org scale requires building explicit sponsorship structures. If sponsorship is entirely informal, it will be distributed inequitably. Organizations with formal sponsorship programs, where senior engineers are matched with high-potential engineers and held accountable for advocating for them, produce more equitable promotion distributions and better retention. This is a policy intervention, not a cultural one, and it requires staff-level advocacy to get implemented.

The highest-maturity version of career growth culture is one where these seven principles are self-reinforcing: visible work creates sponsorship opportunities, sponsorship creates advocacy in promotion discussions, successful promotions validate the process and create new mentors and sponsors. Staff engineers who design their team's career growth culture with this flywheel in mind build organizations that attract strong talent precisely because the career growth environment is known to be healthy.
