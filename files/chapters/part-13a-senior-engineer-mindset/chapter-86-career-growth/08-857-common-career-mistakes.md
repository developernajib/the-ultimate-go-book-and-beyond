## 86.7 Common Career Mistakes

Even talented engineers can stall their careers by falling into predictable traps. The anti-patterns listed below are drawn from real observations at major technology companies. Recognizing these patterns early can save you years of frustration. As you read through each one, honestly assess whether any of them describe your current behavior, and use the suggested fixes to course-correct before these habits become deeply ingrained.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    CAREER ANTI-PATTERNS                          │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  1. WAITING TO BE NOTICED                                        │
│     Mistake: Assuming great work speaks for itself               │
│     Reality: You need to make your work visible                  │
│     Fix: Share updates, write docs, present results              │
│                                                                  │
│  2. OPTIMIZING FOR CURRENT ROLE                                  │
│     Mistake: Being great at current level, not next              │
│     Reality: You get promoted for next-level work                │
│     Fix: Take on stretch projects, grow scope                    │
│                                                                  │
│  3. NEGLECTING RELATIONSHIPS                                     │
│     Mistake: Focusing only on technical skills                   │
│     Reality: Relationships enable opportunities                  │
│     Fix: Build network within and outside your team              │
│                                                                  │
│  4. COMPARING TO OTHERS                                          │
│     Mistake: Measuring against peers constantly                  │
│     Reality: Everyone's path is different                        │
│     Fix: Focus on your own growth trajectory                     │
│                                                                  │
│  5. AVOIDING HARD CONVERSATIONS                                  │
│     Mistake: Not discussing career with manager                  │
│     Reality: Manager can't help if they don't know               │
│     Fix: Regular career conversations, be specific               │
│                                                                  │
│  6. JOB HOPPING TOO MUCH (OR NOT ENOUGH)                         │
│     Too much: No deep impact, looks unstable                     │
│     Too little: Miss market opportunities                        │
│     Balance: 2-4 years per role typically                        │
│                                                                  │
│  7. ONLY DOING ASSIGNED WORK                                     │
│     Mistake: Waiting for opportunities to come to you            │
│     Reality: Staff+ find and create opportunities                │
│     Fix: Identify problems, propose solutions                    │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Junior → FAANG: Recognizing Anti-Patterns Before They Cost You an Offer

The career anti-patterns in this section are most dangerous for engineers who are technically strong but operationally naive. FAANG interview loops include behavioral rounds specifically because strong technical performance does not predict these behaviors. A candidate can ace four coding rounds and then reveal "only doing assigned work" thinking in a behavioral question: "I completed all my tickets every sprint and never missed a deadline." That answer, without any evidence of initiative, reads as L3 behavior to an L5 interviewer.

The "waiting to be noticed" pattern is especially costly in a FAANG context because visibility is competitive. Dozens of engineers at your level are shipping comparable work. The ones who get promoted are the ones whose work is seen and cited by people outside their team. Before your next quarterly review, ask yourself: does your manager's manager know your name? If not, that is the gap to close.

"Only doing assigned work" is the most disqualifying pattern for senior-level offers. Every L5 behavioral interview includes at least one "tell me about a time you went beyond your scope" question. If your honest answer is "I never really did that," you need to create that experience before applying.

### Senior @ FAANG: Anti-Pattern Recognition as a Team Health Signal

At L6 and above, the anti-patterns in this section become organizational health indicators that you monitor at the team level, not just warning signs for your own career.

"Waiting to be noticed" at scale manifests as a team where engineers do excellent work that never propagates outside the team's Slack channel. This is a visibility infrastructure problem, not a talent problem. Staff engineers fix it by creating publishing norms: weekly engineering updates, internal blog posts, regular all-hands presentations, and explicit attribution in cross-team communications.

"Only doing assigned work" at team scale means the team is reactive rather than proactive. Staff engineers who have internalized what good looks like can detect this from the team's RFC-to-feature ratio: if the team is shipping many features but writing zero RFCs and proposing zero technical investments, it is operating below its potential. The fix is not to tell engineers to be more proactive. It is to create structured space for proactive work: dedicated exploration time, explicit "problem identification" meetings, and public recognition when engineers find and define problems rather than just solve them.

"Neglecting relationships" at org scale shows up as a team that is constantly surprised by decisions made elsewhere in the organization. The team finds out about platform changes after they ship, discovers that another team built the same library, or loses priority battles because no one knew the fight was coming. Staff engineers address this with deliberate relationship maintenance at the organizational level: regular syncs with adjacent teams, participation in cross-functional working groups, and awareness of who the key decision-makers are in the org before a decision needs to be made.
