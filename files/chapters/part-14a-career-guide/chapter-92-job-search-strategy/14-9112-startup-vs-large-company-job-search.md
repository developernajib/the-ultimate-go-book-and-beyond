## 92.13 Startup vs Large Company Job Search

The job search process differs significantly depending on whether you are targeting startups or large established companies. Startups tend to move faster, have less structured interviews, and evaluate for breadth and adaptability. Large companies have standardized processes, leveling systems, and more predictable compensation structures. Understanding these differences helps you tailor your preparation and set appropriate expectations for each type of company.

### Startup Job Search Strategy

Finding startup roles requires different channels than large company roles. Startups often do not post on mainstream job boards, and many of the best opportunities are found through venture capital portfolio pages, startup-specific job boards, and direct networking. The interview process at startups is also more variable, so you need to be prepared for anything from a take-home project to a conversation with the founder over coffee.

```markdown
# Finding and Landing Startup Roles

## WHERE TO FIND STARTUP JOBS:

### Job Boards:
- Wellfound (formerly AngelList Talent)
- Work at a Startup (YC companies)
- Underdog.io (curated startups)
- Startup Jobs (general)
- LinkedIn (filter by company size)

### Venture Sources:
- a16z portfolio (a16z.com/portfolio)
- Sequoia portfolio companies
- Y Combinator companies (workatastartup.com)
- Other VC portfolio pages

### Direct Discovery:
- Product Hunt launches
- Tech news (TechCrunch, etc.)
- Twitter/X startup community
- Local startup ecosystem events

## STARTUP INTERVIEW DIFFERENCES:

### Process (Usually):
- Faster (1-3 weeks total)
- Fewer rounds (3-4 typically)
- More varied (less standardized)
- May include practical projects
- Founder/CEO often involved

### What They Look For:
- Adaptability and learning speed
- Ownership mentality
- Full-stack thinking (even if specialist)
- Culture fit (small team dynamics)
- Comfort with ambiguity

### Questions to Ask:
- "What's the runway?"
- "What's the path to profitability?"
- "Who are your investors?"
- "What happens if you don't hit targets?"
- "What's the equity structure?"

## STARTUP COMPENSATION:

### Understanding the Trade-offs:
| Component | Startup | Big Tech |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Base      | Lower   | Higher   |
| Equity    | High % ownership | RSUs |
| Liquidity | Unknown | Liquid   |
| Upside    | Massive potential | Steady growth |
| Risk      | Company may fail | Layoffs possible |

### Evaluating Startup Equity:
- What % of company?
- Fully diluted shares?
- Strike price vs 409A?
- Vesting schedule?
- Cliff?
- What triggers acceleration?
- Latest valuation?
- Preferred vs common?

### Red Flags:
⚠️ Won't discuss equity details
⚠️ No clear path to liquidity
⚠️ High burn rate with short runway
⚠️ Founder conflicts visible
⚠️ High turnover in leadership
```

### Large Company Job Search Strategy

Landing a role at a large tech company requires a different approach than startups. The processes are more standardized, the competition is fiercer due to brand recognition, and the leveling system directly determines your compensation. Referrals are especially important at large companies because the volume of applications is so high that cold submissions often get lost in the applicant tracking system.

```markdown
# Landing Roles at Large Companies

## FINDING ROLES:

### Company Career Pages:
- Most roles posted internally first
- Sign up for job alerts
- Check frequently (roles fill fast)

### Referral Networks:
- Use LinkedIn to find connections
- Large companies have strong referral bonuses
- Employees incentivized to refer

### Executive Recruiters:
- For senior roles especially
- Build relationships before you need them
- They know the hiring managers

## BIG COMPANY INTERVIEW DIFFERENCES:

### Process (Usually):
- Longer (4-12 weeks)
- More standardized
- Multiple rounds
- Leveling discussions
- May have hiring committees

### What They Look For:
- Specific technical skills
- Evidence of scale experience
- Cultural alignment
- Collaboration examples
- Growth trajectory

### Navigating Levels:
Understanding the interview determines your level:
- Perform at the level you want, not where you are
- Ask about leveling expectations
- Senior+ requires leadership evidence
- Staff+ requires cross-team impact

## LARGE COMPANY COMPENSATION:

### Understanding Components:
| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Base | Annual salary, reviewed yearly |
| Stock | RSUs, typically 4-year vest |
| Bonus | Target %, varies by performance |
| Signing | One-time, often negotiable |
| Refresh | Annual stock grants (varies) |

### Level Differences:
- Huge jump between levels
- Research levels.fyi for ranges
- Understand refresh policies
- Ask about promotion rates

### Benefits Value:
- Healthcare (often excellent)
- 401k match
- Parental leave
- Education stipends
- Commute benefits
These can add $30-50K+ in value
```

### Comparison Framework

Choosing between a startup and a large company is not a one-size-fits-all decision. It depends on your career stage, financial situation, risk tolerance, and what you are optimizing for at this point in your life. The framework below helps you think through the trade-offs systematically rather than making an emotional decision based on hype or fear.

```markdown
# Startup vs Big Tech Decision Matrix

## WHEN TO CHOOSE STARTUP:

### You Should If:
✓ High risk tolerance
✓ Want ownership and impact
✓ Thrive in ambiguity
✓ Want variety in work
✓ Don't need brand name now
✓ Believe in the specific company

### You Shouldn't If:
✗ Need stable income
✗ Want clear career path
✗ Prefer deep specialization
✗ Risk-averse personality
✗ Visa complications

## WHEN TO CHOOSE BIG TECH:

### You Should If:
✓ Want brand name on resume
✓ Value stability and benefits
✓ Want to work at scale
✓ Prefer structure and mentorship
✓ Early in career (learning)
✓ Have financial obligations

### You Shouldn't If:
✗ Want maximum equity upside
✗ Hate bureaucracy
✗ Need to wear many hats
✗ Want to move fast on decisions
✗ Dislike corporate culture

## HYBRID APPROACH:

### Career Sequencing:
"""
Common Pattern:
1. Big tech (learn, brand)
2. Startup (ownership, growth)
3. Either (based on stage of life)

Alternative:
1. Startup (early responsibility)
2. Big tech (scale experience)
3. Return to startup (leadership)
"""

### The "Both" Strategy:
- Big tech during market downturns
- Startup when market is hot
- Early career: optimize for learning
- Later career: optimize for impact
```

### Junior → FAANG: Choosing the Right First and Second Company

The startup vs large company decision at early career is primarily a learning environment decision, not a compensation decision. Large companies have structured onboarding, dedicated mentorship programs, defined leveling systems, and the resources to give you exposure to production systems at scale. Startups give you broader ownership, faster feedback loops, and the experience of operating with less infrastructure and less process. Both are valuable. The question is which type of learning your current skill level needs most.

A practical heuristic: if you have solid fundamentals and want to see how large-scale systems are engineered, designed, and operated, a large company for your first or second role will expose you to patterns you cannot replicate in a startup setting. If you have strong fundamentals and want to develop product sense, end-to-end ownership, and comfort with ambiguity, a well-funded startup in an interesting space will develop those muscles faster.

For Go engineers specifically: the large-company Go roles (Google infrastructure, Cloudflare, Stripe, Uber) are exceptionally good early-career learning environments if you can get in, because you will see Go at the scale that defined many of its design decisions. The challenge is that these roles are competitive and early-career hiring at these companies is limited. A strong two to three year stint at a funded startup using Go in production is an excellent alternative that can qualify you for FAANG mid-level hiring.

### Senior @ FAANG: The Strategic Value of Each Company Type at Senior Level

At senior and staff level, the startup vs large company decision has different dimensions. The financial analysis is more complex (startup equity vs FAANG RSUs vs cash), the career trajectory implications are more pronounced (startup staff engineer vs FAANG L6 have very different compensation ceilings and market value trajectories), and the organizational influence available differs substantially.

The case for large company at senior level: FAANG staff engineer roles have clear leveling, competitive compensation that compounds through RSU refreshes, access to complex distributed systems problems at genuine scale, and name-brand recognition that positively affects future job searches. The case against: slower decision-making, more organizational politics, and less direct product impact per engineer.

The case for senior startup at this level: you often have more organizational influence, faster equity appreciation if the company has strong fundamentals, and more direct product impact. The case against: equity is illiquid and high-risk, compensation variance is high, and leadership quality is less predictable.

At L6 and above, the governance question for the startup case is whether the company has the engineering leadership quality to deploy your skills effectively. A staff engineer at a company without experienced technical leadership will spend the majority of their time on organizational and process problems rather than the technical leadership work they are qualified for. Due diligence on the technical leadership team (the CTO, VPE, and staff+ engineers already at the company) is as important as due diligence on the product and the equity.
