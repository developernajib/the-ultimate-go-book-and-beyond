## 91.13 Recommendation and Referral Strategy

### Getting Quality Recommendations

LinkedIn recommendations and professional references can significantly strengthen your candidacy, but only if they are specific, credible, and well-written. A vague recommendation that says "great to work with" adds nothing, while a specific one that describes a concrete achievement and its impact can tip the balance in your favor. The strategy below covers who to ask, when to ask, how to make it easy for them, and what distinguishes a recommendation that actually helps from one that does not.

```markdown
# Recommendations Strategy

## WHO TO ASK:

### Most Valuable (in order):
1. Direct manager (current or recent)
2. Senior engineer you worked closely with
3. Cross-functional partner (PM, TPM)
4. Direct report you managed
5. Peer on your team

### How Many:
- LinkedIn: 3-5 quality recommendations
- Job applications: 2-3 references ready

### When to Ask:
- After completing a successful project together
- When leaving a job (before you leave!)
- During performance review period
- After a specific accomplishment

## HOW TO ASK:

### The Ask Template:
"""
Hi [Name],

I'm updating my LinkedIn profile and would love a
recommendation from you if you're comfortable.

We worked together on [specific project] where I
[specific contribution]. If you could speak to
[specific skill/quality], that would be most helpful.

Happy to reciprocate! Let me know if you'd like a
recommendation as well.

Thanks,
[Your name]
"""

### Make It Easy for Them:
- Remind them what you worked on
- Suggest what to highlight
- Offer to draft something they can edit
- Give them plenty of time (2 weeks)

### Follow Up:
- Thank them genuinely
- Offer to reciprocate
- Stay in touch periodically

## WHAT MAKES A GOOD RECOMMENDATION:

### STRONG RECOMMENDATION:
"""
I worked with Alex on the payment routing system at
Stripe. Alex's ability to break down complex distributed
systems problems was exceptional. When we faced a critical
latency issue affecting $50M in daily transactions, Alex
not only diagnosed the root cause but designed and
implemented a solution that reduced p99 latency by 70%.

Beyond technical skills, Alex elevated the entire team
through code reviews and documentation. I'd hire Alex
again in a heartbeat for any backend systems role.
"""

What makes it strong:
- Specific project mentioned
- Quantified impact
- Multiple dimensions (technical, teamwork)
- Clear endorsement

### WEAK RECOMMENDATION:
"""
Alex is a great engineer. We worked together at Stripe
and Alex was always helpful and a good team player.
I enjoyed working with Alex and would recommend them.
"""

What makes it weak:
- Generic statements
- No specific examples
- No measurable impact
- Could describe anyone

## GETTING REFERRALS:

### Building Referral Network:
1. Stay connected with former colleagues
2. Attend industry events and meetups
3. Be helpful to your network first
4. Keep LinkedIn updated

### How to Ask for a Referral:
"""
Hi [Name],

I saw that [Company] is hiring for [Role]. I'm really
interested because [specific reason related to company].

Given my experience with [relevant skill], I think I'd
be a strong fit. Would you be comfortable referring me?

I've attached my resume. Happy to chat about the role
if you'd like more context on my fit.

Thanks for considering!
[Your name]
"""

### Referral Etiquette:
- Only ask people who know your work
- Don't mass-message your network
- Be specific about the role
- Make it easy (provide resume, role link)
- Thank them regardless of outcome
- Update them on process
```

### Building Your Professional Network

Networking does not have to mean awkward small talk at events. For engineers, the most effective networking is built on genuine helpfulness: answering questions, sharing knowledge, and staying in touch with people you have actually worked with. The approach below is designed specifically for introverts and people who dislike traditional networking, focusing on activities that feel natural and build authentic relationships over time.

```markdown
# Networking Strategy for Engineers

## THE INTROVERT-FRIENDLY APPROACH:

### You Don't Need to "Network":
Instead, focus on:
- Helping others with their problems
- Sharing knowledge publicly
- Staying in touch with people you've worked with
- Having genuine conversations about shared interests

### Practical Activities:
- Answer questions on Stack Overflow
- Comment helpfully on technical posts
- Congratulate people on achievements
- Share interesting content with commentary
- Introduce people who should know each other

## MAINTAINING YOUR NETWORK:

### Regular Touchpoints:
"""
MONTHLY:
- Send 2-3 "catching up" messages to old colleagues
- Engage with 5-10 LinkedIn posts from your network
- Share one piece of content (article, learning)

QUARTERLY:
- Coffee/call with 2-3 people in your industry
- Attend one meetup or event
- Update your own profile/presence

YEARLY:
- Review and update your network list
- Reconnect with people you've lost touch with
- Assess: "Who should I know that I don't?"
"""

### The Networking Document:
Keep a simple spreadsheet:
| Name | Company | How we know each other | Last contact | Notes |
|------|---------|------------------------|--------------|-------|

Update after every interaction.
Set reminders to reach out quarterly.

## EVENT NETWORKING (When You Have To):

### Before the Event:
- Review attendee/speaker list
- Identify 2-3 people you'd like to meet
- Prepare simple talking points

### During the Event:
Conversation starters:
- "What brings you to this event?"
- "What are you working on?"
- "What's the most interesting thing you've learned today?"

Graceful exits:
- "I'm going to grab another drink"
- "I want to say hi to someone before they leave"
- "Let me give you my card before I forget"

### After the Event:
- Send LinkedIn connections same day
- Include personal note ("Great talking about X")
- Follow up on any commitments you made
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building the Referral Network Before You Need It

The most common referral mistake at early career is asking for one when you need it. By that point, you are asking a near-stranger for a professional favor. The effective approach is to build genuine relationships with engineers at target companies before you are in job search mode, so that when the time comes, a referral is a natural next step in an existing relationship rather than a cold ask.

Concrete tactics that work: contribute to open-source projects that engineers at your target companies maintain, and have substantive technical conversations in issue threads and PRs. Attend technical meetups and engage meaningfully with the content rather than treating them as networking events. When an engineer at a target company publishes a blog post or conference talk you find genuinely interesting, send them a thoughtful response that adds to the technical conversation. Over three to six months of this, you will have real relationships with real engineers who are willing to refer you because they have already evaluated your technical judgment through your contributions and conversation.

When you do ask for a referral, make it easy. Send your potential referrer a one-paragraph summary of the specific role you want and why you are a strong fit, your updated resume, and three bullet points of specific technical work they can reference when writing the referral. Do not ask them to figure out what to say about you.

### Senior @ FAANG: Referrals and Recommendations as Network Governance

At senior and staff level, referrals and recommendations are not primarily tools for finding your next job. They are components of a long-term professional network that flows in both directions. The staff engineers who are most successful in the job market are the ones who have actively given referrals and recommendations to strong engineers throughout their careers, because those engineers are now advocates distributed across the industry.

The governance principle: manage your referral network as a long-term investment with explicit tracking. Know which strong engineers you have worked with are now at companies you respect, maintain those relationships with genuine check-ins and mutual value exchange, and be generous with referrals and recommendations for engineers whose work you can credibly vouch for. This is not transactional. It is a professional community responsibility that also happens to have career benefits.

LinkedIn recommendations at staff level should be solicited strategically. A recommendation from a well-known engineering director at a FAANG company carries far more weight than three recommendations from peer engineers. Invest in relationships with people whose professional judgment is widely respected in your target community, and ask for recommendations from them when you have genuinely delivered value in a context they can speak to. A specific, well-written recommendation from a credible source is a strong hiring committee signal at L6 and above.
