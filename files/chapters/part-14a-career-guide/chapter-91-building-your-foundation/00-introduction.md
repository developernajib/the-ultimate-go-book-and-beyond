# Chapter 91: Building Your Foundation - CV, LinkedIn, GitHub, Portfolio

> Your resume gets you the phone screen. Your GitHub gets you taken seriously. Your portfolio gets you the offer. Each one does a different job, and most engineers only invest in one of them.

The engineering job market is not a meritocracy in the way most engineers assume. Exceptional technical skill is necessary but not sufficient to land the roles you want at the companies you want to work for. Before any human at a target company evaluates your technical ability, a series of filters stand between you and the interview: ATS keyword matching, recruiter LinkedIn searches, brief portfolio scans, and hiring manager gut-checks based on a sixty-second resume review. Engineers who understand this process and optimize their materials for it consistently get more interviews than equally skilled engineers who don't. This is not about gaming the system - it is about communicating your genuine capabilities in the format that the system is designed to receive.

The foundational materials - resume, LinkedIn profile, GitHub presence, and project portfolio - each address a different moment in the hiring process and a different kind of evaluator. Your resume must be scannable by an ATS and compelling to a hiring manager who is reading it for twenty seconds before deciding whether to continue. Your LinkedIn profile must surface in recruiter searches with the right keywords while also telling a coherent professional story to the hiring manager who looks you up after seeing your application. Your GitHub profile must communicate technical seriousness and engagement to the engineer who is asked to evaluate your background - pinned repositories, contribution patterns, and code quality all speak before you say a word. Your portfolio, if you have one, is where you demonstrate real impact: not what you built, but what changed as a result of what you built.

Most engineers underinvest in these materials because they feel like self-promotion, which engineers are culturally trained to be uncomfortable with. The reframe that changes this: your resume and portfolio are not about promoting yourself. They are about making it easy for the right companies to find you and evaluate you accurately. If you have done strong work and your materials don't reflect it, that is not humility - it is a communication failure. The engineers who consistently land the most competitive roles are not always the strongest technically, but they are almost always the best at clearly articulating the value of their work in terms that hiring managers can evaluate. This chapter gives you the frameworks, templates, and honest assessments you need to build materials that match the quality of your technical abilities.

**What you'll learn in this chapter:**

- How to write a resume that passes ATS filtering and survives a twenty-second human scan - structure, keywords, length, and the impact-first bullet point formula
- How to quantify and frame your engineering accomplishments in terms of business outcomes - "reduced latency by 40%" rather than "optimized service performance"
- LinkedIn optimization for engineers: headline, summary, experience section, and skills configuration to maximize visibility in recruiter searches
- How to build a GitHub profile that communicates technical credibility: pinned repositories, README quality, contribution patterns, and what hiring managers actually look for
- Portfolio strategy: what to include, how to present it, and how to connect project work to measurable outcomes that resonate with non-engineer decision-makers
- Building a consistent professional brand across all platforms - the story that all your materials tell together about who you are and what you bring
- Common resume and portfolio mistakes that Go engineers make - too technical, too vague, wrong format for the target role - and how to fix them
- How to tailor your materials for specific company types: startups, scale-ups, big tech, and consulting - each has different expectations and evaluation criteria

**The senior engineer perspective:**

Hiring managers and engineering directors at top technology companies are consistent about what makes a resume immediately compelling: specificity and demonstrated impact. "I have read ten thousand engineering resumes," one Google hiring manager wrote, "and the ones that get my attention in the first five seconds all have the same thing: a bullet point that tells me what got better because this person was on the project. Not what they did - what changed." At Amazon, where bar-raisers review dozens of candidates per hiring loop, the candidates who advance most consistently are the ones whose materials tell a clear story about scope increasing over time. A Stripe engineering director noted that GitHub profiles have become a meaningful signal: "I'm not looking for green squares every day. I'm looking for projects that are finished, documented, and have evidence of thoughtful design decisions. One great repository tells me more than a hundred commits to other people's repos."

**Prerequisites:** Chapters 1-89, completion of at least one significant software project you can describe and quantify.

### Junior → FAANG: First Impressions and the Materials That Get You In the Room

Most early-career engineers make one of two mistakes with their materials: they write a resume that lists technologies instead of outcomes, or they build a GitHub profile that shows activity instead of judgment. Neither passes a senior engineer's five-second scan.

The core habit to build immediately is impact framing. Every bullet point on your resume should answer two questions: what changed, and by how much. "Built REST API using Go and PostgreSQL" is a job description. "Built a Go-based order processing API that reduced p99 latency from 800ms to 95ms, enabling the team to onboard three new enterprise clients" is a hiring signal. The difference is not about being impressive, it is about being specific.

For GitHub, pick two or three projects and finish them completely. A finished project has a README that explains the problem, the design decisions made, how to run it locally, and what you would do differently next time. One project with that level of documentation is worth more than a dozen half-built repositories. Recruiters and hiring managers at FAANG companies consistently report that GitHub is used to verify technical claims on the resume, not to discover new ones. Make sure your pinned repositories directly match the skills you claim.

Interview entry point: when asked "tell me about your background," your answer should map directly to what is on page one of your resume and your top pinned repository. Consistency across materials and verbal narrative builds immediate credibility.

### Senior @ FAANG: Platform Thinking for Professional Materials at L5-L7

At senior level and above, the evaluation frame shifts from "can this person build things" to "has this person operated at the scope this role requires, and can they raise the bar of the teams around them." Your materials need to answer that question before the first screen call.

The most common failure mode for L5-L6 candidates is a resume that reads like a list of projects rather than a record of increasing scope. A bar-raiser at Amazon, Google, or Meta is specifically looking for a narrative arc: individual contributor work that grew into cross-team influence, technical decisions that affected organizational direction, and situations where the candidate operated with ambiguity and drove clarity. If your resume does not show this arc explicitly, you will be leveled down regardless of your actual scope.

Architectural decision records (ADRs) and public technical writing carry significant weight at senior level. A candidate who has written a well-reasoned technical post explaining why they chose one architecture over another, what the tradeoffs were, and what they would do differently given new information is demonstrating exactly the judgment that L6 and above requires. If you have done this work internally, find ways to represent it publicly without violating confidentiality. The GitHub README for a significant project can serve this function: explain the design choices, the alternatives considered, and the production learnings.

For staff-level candidates (L7 and above), the materials question becomes a governance question: can this person define technical direction for a domain, not just execute on it? Your portfolio should include examples of multi-team alignment, engineering roadmaps you drove, or platform capabilities you built that unblocked other teams. Hiring committees at this level review the entire packet looking for evidence of multiplier effect. A strong L7 packet does not ask "what did you build". It answers "what became possible because you were here."

Maturity model signal: the difference between an L5 candidate and an L6 candidate in the materials review is usually scope, not quality. The L5 candidate's work is high-quality and well-executed. The L6 candidate's work changed how other people worked. Make sure your materials answer which one you are.
