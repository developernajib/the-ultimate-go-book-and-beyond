## 91.11 Level-Specific Foundation Strategies

### Junior Engineers (0-2 years)

As a junior engineer, your biggest challenge is competing for roles that require experience you have not had the chance to accumulate yet. The good news is that GitHub projects, personal blogs, and well-crafted applications can compensate significantly for a thin work history. The strategy below focuses on making the most of what you do have and building the signals that help you stand out from other early-career candidates.

```markdown
# Building Your Foundation as a Junior

## THE CHALLENGE:
You don't have much experience to show.
Every job wants "2+ years experience."

## YOUR STRATEGY:

### RESUME:
Emphasize:
- Education (still relevant at this stage)
- Projects (school and personal)
- Internships (all of them)
- Technical skills (be specific)

Example bullet for project:
"Built real-time chat application using Go and WebSockets,
supporting 100+ concurrent users with message persistence
to PostgreSQL and Redis pub/sub for scaling"

### GITHUB:
Critical for juniors. Your code IS your resume.
Must have:
- 3-5 complete, working projects
- At least one significant project (not tutorials)
- Clean, well-documented code
- Tests (shows you know how)

Project ideas:
- Build a CLI tool you actually use
- Implement a data structure library
- Create a web scraper for something useful
- Build a simple API for a hobby interest

### LINKEDIN:
Headline: "Software Engineer | Go, Python, Kubernetes"
(List technologies, not just job hunting status)

About section:
- What you're learning
- What you're building
- What you're looking for
- Your enthusiasm (genuine)

### BLOG:
Great way to stand out:
- Write about what you're learning
- Document your project builds
- Share beginner-friendly explanations
- "How I learned X" posts work well

## WHAT TO AVOID:
- Empty GitHub with only forks
- Resume with only coursework
- LinkedIn with no technical details
- Applying only to "junior" roles (apply to all)
```

### Mid-Level Engineers (2-5 years)

At the mid-level stage, you have real experience to show but face the highest competition because this is the most crowded level in the market. Your materials need to clearly differentiate you from the hundreds of other engineers with similar years of experience. The key is demonstrating measurable impact and professional growth, showing that you are on an upward trajectory rather than plateauing at your current level.

```markdown
# Building Your Foundation as Mid-Level

## THE CHALLENGE:
You have experience but need to differentiate.
Competition is highest at this level.

## YOUR STRATEGY:

### RESUME:
Emphasize:
- Impact metrics (mandatory now)
- Progression and growth
- Technical complexity handled
- Collaboration and ownership

Example bullet:
"Led migration of legacy monolith to microservices,
reducing deployment time from 4 hours to 15 minutes
and enabling 3 teams to deploy independently"

Education: Move to bottom, minimize

### GITHUB:
Show depth over breadth:
- Contributions to real open source
- One or two substantial projects
- Evidence of code quality focus
- Maybe start maintaining something

### LINKEDIN:
Headline: "Senior Engineer at [Company] | Go, Distributed Systems"
(Title + specialty is more searchable)

About section:
- Career narrative (progression)
- Key achievements with metrics
- What you're known for
- What you're looking for next

Get recommendations now:
- From your manager
- From peers you've helped
- From people on other teams

### BLOG:
Establish expertise:
- Write about your domain
- Share problem-solving stories
- Create reference content
- Build an audience

### SPEAKING:
Start doing this:
- Internal tech talks
- Local meetups
- Company brown bags
- Lightning talks

## WHAT TO AVOID:
- Resume without metrics
- GitHub with abandoned projects
- No evidence of collaboration
- Same resume as 2 years ago
```

### Senior Engineers (5-10 years)

At the senior level, companies expect more than strong individual technical work. Your materials need to demonstrate leadership, mentorship, cross-team influence, and the ability to drive technical strategy. If your resume reads like a list of features you personally built, it will not convey the scope and influence that senior roles demand. The strategy below focuses on shifting your narrative from "what I coded" to "what I made possible for the organization."

```markdown
# Building Your Foundation as Senior

## THE CHALLENGE:
You need to show you're more than a good coder.
Leadership and influence must be evident.

## YOUR STRATEGY:

### RESUME:
Emphasize:
- Leadership and mentorship
- Organizational impact
- Technical strategy
- Cross-team influence

Example bullets:
"Designed company-wide API standards adopted by 12 teams,
reducing integration bugs by 60% and accelerating new
service development time by 2 weeks"

"Mentored 6 engineers through promotion cycles with 100%
success rate; established team's technical interview process"

Two pages acceptable if dense with impact.

### GITHUB:
Quality signal:
- Maintained open source project
- Meaningful contributions to major projects
- Technical blog integrated
- Evidence of teaching (well-documented code)

### LINKEDIN:
Headline: "Senior/Staff Engineer | Building [thing] at [Company]"
(More specific about what you do)

About section:
- Leadership philosophy
- Technical expertise areas
- Key career achievements
- Speaking/writing highlights

Recommendations:
- From reports you've mentored
- From managers on your impact
- From cross-functional partners

### THOUGHT LEADERSHIP:
Essential at this level:
- Regular technical blogging
- Conference speaking
- Open source visibility
- Industry recognition

### NETWORK:
Use your network:
- Stay connected with former colleagues
- Be known by recruiters in your space
- Have references ready to vouch for you

## WHAT TO AVOID:
- Resume focused only on individual contribution
- No evidence of mentoring/leadership
- Zero online presence
- Waiting for opportunities to find you
```

### Staff+ Engineers (10+ years)

Staff-level and above roles are fundamentally different from senior roles, and your materials must reflect that difference. At this level, you are judged on organizational and company-wide impact, not individual contributions. Most Staff+ hires happen through network referrals and industry reputation rather than job applications, which means your public presence and professional network become your most important career assets.

```markdown
# Building Your Foundation as Staff+

## THE CHALLENGE:
You're judged on organizational impact.
Resume alone can't capture what you do.

## YOUR STRATEGY:

### RESUME:
Emphasize:
- Company-level impact
- Strategic technical decisions
- Architecture and vision
- Team and org building

Example bullets:
"Defined 3-year technical roadmap for platform team,
resulting in 40% infrastructure cost reduction and
enabling company to scale from 1M to 10M users"

"Established engineering career ladder adopted
company-wide, improving retention by 25% and
creating clear promotion paths for 200+ engineers"

Format: Achievement-focused, less about daily work

### LINKEDIN:
Headline: "Staff Engineer at [Company] | [Domain] Expert"
or: "Principal Engineer | Building [X] at Scale"

About section:
- Your technical vision
- Problems you solve at scale
- Leadership philosophy
- Industry contributions

### THOUGHT LEADERSHIP:
This IS your resume at this level:
- Conference keynotes
- Industry publications
- Podcast appearances
- Advisory roles
- Books or courses

### NETWORK:
Your network is your career:
- Other Staff+ engineers
- VPs and CTOs
- Recruiters specializing in leadership
- Industry leaders in your space

### PORTFOLIO:
Different than IC:
- Architecture documents (sanitized)
- Technical strategy artifacts
- Team building examples
- Influence demonstrations

## HOW STAFF+ GETS HIRED:
1. Network referrals (most common)
2. Executive recruiters
3. Industry reputation
4. Previous working relationships

## WHAT TO AVOID:
- Resume that reads like IC work
- No public presence
- Relying on job applications
- Not maintaining your network
```

### Junior → FAANG: Bridging the Experience Gap with Intentional Portfolio Work

The level-specific advice in this section is accurate, but the underlying principle is worth naming explicitly: at early career, you are not trying to compete with experienced candidates on depth. You are trying to demonstrate that you have the learning agility and craft foundation to grow into depth quickly. The signals that convey this are different from the signals that convey depth.

Specifically: a junior candidate who has completed one non-trivial project end-to-end, documented the decisions they made, shipped it somewhere real, and can articulate what they would change on a second attempt is demonstrating exactly the learning agility that FAANG engineering managers say they prioritize in early-career hiring. You do not need five years of experience. You need evidence of the habits that produce good engineering over five years.

For Go engineers at early career: the Go standard library is a better teacher than any framework. Building an HTTP service using only `net/http`, managing configuration without a config library, and writing table-driven tests using only the standard `testing` package demonstrates more understanding of Go than a sophisticated application built on top of three framework layers. Make that choice deliberately and explain it in your README.

### Senior @ FAANG: Level-Specific Strategies as Career Architecture

At L6 and above, the level-specific strategy section of this chapter should be read as a roadmap for the next level, not a description of the current one. If you are at L5, the L6 strategy section tells you what you need to demonstrate to make a credible case for promotion or a level-matched external hire. If you are at L6, the staff section tells you what organizational scope and external visibility you need to build to make a staff-level transition viable.

The most actionable insight for L6 candidates preparing for a staff transition: the difference between a strong L6 and an L7 is almost never technical depth. It is organizational scope and the ability to identify and solve the problems that no one has yet named. An L7 engineer is not doing L6 work faster or better. They are defining what problems L6 engineers should be working on, why those problems matter, and how the organization should be structured to solve them effectively.

Building the foundation for a staff-level transition requires intentional scope expansion over an 18-to-24-month horizon. This means actively seeking cross-team technical alignment work, driving technical direction documents that span multiple teams or quarters, and building the relationships with engineering directors and VPs who will eventually champion your case in a promotion committee. The technical skills are necessary but not sufficient. The organizational and influence skills are what the committee is actually evaluating.
