## 91.5 Building Your Brand

### Consistent Professional Brand

A professional brand is the coherent story that all your online materials tell together about who you are and what you bring to a team. When a recruiter finds your LinkedIn, then checks your GitHub, then reviews your resume, each platform should reinforce the same narrative. Inconsistencies between platforms raise red flags, while a unified story builds confidence that you are exactly who you claim to be. The elements below form the building blocks of that consistent brand.

```
BRAND ELEMENTS:

IDENTITY:
• Same name across platforms
• Same professional photo
• Consistent bio/tagline

EXPERTISE:
• What are you known for?
• What topics do you write/speak about?
• What problems do you solve?

VOICE:
• How do you communicate?
• What's your perspective?
• What values do you convey?

PRESENCE:
• Where can people find you?
• How active are you?
• What content do you create?
```

### Content Creation

Creating and sharing technical content is one of the most effective ways to build visibility in the engineering community without traditional networking. Writing blog posts, giving talks, or contributing to discussions positions you as someone who thinks deeply about engineering problems and can communicate clearly about them. You do not need to become a content creator full-time. Even a small, consistent effort compounds significantly over months and years.

```
WAYS TO BUILD VISIBILITY:

BLOG POSTS:
• Technical deep dives
• Problem-solving stories
• Technology comparisons
• "Lessons learned" posts

Platforms: Personal blog, Medium, Dev.to, company blog

SPEAKING:
• Meetup presentations
• Conference talks
• Podcast appearances
• Internal tech talks

SOCIAL:
• Twitter/X for tech discussions
• LinkedIn for professional updates
• HN/Reddit for community engagement

TEACHING:
• YouTube tutorials
• Online courses
• Mentoring
• Open source documentation

START SMALL:
• 1 blog post per month
• 1 meetup talk per quarter
• Regular LinkedIn updates
• Engage with others' content
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building a Coherent Brand Without Overclaiming

Early-career engineers often make one of two brand mistakes: they either undersell (listing technologies without context or impact, using passive language throughout) or they overclaim (describing themselves as "passionate full-stack cloud-native engineer" without evidence behind any of those terms). Both undermine credibility.

The most effective early-career brand is specific and honest. Pick one or two technical areas where you have genuine depth, name them consistently across all platforms, and let the work speak for that claim. If you are a Go backend engineer who has focused on building reliable HTTP services with proper error handling and observability, say exactly that. A narrow, honest claim backed by GitHub evidence is more convincing to a hiring manager than a broad claim with no support.

One blog post or technical writeup per quarter about something real you solved or learned is enough to establish that you are a thoughtful practitioner. It does not need to be long or comprehensive. A 500-word post explaining why you chose one Go error handling pattern over another, with code examples, is a brand signal that almost no junior candidate produces.

### Senior @ FAANG: Brand as Governance and Technical Authority

At L5 and above, brand is not self-promotion. It is the observable record of how you have exercised technical judgment in public. A staff engineer's brand is built through a series of deliberate choices about where to invest their public presence: which conferences to speak at, which technical communities to contribute to, which hard technical debates to engage in publicly with reasoned positions.

The most durable senior engineering brands are built on a single identifiable area of technical authority. Not "distributed systems" in general, but "distributed consensus and coordination" specifically. Not "cloud infrastructure" but "zero-downtime database migration patterns." The narrower and more defensible the claim, the more credible the brand, because specificity implies deep experience rather than surface familiarity.

For Go engineers at staff level, the Go proposal process and the Go issue tracker are underutilized brand-building venues. A well-reasoned proposal comment or issue report that demonstrates deep understanding of the language's design principles and the tradeoffs involved in a change is visible to thousands of engineers and to the Go team. This kind of contribution is qualitatively different from blog posts because it is evaluated in a context where technical rigor is the explicit standard.

Platform thinking for senior brand: your goal is not to be famous. It is to be credible to the specific hiring managers at the specific companies you want to work for. Map backwards from those targets: which engineers at those companies write about technical topics publicly, which conferences do their technical leaders attend and speak at, and which open-source communities do they draw engineers from. Invest your brand-building time in those specific venues rather than trying to be broadly visible.
