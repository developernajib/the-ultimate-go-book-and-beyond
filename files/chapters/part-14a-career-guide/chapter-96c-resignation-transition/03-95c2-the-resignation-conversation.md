## 96C.2 The Resignation Conversation

### Timing Your Resignation

When you resign matters almost as much as how. The day of the week, time of day, and your team's current project phase all affect how the conversation goes and how your departure is perceived. Get the timing right and the conversation goes smoothly. Get it wrong and you create unnecessary friction.

```
WHEN TO HAVE THE CONVERSATION:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  BEST TIME: Tuesday or Wednesday, mid-morning                    │
│                                                                  │
│  WHY:                                                            │
│  - Not Monday (manager dealing with week startup)                │
│  - Not Friday (leaves weekend to stew)                           │
│  - Not end of day (feels like you're running away)               │
│  - Not during crunch/deadline                                    │
│                                                                  │
│  SETTING:                                                        │
│  - Private: 1:1 meeting room or video call                       │
│  - Scheduled: "Can we chat? I have something to discuss"         │
│  - In person preferred (if in office)                            │
│                                                                  │
│  ORDER OF NOTIFICATION:                                          │
│  1. Your direct manager (ALWAYS first)                           │
│  2. Skip-level if appropriate                                    │
│  3. HR (often manager notifies them)                             │
│  4. Close teammates (after manager approves timing)              │
│  5. Broader team (usually manager announces)                     │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

NEVER tell teammates before your manager. This is a serious
breach of professional etiquette and damages trust.
```

### The Conversation Script

The resignation conversation should be short, direct, and positive. Lead with your decision, state your last day, and stop talking. Let your manager respond before you fill the silence. Most managers have seen this before and will guide the rest of the conversation.

```
THE RESIGNATION CONVERSATION:

OPENING:
"I wanted to let you know that I've decided to accept
an opportunity at another company. My last day will
be [date, typically 2 weeks out]."

Stop. Let them respond.

COMMON RESPONSES AND HOW TO HANDLE:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  "I'm disappointed but understand"                               │
│  → "Thank you for understanding. I've learned a lot here         │
│     and appreciate all the opportunities."                       │
│                                                                  │
│  "Why are you leaving?"                                          │
│  → Keep it positive and vague:                                   │
│     "It's an opportunity I couldn't pass up"                     │
│     "It aligns well with my long-term career goals"              │
│     DO NOT badmouth or list complaints                           │
│                                                                  │
│  "Where are you going?"                                          │
│  → Your choice to share or not:                                  │
│     "I'll be joining [Company]" (if comfortable)                 │
│     "I prefer to wait until I've started" (also fine)            │
│                                                                  │
│  "What would it take to keep you?"                               │
│  → See counter offer section below                               │
│                                                                  │
│  "Can you stay longer than 2 weeks?"                             │
│  → Consider if reasonable and won't affect new job               │
│     "I can discuss extending to [X] if that helps"               │
│     But don't let them guilt you indefinitely                    │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Emotional Preparation

Resigning triggers real emotions on both sides. Guilt about leaving your team, anxiety about the conversation, and second-guessing your decision are all normal. Naming these feelings ahead of time helps you stay composed when the moment arrives.

```
MANAGING THE EMOTIONAL ASPECTS:

FOR YOU:
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  FEELINGS YOU MIGHT HAVE:                                         │
│  ├── Guilt (especially if team is understaffed)                   │
│  ├── Relief                                                       │
│  ├── Excitement about new opportunity                             │
│  ├── Sadness about leaving colleagues                             │
│  ├── Anxiety about the conversation                               │
│  └── Second-guessing your decision                                │
│                                                                   │
│  REMEMBER:                                                        │
│  ├── You are not abandoning anyone                                │
│  ├── Companies survive employee departures                        │
│  ├── You owe your career to yourself, not your employer           │
│  ├── Your teammates would do the same                             │
│  └── This is a normal business transaction                        │
│                                                                   │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

FOR YOUR MANAGER:
They may have various reactions:
- Disappointment (lost a good employee)
- Frustration (now have to backfill)
- Happiness for you (good managers)
- Anger (rare, unprofessional)

Stay calm regardless of their reaction. Be professional.
Don't get pulled into arguments or emotional discussions.
```

### What NOT to Say

The resignation conversation is not the time for feedback, complaints, or honesty about your frustrations. Everything you say will be remembered and may be repeated. Keep it positive and brief. Save substantive feedback for the exit interview, if you choose to give any at all.

```
THINGS TO AVOID:

✗ "I've been looking for months"
  (Makes them feel blindsided, questions your engagement)

✗ "The new job pays way more"
  (Invites counter offer drama, seems mercenary)

✗ "I can't stand working with [colleague]"
  (Gossip spreads, burns bridges)

✗ "The project I'm on is going to fail anyway"
  (Unprofessional, damages your reputation)

✗ "Management here is terrible"
  (Even if true, not the time or place)

✗ "I've told some teammates already"
  (Breach of protocol, undermines manager)

✗ Extensive criticism or "exit interview" in the moment
  (Save it for the actual exit interview, if at all)

KEEP IT:
✓ Brief
✓ Positive
✓ Professional
✓ Forward-looking
```

### Junior → FAANG: The Resignation Conversation as a Professional Benchmark

The resignation conversation is one of the few professional situations where the right behavior is completely defined and entirely learnable. Have the conversation with your manager directly, in a scheduled private meeting, not in a message or email. Express genuine gratitude for your time at the company. State clearly that you have accepted a new opportunity. Provide your intended last day. Do not volunteer details about where you are going unless directly asked, and even then you are entitled to be brief.

The conversation should take five to ten minutes. A longer conversation usually means you are over-explaining, which can come across as apologetic or defensive. A shorter conversation can feel abrupt. Practice the opening two or three sentences out loud before the meeting so that you can deliver them calmly regardless of how anxious you feel.

The most common early-career mistake in the resignation conversation: leading with the reasons you are leaving rather than with gratitude and clarity about the decision. If your manager asks why you are leaving, give a brief, forward-looking answer about what the new opportunity offers, not a list of grievances about what was wrong with the current role.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Resignation Conversation as a Leadership Transition Moment

At senior level, the resignation conversation is the beginning of a leadership transition, not just a procedural notification. Your manager will be thinking immediately about the organizational impact: who takes on your responsibilities, what projects are at risk, and how to communicate the departure to the broader team. Coming to the conversation prepared to discuss these questions demonstrates the organizational awareness expected at senior levels.

Offer to work collaboratively on the transition plan. Suggest a follow-up meeting specifically to discuss the knowledge transfer and succession plan. This signals that you are not just fulfilling an obligation but genuinely invested in leaving the organization in a stable state. It is also genuinely useful for your manager, who has to solve these problems regardless.

The senior-level resignation conversation should explicitly confirm your notice period and any flexibility around it. Two weeks is a minimum at most companies, but senior engineers often offer and negotiate four to six weeks for complex transitions. Being willing to stay longer (within reason) if it genuinely helps the organization is a significant reputational signal.
