# Chapter 96C: Resignation & Transition

*How you leave a job is as important as how you got it. The engineering world is smaller than you think, and reputations travel faster than resumes.*

Leaving a job is a professional skill that most engineers only practice a handful of times in their careers, yet it carries consequences - financial, reputational, and emotional - that can reverberate for years. A poorly executed resignation can forfeit unvested equity worth tens of thousands of dollars, burn a reference who would otherwise have championed your next opportunity, and create legal exposure if sensitive materials are handled carelessly in the transition. Conversely, an engineer who resigns with grace, executes a thorough handoff, and leaves on genuinely good terms with their team often walks away with a network of advocates that materially accelerates every subsequent career move.

The financial dimension alone makes this worth careful planning. In modern tech compensation structures - particularly for Go engineers at infrastructure companies where equity grants are substantial - the timing of a resignation can mean the difference between leaving $50,000 to $200,000 on the table or capturing it cleanly. Quarterly RSU vesting cliffs, annual bonus payment dates, performance review cycles, and stock option exercise windows all need to be mapped before you make the conversation with your manager. Engineers who resign two weeks before a quarterly vest or the day after bonus eligibility is cut off routinely leave money behind that a single spreadsheet and calendar review would have protected.

This chapter gives you a complete, sequenced guide to resigning professionally. It starts with the financial and logistical preparation that must happen before any conversation takes place, walks through how to have the resignation conversation itself with different manager personality types, covers how to write a resignation letter that is professional without being cold, and addresses the counter-offer situation in depth - because nearly every strong engineer will receive one, and nearly every engineer who accepts one eventually regrets it. The final sections cover executing an exemplary notice period and maintaining the relationships that will define your professional network for the next decade.

**What you'll learn in this chapter:**
- The financial checklist every engineer must complete before resigning (equity, bonuses, benefits)
- How to time your resignation to maximize vested compensation
- Scripts and strategies for the resignation conversation across different management styles
- Writing a resignation letter that is professional, clean, and preserves relationships
- How to evaluate counter-offers honestly and why most accepted counter-offers fail within 12 months
- Executing a high-quality knowledge transfer and handoff during your notice period
- What is and is not legally permissible to take with you when you leave
- Maintaining professional relationships with former colleagues and managers post-departure

**Why this matters at scale:**
At companies like Google and Meta, where senior engineers routinely hold RSU grants in the hundreds of thousands of dollars on multi-year vesting schedules, the financial cost of an unplanned resignation can be catastrophic - internal data at several large companies shows that a meaningful percentage of voluntary departures happen within 30 days of a major vesting event, almost certainly due to poor planning rather than intent to leave money behind.

### Junior → FAANG: Resignation Fundamentals and Why Professionalism Here Compounds

At early career, every resignation is an opportunity to practice a professional behavior that you will need to execute well multiple times over a 30-year career. The engineer who resigns professionally at their first job builds a pattern that compounds: the manager who received a graceful resignation will write a strong reference. The teammates who benefited from a thorough handoff will remain allies in a small industry. The company you left respectfully may hire you back at a senior level in the future.

The most important early-career principle: do not tell colleagues about your resignation before telling your manager. This is a basic professional norm that is violated constantly by early-career engineers who are excited about their new opportunity and want to share the news. If your manager hears about your resignation from a colleague before hearing it from you, the relationship is damaged in a way that is difficult to repair, and the reference you could have had becomes uncertain.

Practical preparation: before the resignation conversation, confirm in writing with your new employer (start date, compensation, reporting structure) so that you are giving notice based on a confirmed offer, not a verbal one. Also review your current employment agreement for any notice period requirements, non-compete clauses, or IP assignment terms that affect what you can work on at your new employer.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Resignation Governance and the Network Capital Perspective

At senior level, resignation is a governance event with organizational consequences that extend well beyond the immediate team. A staff engineer's departure triggers succession planning questions, project ownership transitions, and sometimes organizational re-structuring. Managing these consequences thoughtfully is the difference between a senior engineer who leaves as an organizational leader and one who leaves as someone the company is relieved to see go.

The governance practice: before tendering resignation, have a clear plan for the knowledge transfer of every significant technical responsibility you hold. This includes design documents for systems you own, post-mortem insight for operational responsibilities, and explicit conversations with your manager about who should take on your most critical pending decisions. This level of transition planning takes time to execute well, which is one reason senior engineers often negotiate longer notice periods than the standard two weeks.

The network capital perspective: senior engineers have typically built relationships with people across the industry who represent significant future career optionality. How you leave each organization affects the density and quality of this network. A well-executed senior resignation that includes a genuine transition plan, warm departures with key colleagues, and an exit interview conducted in good faith typically results in a stronger professional network than the organization you are leaving, not a weaker one.
