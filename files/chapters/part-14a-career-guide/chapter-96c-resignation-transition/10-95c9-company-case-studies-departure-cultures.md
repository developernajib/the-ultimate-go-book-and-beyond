### Production Pitfalls: Resignation & Transition Mistakes

#### Pitfall 1: Resigning Before the New Job is Secure

**Real Incident Report**: A candidate received a verbal offer and immediately resigned. Two weeks later, the new company's background check found a discrepancy (a job duration was off by a month on their resume). The offer was rescinded. They were now unemployed with no job lined up.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Resigning on verbal offer
- Not waiting for background check completion
- Starting notice period too early
- Counting on offers that aren't finalized

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Resignation Timing Checklist

## Only resign when you have:
□ Written offer letter signed by company
□ Background check cleared
□ Start date confirmed
□ Any contingencies resolved

## Contingencies to wait for:
- Background check (criminal, employment verification)
- Drug screening (if required)
- Reference checks
- Offer letter review by your lawyer (for executive roles)

## Red flags to watch:
- "Start making plans, offer coming soon"
- "Background check is just a formality"
- "We'll send the letter after you resign"

## The safe order:
1. Receive written offer
2. Background check completes
3. Both parties sign
4. THEN give notice
```

**Key Insight**: Until the offer is fully signed and contingencies cleared, you have nothing. Never burn bridges when transitioning between roles.

#### Pitfall 2: Telling Colleagues Before Your Manager

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer told a close friend on the team about their new job. That friend mentioned it to another colleague. Within hours, it reached the manager - who heard from someone else that their report was leaving. The manager felt disrespected and blindsided. The remaining notice period was awkward.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Telling anyone before your manager
- Sharing plans on social media
- Assuming "just one person" is safe
- Underestimating how fast information travels

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Resignation Communication Order

## The sequence:
1. Direct manager (first, private conversation)
2. Skip-level if appropriate (same day)
3. HR (for formal process)
4. Close team members (after manager approves)
5. Broader team (team meeting or email)
6. Public announcement (LinkedIn, last day)

## Telling your manager:
"I wanted you to be the first to know - I've accepted
an offer at [Company] and will be leaving. My last
day will be [date]. I want to ensure a smooth
transition."

## If your manager is out:
- Wait if possible
- If urgent, tell skip-level first
- Don't tell peers instead

## Golden rule:
Your manager should never hear about your
resignation from someone else.
```

**Key Insight**: The tech industry is small. How you leave is remembered. Your manager hearing secondhand permanently damages that relationship.

#### Pitfall 3: Burning Bridges During Notice Period

**Real Incident Report**: During their two-week notice, an engineer stopped caring: missed meetings, delivered sloppy work, made negative comments about the company. Years later, a former colleague was the hiring manager at their dream company. The reference? "Great engineer, but checked out during their last weeks. I'd be cautious."

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Short-timer syndrome
- Airing grievances during notice
- Doing minimum work
- Being negative about the company

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Finishing Strong

## Notice period goals:
- Leave projects in good state
- Transfer knowledge thoroughly
- Maintain relationships
- Build your reputation

## Transition checklist:
□ Document all ongoing work
□ Create handoff guides for your projects
□ Train replacement or backup
□ Complete any critical work
□ Offer to be available for questions after leaving

## What NOT to do:
- Air grievances ("finally I can say...")
- Stop trying ("I'm leaving anyway")
- Take credit for others' work
- Bad-mouth the company
- Recruit colleagues aggressively

## The reality:
Your last impression is lasting
Former colleagues become future references
The industry is smaller than you think
```

**Key Insight**: Your reputation follows you. The two weeks of notice can define how you're remembered at a company for years.

#### Pitfall 4: Accepting a Counter Offer

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer was ready to leave for 20% more pay. Their current company counter-offered to match. They stayed. Within 6 months, they were passed over for promotion ("not committed to the team"), given less interesting work, and eventually laid off in the next round. The relationship never recovered.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Accepting counter offers without deep consideration
- Believing the issues that made you look will change
- Not understanding the statistics
- Using resignation as negotiation tactic

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Counter Offer Decision Framework

## When to potentially accept:
✓ Your ONLY issue was compensation
✓ You'd be genuinely happy staying 2+ years
✓ Your manager's response was supportive, not punishing
✓ The counter addresses root causes, not just money

## When to decline:
✗ You were leaving for growth/culture/opportunity
✗ Counter only matches money, not other concerns
✗ Manager seemed angry or used guilt
✗ You've already mentally left
✗ Trust is broken

## The statistics:
- 50%+ of counter-offer accepters leave within 12 months
- Many are viewed as "flight risks" afterward
- Root problems rarely change just because you tried to leave

## The question to ask:
"Why wasn't this offered before I resigned?"
```

**Key Insight**: Counter offers are Band-Aids. If you were valued appropriately, you wouldn't have needed to resign to get fair treatment.

#### Pitfall 5: Poor Knowledge Transfer

**Real Incident Report**: A senior engineer left with two weeks notice and zero documentation. They owned three critical systems. For six months after they left, the team struggled with outages they didn't know how to fix. The former engineer's reputation was "brilliant but left us in a terrible spot."

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Not documenting during notice period
- Assuming your knowledge is obvious
- Leaving without training backup
- Hoarding information to feel irreplaceable

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Knowledge Transfer Template

## For each system/project you own:
□ Architecture overview (diagram)
□ How to deploy
□ How to debug common issues
□ Who to contact for dependencies
□ Known issues and workarounds
□ Future work planned

## For each process you own:
□ Step-by-step instructions
□ Frequency and triggers
□ What can go wrong and how to fix
□ Who needs to be informed

## Transition document:
- List of everything you own
- Priority order for handoff
- Recommended new owners
- Timeline for each handoff

## Offer ongoing availability:
"Feel free to email me questions for the first
few weeks. I want to make sure the team is set up
for success."
```

**Key Insight**: How you leave affects your legacy. Thorough handoff is the professional standard - and makes you someone people want to hire again.

#### Quick Reference: Resignation Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|--------------|---------|-----|
| Premature resignation | Offer rescinded, now unemployed | Wait for written offer + background clear |
| Manager last to know | Awkward, damaged relationship | Tell manager first, always |
| Checked out during notice | "Left us in bad shape" reputation | Finish strong, document everything |
| Accepted counter | Left anyway within 12 months | Decline unless root cause is only money |
| No knowledge transfer | Team struggled for months | Create thorough handoff documentation |
| Burning bridges | Negative reference years later | Stay professional, never vent |

### Transition Synthesis: Departure Protocol Checklist

How you leave a job is remembered as long as how you performed in it, the engineering industry is small, and the senior engineers and managers who witnessed your exit will be references, interviewers, and colleagues again. The rules below protect two things simultaneously: your professional reputation and your financial interests, since mis-timed resignations can forfeit unvested equity, bonuses, or severance that were days or weeks away from vesting.

```
RESIGNATION GOLDEN RULES:

1. Don't resign until new job is confirmed (background cleared)
2. Tell your manager first, always
3. Be professional regardless of circumstances
4. Create a thorough transition plan
5. Keep the resignation letter positive and brief
6. Be skeptical of counter offers
7. Finish strong - no short-timer syndrome
8. Maintain relationships - industry is small
9. Document what you're legally entitled to keep
10. Leave gracefully - last impressions matter

TIMING CHECKLIST:
□ Check vest dates before committing to start date
□ Consider bonus timing
□ Background check cleared
□ Start date confirmed in writing

PROFESSIONALISM CHECKLIST:
□ Manager notified first
□ Resignation letter sent same day
□ Transition plan created
□ Knowledge transfer scheduled
□ Farewell communication planned
```

### Case Study Discussion Exercises

1. **Pre-Resignation Audit**: Review your current equity vesting schedule and bonus timing. When is the optimal time to resign?

2. **Transition Plan Draft**: Write a transition document for your current role. What projects are you on? Who would take them over?

3. **Resignation Script Practice**: Write out and practice your resignation conversation. Have a friend role-play as your manager with different reactions.

4. **Exit Interview Prep**: What are 2-3 pieces of constructive feedback you would share? Frame them diplomatically.

5. **Network Audit**: How many colleagues do you have personal contact information for? Start exchanging before you need to.

### Junior → FAANG: Learning From Company Departure Cultures

The departure culture at a company tells you something important about its engineering culture overall. Google's strong alumni network reflects a culture that values long-term professional relationships. Amazon's sometimes abrupt departure process reflects a culture that prioritizes security and business continuity. Meta's warm departure culture reflects a high-velocity environment that has learned to manage frequent transitions without organizational disruption.

Understanding the departure culture of your target company before you join helps you plan your eventual transition from the beginning. If you are joining a company known for abrupt access termination, backing up personal contacts early in your tenure is part of your professional hygiene, not just a pre-resignation task.

### Senior @ FAANG: Departure Culture as an Organizational Health Signal

At senior level, the case studies in this section are relevant not just as personal guidance but as organizational intelligence. How an organization treats departing engineers reveals its operating values in a way that onboarding programs and engineering blogs do not. Organizations that maintain strong alumni networks and facilitate graceful departures tend to have stronger engineering cultures overall: they recognize that engineers are professionals with long careers, not resources to be extracted and discarded.

The governance implication: if you are ever in a position to influence your organization's departure culture (as an engineering manager, HR partner, or organizational leader), the investment in alumni network support and graceful transition processes produces measurable returns in employer reputation, future hiring quality, and the probability that departed engineers return as customers, partners, or re-hires at senior levels.
