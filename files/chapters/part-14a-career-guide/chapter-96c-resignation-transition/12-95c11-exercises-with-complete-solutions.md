### Exercise 1: Optimal Resignation Timing Analysis

**Scenario**: You're at a FAANG company with the following compensation structure:
- RSU vesting: Quarterly (Feb 15, May 15, Aug 15, Nov 15)
- Annual bonus: Paid March 15 for previous year
- Next RSU vest: Feb 15 ($30,000)
- Expected bonus: $40,000 (paid March 15)

You have a new job offer with a flexible start date. When should you resign, and what start date should you negotiate?

**Complete Solution**:

```markdown
# Resignation Timing Analysis

## Key Dates:
- Today: January 10
- Next RSU vest: February 15 ($30,000)
- Bonus payment: March 15 ($40,000)
- Total at stake: $70,000

## Analysis:

### Option A: Start February 1 (Resign January 18)
- RSU vest: Forfeit $30,000 (not employed Feb 15)
- Bonus: Forfeit $40,000 (not employed March 15)
- Total loss: $70,000

### Option B: Start March 1 (Resign February 15)
- RSU vest: Receive $30,000 (employed on vest date)
- Bonus: Forfeit $40,000 (not employed March 15)
- Total loss: $40,000
- Gain vs Option A: $30,000

### Option C: Start March 20 (Resign March 6)
- RSU vest: Receive $30,000
- Bonus: Receive $40,000 (employed on payment date)
- Total loss: $0
- Gain vs Option A: $70,000

## Recommendation: Option C

### Negotiation Script with New Employer:

"I'm excited to accept this offer. I'd like to discuss the start date.
My current equity vests February 15, and my annual bonus pays out
March 15. Would it be possible to start March 20 instead of March 1?

This two-and-a-half-week delay would allow me to capture $70,000 in
already-earned compensation. I want to start on the right foot with
your team, fully focused rather than thinking about money I left
behind."

### Risk Considerations:

1. **New employer urgency**: If they can't wait, calculate if the
   new job's sign-on or higher salary compensates for the loss.

   Example: If new job offers $10K sign-on and $30K higher base,
   starting Feb 1 might still make sense:
   - Loss: $70K
   - Gain: $10K + $30K extra income over first year = $40K
   - Net: -$30K, but with career benefits

2. **Current employer reaction**: Some companies may push back
   on staying until bonus payment if they know you're leaving.
   Don't tell them until 2 weeks before planned last day.

3. **Clawback clauses**: Check if your current employer has a
   bonus clawback (e.g., "must repay if leave within 90 days").
   This affects whether to wait.

## Timeline:

Week of Jan 10: Accept new offer, negotiate March 20 start
Feb 15: RSU vests (continue working normally)
Mar 3: Give notice (2 weeks to March 17)
Mar 15: Bonus pays out
Mar 17: Last day at current company
Mar 20: Start new job

## Document:

To new employer:
- Request March 20 start in writing
- Confirm offer remains valid
- Get background check done before giving notice

From current employer (before resigning):
- Screenshot vesting schedule
- Note bonus payment date and requirements
- Save copies of offer letter compensation details
```

### Exercise 2: Difficult Manager Resignation Role-Play

**Scenario**: Your manager has a reputation for reacting poorly to resignations. They've been known to guilt-trip people, become cold and distant, or even push people out early. You've decided to leave. Write out your conversation script and contingency responses.

**Complete Solution**:

```markdown
# Resignation Conversation Script

## Setting Up the Meeting:
Message to manager: "Hi [Manager], do you have 15 minutes for a
quick 1:1 today or tomorrow? Something I'd like to discuss."

Don't say what it's about - they'll guess, but let them.

---

## Opening Statement (Memorize This):

"[Manager], I want you to hear this from me directly before anyone
else. I've made the decision to accept an opportunity at another
company. My last day will be [date, 2 weeks out].

I want you to know this wasn't an easy decision. I've learned a lot
working with you and this team. I'm committed to making the
transition as smooth as possible."

Then STOP. Let them respond.

---

## Contingency Responses:

### If they react with GUILT:
Manager: "You're really going to leave us in the middle of [project]?
The team is counting on you."

You: "I understand the timing isn't ideal. That's why I want to
make sure these next two weeks are as productive as possible.
I've already started documenting my projects and can train [person]
on [critical area]. What would be most valuable to focus on?"

Key: Acknowledge, redirect to transition, don't apologize excessively.

### If they become COLD/DISTANT:
Manager: "Fine. Put in your notice with HR."

You: "I will. I also wanted to offer - I'm happy to extend to three
weeks if that helps with transition. And I'm committed to thorough
documentation and knowledge transfer regardless."

Key: Stay warm even if they're cold. Your professionalism shows.

### If they try to COUNTER OFFER:
Manager: "What would it take to keep you? We can talk about
compensation, role changes, whatever you need."

You: "I really appreciate that, and it means a lot that you value
my contributions. I've thought carefully about this decision.
It's not about compensation - it's about [career direction]. My
decision is made, but I'm grateful for the offer."

Key: Be firm. Don't negotiate or waver.

### If they ask WHERE you're going:
Manager: "Where are you going?"

Option A (If you're comfortable sharing):
"I'll be joining [Company] as a [title]."

Option B (If you're not comfortable):
"I'd prefer to wait until I've started to share that. I hope
you understand."

Key: Both are acceptable. Share only if genuinely comfortable.

### If they push you out EARLY:
Manager: "Actually, we don't need you for two weeks. Today
is your last day."

You: "I understand. I'd like to confirm a few things: I assume
I'll be paid through [date I offered], I can collect my personal
belongings, and HR will process my benefits information. Is there
anything you need from me before I leave today?"

Key: Stay calm, protect your interests, don't argue.

### If they ask for LONGER notice:
Manager: "Two weeks isn't enough. Can you stay until [longer date]?"

You: "I appreciate you wanting more time. I can potentially extend
to [one week more at most] if that helps, but my new start date is
confirmed. What's most critical to cover in the time we have?"

Key: Don't let guilt push you past what works for your new job.

---

## Closing the Conversation:

"I'll send you a formal resignation email after this conversation.
I'll also draft a transition document today and share it with you
tomorrow so we can discuss priorities. Thank you again for
everything."

---

## After the Conversation:

1. Send resignation email within 2 hours
2. Start transition document immediately
3. Tell no one else until manager says it's okay to share
4. Document the conversation if anything unusual happened
5. Continue normal work - don't let their reaction affect you

---

## If Things Go VERY Badly:

Document everything. If manager:
- Threatens your reference
- Says anything discriminatory
- Attempts to sabotage your remaining time
- Withholds pay or benefits inappropriately

→ Involve HR professionally
→ Keep all communication in writing
→ Consult employment lawyer if serious

Most resignations are fine. But know your rights.
```

### Junior → FAANG: Using Exercises to Practice Before You Need Them

The resignation exercises are most useful when done well before you are in a real departure situation. Writing your resignation letter template, practicing the resignation conversation out loud, and drafting your transition plan for your current role as it exists today gives you a ready toolkit that removes the cognitive load of creating these artifacts under deadline pressure.

The network audit exercise is worth doing now, regardless of whether you are planning a departure. Knowing which of your current colleagues you have personal contact information for tells you where your professional relationship maintenance has gaps. Start exchanging contact information and connecting on LinkedIn with colleagues whose relationship you value, as a routine practice rather than a last-minute departure task.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Departure Simulation and the Long-Term Relationship Audit

At senior level, extend the exercises to include a six-month post-departure relationship maintenance plan. After leaving an organization, the professional relationships that persist are the ones that were explicitly maintained: a quarterly check-in email, a LinkedIn engagement habit, or a recurring virtual coffee with two or three key former colleagues. Plan this explicitly before you leave rather than hoping it happens organically.

The transition document exercise at senior level should be treated as a real deliverable. Create the full transition document for your current role as it exists today, covering all the dimensions described in the pre-resignation preparation section. This document has two uses: it is your actual transition document when you eventually leave this role, and it identifies the knowledge transfer gaps in your team's current documentation that you can address proactively.

## Summary

### Key Takeaways
- **Pre-Resignation Sequencing**: Never resign on a verbal offer; wait for written confirmation and cleared background checks, and carefully align departure timing with RSU vesting and bonus payment schedules.
- **Dignified Communication**: Always notify your direct manager first in a private, scheduled conversation, followed immediately by a concise, professional resignation letter that avoids grievances.
- **Flawless Transition & Network Capital**: Treat your notice period as a critical deliverable sprint by documenting systems, training successors, and nurturing long-term relationships that define your industry network.
