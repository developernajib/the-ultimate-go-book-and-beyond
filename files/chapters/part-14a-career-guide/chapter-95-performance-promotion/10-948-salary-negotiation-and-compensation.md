## 95.9 Salary Negotiation and Compensation

### Understanding Tech Compensation

Tech compensation is intentionally complex, companies structure it across multiple components so that the headline number in an offer letter rarely tells the full story. Base salary is the only component that is guaranteed and compounds over time (future raises and bonuses are often calculated as a percentage of it), while equity and bonuses carry real risk: unvested RSUs disappear if you leave early, and bonuses are reduced or eliminated if the company misses targets. Understanding total compensation (TC) as the sum of all components lets you make apples-to-apples comparisons between offers and identify which components to prioritize in negotiation.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│             TECH COMPENSATION COMPONENTS                         │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  BASE SALARY:                                                    │
│  ├── Fixed annual amount                                         │
│  ├── Paid in regular paychecks                                   │
│  └── Usually negotiable at hire, harder to change later          │
│                                                                  │
│  ANNUAL BONUS:                                                   │
│  ├── % of base salary (typically 10-30%)                         │
│  ├── Based on individual + company performance                   │
│  └── Not guaranteed (depends on metrics)                         │
│                                                                  │
│  EQUITY (RSUs/Options):                                          │
│  ├── RSUs: Shares given over vesting period (4 years typical)    │
│  ├── Options: Right to buy at set price                          │
│  └── Value fluctuates with stock price                           │
│                                                                  │
│  SIGNING BONUS:                                                  │
│  ├── One-time payment at start                                   │
│  └── Often used to offset unvested equity from current job       │
│                                                                  │
│  OTHER:                                                          │
│  ├── 401k matching                                               │
│  ├── Health insurance value                                      │
│  ├── PTO/sabbatical policies                                     │
│  └── Perks (food, transit, etc.)                                 │
│                                                                  │
│  TOTAL COMPENSATION (TC):                                        │
│  TC = Base + Expected Bonus + Annual Equity Value                │
│                                                                  │
│  Example L5 at Big Tech:                                         │
│  Base: $200,000 + Bonus: $30,000 + Equity: $70,000 = $300,000    │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Negotiation Strategies

```markdown
# SALARY NEGOTIATION PLAYBOOK

## PREPARATION (Before the Offer)

### Research Your Market Value:
1. Levels.fyi - Crowdsourced compensation data
2. Glassdoor - Company-specific salary ranges
3. Blind - Anonymous professional network
4. Your network - Ask trusted connections (carefully)

### Know Your Numbers:
- Target: What you'd be thrilled with
- Walk-away: Below this, you decline
- Expect: Realistic outcome based on research

### Example:
For L5 at Big Tech Company:
- Research shows: $280K-$350K TC
- Target: $340K
- Walk-away: $290K
- Expect: $310-$320K

## THE NEGOTIATION

### Rule 1: Never Give the First Number
When asked "What are your salary expectations?":

DON'T SAY: "I'm looking for $300,000"
(You might have gotten $350,000)

DO SAY: "I'd like to learn more about the role and understand
the full compensation package before discussing numbers. What's
the budgeted range for this position?"

### Rule 2: Negotiate Total Compensation, Not Just Salary
If base salary is capped, explore:
- Signing bonus (covers gap for first year)
- Additional equity (increases TC long-term)
- Earlier vesting schedule
- Higher annual bonus target

### Rule 3: Let Silence Work for You
After receiving an offer:
"Thank you for the offer. I'm very interested in the role.
I'd like to take a day or two to review the full package.
When do you need a decision by?"

NEVER accept immediately. Even if it's great - always pause.

### Rule 4: Express Enthusiasm While Negotiating
"I'm very excited about this opportunity and confident I can
make a strong impact. I'd like to discuss the compensation
to make sure we can come to an agreement."

### The Counter-Offer Script:

"Thank you for the offer of [X base, Y equity]. I'm excited
about the role and the team. Based on my research and the
value I'll bring, I was hoping for [Target TC]. Is there
flexibility in [base/equity/signing bonus]?"

### If They Say No:

"I understand there may be constraints. Are there other
components we could adjust? Perhaps a signing bonus to
bridge the gap, or additional equity?"

### When to Walk Away:
- Offer is below your walk-away number AND they won't budge
- Process reveals red flags (dishonesty, disrespect)
- Better opportunity elsewhere with similar fit

### When NOT to Negotiate:
- Early career (< 2 years): small negotiation upside, risk seeming difficult
- Very small companies with transparent bands
- Government/non-profit with fixed scales

## POST-NEGOTIATION

### Get It In Writing:
"Thank you for working with me on this. Could you please
send the updated offer letter reflecting our discussion?"

### Confirm All Details:
- Base salary
- Bonus target percentage
- Equity grant (number of shares AND strike price if options)
- Vesting schedule
- Start date
- Any special agreements (remote work, etc.)
```

### Internal Raise and Promotion Compensation

```markdown
# GETTING PAID FOR INTERNAL PROMOTION

## THE PROBLEM:
Internal promotions often come with smaller raises than
external offers would command. Companies rely on inertia.

## TYPICAL INTERNAL RAISE RANGES:
- L4 → L5: 8-15% base increase
- L5 → L6: 10-20% base increase
- Equity refresh: Often underwhelming

## GETTING FAIR COMPENSATION:

### Step 1: Know Market Rates
Research what external L6 candidates receive.
You should be paid at least the bottom of that range.

### Step 2: Have the Conversation
"I'm thrilled about the promotion to L6. I want to make
sure my compensation reflects the new level. Looking at
market data, L6s typically receive [range]. Where does
this offer fall in that range?"

### Step 3: Be Prepared to Make the Case
"I understand internal moves may have constraints, but
I'd like to be paid fairly for L6 work. I'm already
doing the job, and comparable external candidates
receive [amount]. Can we discuss getting closer to
that range?"

### Step 4: Know Your Alternatives
If they won't budge significantly:
- Ask for signing bonus equivalent
- Request larger equity refresh
- Get commitment for review at 6 months
- Consider external opportunities

### The External Offer Card:
Use CAREFULLY. Only if you'd actually leave.

"I have an external offer at [amount] but I'd prefer
to stay. Can we match or come close?"

Risk: They may call your bluff or resent the tactic.
Only use if you have a real offer and would leave.

## RETENTION COUNTER-OFFERS:
If you're leaving and they counter:

Consider:
- Why didn't they pay you fairly before?
- Will they resent you?
- Will they watch for you to leave?
- Statistics show 50%+ of people who accept
  counter-offers leave within 18 months anyway

Usually better to leave gracefully.
```

### Junior → FAANG: Compensation Fundamentals and the Internal Equity Trap

Early-career engineers at FAANG companies are often surprised to discover that their total compensation is dominated by equity rather than base salary at the mid-level and above. Understanding the structure before negotiating an offer prevents the common mistake of optimizing for base salary when the total compensation difference between offers is primarily in RSU grants and vesting schedules.

The internal equity trap is a specific compensation risk at early career: FAANG companies calibrate annual compensation increases to internal equity benchmarks rather than market rates. If you joined below market rate and did not negotiate aggressively, internal equity constraints may prevent your annual increases from closing the gap. The most reliable way to reset your compensation to market rate is an external offer from a competing company, which gives you negotiation leverage that internal review cycles cannot provide.

Research your market rate using levels.fyi quarterly, not annually. The tech compensation market moves faster than annual review cycles, and knowing you are significantly below market for your level and tenure is actionable information even when you are not actively job searching.

### Senior @ FAANG: Compensation Architecture at L6 and the Equity Optimization Framework

At senior level, compensation is primarily a function of equity composition and vesting timing rather than base salary. The difference between a well-negotiated and poorly-negotiated L6 offer at a major FAANG company can be hundreds of thousands of dollars in total compensation over four years, primarily driven by RSU grant size, vesting cliff structure, and refresh grant timing.

The governance framework for senior compensation management: understand your total compensation trajectory over four years, not just year one. Evaluate the cliff structure (most FAANG RSU grants vest over four years with a one-year cliff), the refresh grant policy (how large are annual refresh grants and how are they determined), and the equity value at multiple scenarios for the company's stock price. An offer with a large upfront grant but no refresh grants may be less valuable than an offer with a smaller upfront grant and generous annual refreshes, depending on tenure plans.

For senior engineers managing multiple offers: the negotiation is not just about matching numbers. It is about understanding which company's compensation structure aligns best with your planned tenure, your risk tolerance for equity versus guaranteed cash, and your assessment of the company's long-term equity value trajectory.
