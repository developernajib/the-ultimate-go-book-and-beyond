## 95.8 Peer Feedback Strategies

### Requesting Effective Peer Feedback

Vague feedback requests,"let me know what you think of my work", produce vague responses that carry little weight in a promotion packet. The most useful peer feedback is generated by asking specific, behavioral questions that prompt reviewers to recall concrete incidents rather than offer general impressions. The template below provides that structure, framing questions around scope, influence, and technical judgment so reviewers naturally produce promotion-ready evidence rather than generic praise.

```markdown
# REQUESTING PEER FEEDBACK

## WHEN TO ASK:
- 2-4 weeks before review cycle
- After completing major project together
- When you've had significant collaboration

## WHO TO ASK:
1. Direct collaborators (worked closely with you)
2. Cross-team partners (saw your influence)
3. Mentees (speak to your leadership)
4. Senior engineers (credibility with committee)

## HOW TO ASK:

### The Request Email:

Subject: Request for Peer Feedback - [Your Name] Review Cycle

Hi [Name],

As part of the upcoming review cycle, I'm collecting peer feedback.
Given our collaboration on [specific project/area], I'd value your
perspective on my work.

A few specific things I'd appreciate feedback on:
1. [Specific contribution you made]
2. [Specific skill you're trying to demonstrate]
3. [Specific area you're working to improve]

If you're willing, please provide 2-3 paragraphs covering:
- What we worked on together
- What went well / what I did effectively
- Areas where I could improve

Timeline: If possible, by [date] would be helpful.

No obligation - I understand everyone's busy. Thank you for considering!

Best,
[Your Name]

---

## WHAT MAKES GREAT PEER FEEDBACK:

### Weak Feedback (not useful):
"Alex is a great engineer and fun to work with.
I enjoyed collaborating with them on projects."

### Strong Feedback (compelling):
"During the payment service migration, Alex demonstrated
exceptional technical judgment. When we discovered the
database schema wouldn't support our target scale, Alex
led a 3-day deep dive with our team, ultimately designing
an approach that reduced migration risk by 80%. They
explained complex concepts clearly in our design review,
earning buy-in from skeptical stakeholders. Their code
review of my work caught a critical race condition that
would have caused production issues.

One area for growth: Alex occasionally takes on too much
personally rather than delegating. I've encouraged them
to give more tasks to junior engineers to grow the team's
capacity."

### The Difference:
- Specific situations and examples
- Concrete outcomes and impact
- Evidence of behaviors, not just traits
- Honest growth areas (shows authenticity)
```

### Writing Great Peer Feedback for Others

```markdown
# WRITING EFFECTIVE PEER FEEDBACK

## STRUCTURE (STAR+I):

### Situation: Context for the collaboration
"During Q3, I partnered with [Name] on the API redesign project."

### Task: What needed to be done
"The project required coordinating across 3 teams with conflicting
priorities and a tight 6-week deadline."

### Action: What they specifically did
"[Name] facilitated weekly syncs, identified the critical path,
and proposed a phased approach that addressed each team's concerns.
When we hit a blocker with the Auth team, they built a proof-of-
concept that demonstrated our approach wouldn't increase their load."

### Result: Quantified outcome
"We delivered on time, and the new API reduced integration effort
for partners by 60%. The project is now cited as an example of
effective cross-team collaboration."

### Impact: Why it mattered
"[Name]'s leadership was instrumental. Without their facilitation,
this project would likely have slipped by 2-3 months based on
previous similar efforts."

## TIPS:

1. BE SPECIFIC: "Great engineer" doesn't help. Specific examples do.

2. QUANTIFY: Numbers are memorable in calibration discussions.

3. SHOW, DON'T TELL: Don't say they're a leader - describe leadership.

4. INCLUDE GROWTH AREAS: 100% positive feedback seems insincere.
   Constructive feedback shows you took it seriously.

5. MATCH THE LEVEL: If they're seeking L6, address L6 behaviors:
   - Cross-team influence
   - Technical leadership
   - Mentoring others
   - Strategic thinking

6. BE HONEST: Committees can tell when feedback is exaggerated.
   Credibility matters.

## EXAMPLE LEVELS OF FEEDBACK:

### L4 → L5 (Mid to Senior):
Focus on: Independence, quality, mentoring beginnings
"[Name] independently owned the feature from design to launch.
Their code quality set a new standard for our team. They've
started mentoring our newest engineer with great early results."

### L5 → L6 (Senior to Staff):
Focus on: Multi-team impact, technical vision, growing others
"[Name] influenced our entire org's approach to testing. Their
RFC was adopted by 5 teams. They've grown two engineers to
senior level and their technical judgment is sought across teams."

### L6 → L7 (Staff to Senior Staff):
Focus on: Organization-wide impact, strategic influence, senior trust
"[Name] shaped our 3-year technical strategy. Directors actively
seek their opinion on critical decisions. Their work on platform
infrastructure saved the org $5M annually."
```

### Junior → FAANG: Building a Peer Feedback Network Before You Need It

Peer feedback at FAANG companies carries significant weight in the promotion calibration process. Engineers who receive specific, credible peer feedback about their impact advance faster than engineers whose peer feedback is generic ("works hard, good to collaborate with"). The difference between generic and specific peer feedback is entirely determined by the quality of the working relationships you built throughout the year.

The practical strategy: identify five to seven engineers across your team and adjacent teams whose work intersects with yours in meaningful ways. Actively help each of them with problems in your technical domain. Give thoughtful code review feedback on their pull requests. When you see a challenge in their work that you can help with, offer to pair on it. These interactions create the raw material that specific peer feedback requires: your reviewer needs concrete examples of your help, judgment, or technical insight in order to write a specific review.

One reciprocal practice that compounds over time: write strong peer reviews for others proactively, without waiting to be asked. Engineers who write specific, well-evidenced peer reviews for others consistently receive better peer reviews in return, because they have demonstrated what a useful review looks like and have built goodwill that motivates the recipient to invest effort in return.

### Senior @ FAANG: Peer Feedback at L6 and the Cross-Organizational Signal

At senior level, peer feedback serves a different function. You are not just collecting endorsements from collaborators: you are building a cross-organizational record of your technical judgment and influence. The most valuable peer feedback for a senior promotion case comes from engineers outside your immediate team who can speak to your cross-team impact, and from engineers at peer level who can attest to your senior-level judgment from a position of technical credibility.

The governance principle for senior peer feedback strategy: diversity of reviewers is more important than volume. Three specific, credible reviews from engineers across three different teams are worth more than seven generic reviews from your immediate teammates. Calibration committees at senior level specifically look for cross-team validation of claimed cross-team impact.

For staff promotion cases, peer feedback from other staff or principal engineers carries the highest weight. Building relationships with engineers at this level, across organizations if possible, gives you access to the most credible peer reviews available. These relationships are not built quickly: investing in them twelve to eighteen months before your target promotion cycle is the minimum necessary lead time.
