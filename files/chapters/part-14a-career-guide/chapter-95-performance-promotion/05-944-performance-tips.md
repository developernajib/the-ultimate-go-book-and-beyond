## 95.4 Performance Tips

### Managing Up Effectively

"Managing up" means actively shaping your manager's understanding of your work and priorities rather than waiting to be directed. Engineers who neglect this put their manager in a position of uncertainty, and uncertain managers default to caution during promotion decisions, headcount requests, and project assignments. By providing no-surprise status updates, framing blockers with proposed solutions, and proactively aligning on what success looks like, you reduce your manager's cognitive load and build the trust that translates directly into advocacy on your behalf.

```
YOUR MANAGER NEEDS:
• No surprises (escalate early)
• Clear status updates
• Problems with proposed solutions
• Your support for team goals

COMMUNICATION:
• Weekly written updates
• Flag risks early
• Be direct about blockers
• Advocate for yourself appropriately

1:1 AGENDA:
1. Progress on key priorities
2. Blockers and needs
3. Feedback (give and receive)
4. Career development
5. General topics
```

### Making Your Work Visible

Technical work that no one outside your immediate team can see has limited leverage on your promotion case, because calibration panels include managers who have never worked with you directly. Visibility is not self-promotion for its own sake, it is ensuring the organizational record reflects your actual contribution so that decision-makers have concrete evidence to reference. The key is to broadcast impact (what changed, why it mattered) rather than activity (what you did), and to do it consistently enough that colleagues naturally think of you when your scope of expertise comes up.

```
HOW TO SHARE WORK:

WITHIN TEAM:
• Clear PR descriptions
• Share wins in standup
• Write documentation

ACROSS TEAM:
• Tech talks
• Design reviews
• Cross-team collaboration

TO LEADERSHIP:
• Newsletter updates
• All-hands presentations
• Direct updates to skip-level

PRINCIPLES:
✓ Share impact, not activity
✓ Give credit to others
✓ Be consistent, not sporadic
✓ Let work speak first
```

### Junior → FAANG: Performance Tips That Build Compound Advantage

The performance tips in this section work best as compounding habits rather than one-time actions. Managing up effectively means establishing a communication pattern with your manager that sustains itself: weekly or biweekly one-on-ones where you share progress, blockers, and questions, supplemented by asynchronous communication about specific achievements or concerns as they arise.

The tip "let work speak first" at early career means: do not claim credit for impact you have not yet demonstrated. If you shipped a feature, wait for a week to see the metrics before writing "I improved X by Y." If the metrics are good, then communicate the outcome. If they are not, communicate what you are doing to investigate. Premature impact claims that turn out to be wrong damage credibility faster than anything else at FAANG companies.

The highest-value early-career performance tip that most guides underemphasize: become the person who resolves ambiguity rather than escalating it. When you encounter a situation where the requirements are unclear, the technical approach is uncertain, or the priority is unresolved, do the work to propose a resolution rather than asking your manager to decide. Engineers who consistently show this instinct are fast-tracked for senior engineering responsibility at FAANG companies.

### Senior @ FAANG: Performance Management as a Platform Capability at L6

At senior level, the performance tips in this section apply not just to your own performance but to the performance environment you create for the engineers around you. An L6 engineer who gives high-quality code review feedback is improving the performance of every engineer they review. An L6 engineer who writes clear technical design documents is reducing the ambiguity cost for everyone who contributes to that domain. An L6 engineer who identifies and addresses systemic friction in the development environment is improving the performance of the entire team.

This multiplier framing is essential for senior performance reviews. The performance tips you apply to your own work should also be evaluated in terms of how they scale through others. "I wrote high-quality code" is an L4 performance signal. "I established code quality standards and review processes that raised the quality of code written by the entire team" is an L6 performance signal. The content of the work may overlap. The frame is entirely different.

The governance principle at L6: every quarter, ask yourself not just "what did I contribute" but "how did the team's capability change because of how I contributed." The answer to the second question is your senior performance case.
