## 95.2 Getting Promoted

### The Promotion Reality

Promotion processes reward demonstrated impact at the next level rather than tenure at the current one, requiring engineers to document and communicate their work in the vocabulary the evaluation committee uses. The following framework shows how to build a promotion case with concrete artifacts, sponsor relationships, and calibrated level expectations.

```
WHAT GETS YOU PROMOTED:

1. ALREADY OPERATING AT NEXT LEVEL
   Not: "Promote me and I'll perform better"
   But: "I'm already doing the job, formalize it"

2. VISIBLE IMPACT
   Work that matters AND is seen by decision-makers

3. CONSISTENT PERFORMANCE
   Not one big project, but sustained excellence

4. SPONSOR/ADVOCATE
   Someone senior who champions your case

5. AVAILABLE HEADCOUNT
   Sometimes external factors matter

WHAT DOESN'T GET YOU PROMOTED:
✗ Tenure alone ("I've been here 3 years")
✗ Hard work without impact
✗ Being liked without delivering
✗ Threats to leave
✗ Comparison to others
```

### Building Your Promotion Case

A strong promotion case is built over months, not assembled the week before packets are due. The most effective engineers maintain a running accomplishment document throughout the year, capturing impact metrics, positive feedback, and project outcomes as they happen. The framework below shows how to structure your promotion packet so that each section directly addresses what promotion committees evaluate.

```
DOCUMENT CONTINUOUSLY:
• Keep a running list of accomplishments
• Note impact metrics as they happen
• Save positive feedback (emails, Slack)
• Track projects against goals

THE PROMOTION PACKET:

IMPACT SUMMARY:
"Over the past year, I've consistently operated at
[target level] as demonstrated by..."

KEY ACCOMPLISHMENTS (3-5):
For each:
• What you did
• Why it mattered
• Quantified impact
• Scope/complexity

SCOPE DEMONSTRATION:
• Teams you influenced
• Projects you led
• People you mentored

FEEDBACK QUOTES:
• Peer feedback
• Stakeholder comments
• Manager feedback

GROWTH EVIDENCE:
• New skills developed
• Increased responsibility
• Leadership examples
```

### Junior → FAANG: The Promotion Prerequisite That Most Engineers Skip

The most consistently underestimated promotion prerequisite at early career is demonstrated impact on work that others depend on. Individual contributions, no matter how technically sophisticated, rarely provide a strong promotion case at FAANG companies past the first promotion. The cases that consistently succeed show that other engineers' work was better or faster because of your contribution: code you wrote that other teams use, standards you established that others follow, or systems you improved that reduced friction for the entire team.

The practical implication is to look for opportunities that have a multiplier effect on others' work as a deliberate part of your regular contribution. Improving the development environment, adding integration test infrastructure, fixing a noisy test suite, or documenting a complex system that new team members frequently struggle with are all most productive multiplier contributions that create evidence for a promotion case that pure feature work cannot.

### Senior @ FAANG: Staff Promotion Evidence and the Organization-Level Narrative

At senior level, the promotion to staff (L7) requires a qualitatively different kind of evidence than any prior promotion. The question is not whether you have performed excellently at L6 scope: it is whether you have operated at L7 scope in a sustained way. The specific evidence patterns that distinguish L7 promotion cases from strong L6 performance cases are: you have driven technical direction for a domain that spans multiple teams, you have delivered systems or established standards that entire organizations rely on, and you have influenced the technical thinking of other senior engineers in a way that is visible and verifiable.

The organizational narrative in a staff promotion packet should answer: what would not have happened, or would have happened worse, without this engineer's presence. If the answer to that question fits in a single team's work, the case is not yet ready. If the answer spans multiple teams and includes changes to how the organization approaches a class of technical problem, the case is ready.

Maturity model signal: L7 candidates who are denied promotion at the first submission typically receive feedback that their impact is "not yet organizational." This means the evidence is within-team rather than cross-team. The twelve months following a denied L7 promotion case should be spent explicitly generating cross-team impact evidence, with the explicit agreement of your manager about what evidence will be sufficient for the next cycle.
