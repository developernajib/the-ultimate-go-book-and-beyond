## 95.10 Building Your Personal Brand

### Establishing Technical Credibility

Technical credibility is built through a consistent public record of sound judgment, code reviews, design docs, conference talks, and open source contributions that others can point to when vouching for your expertise. Unlike internal reputation, a visible external record follows you across companies and makes compensation negotiations easier because interviewers arrive with prior context. The framework below maps specific activities to the credibility signals they produce, so you can invest your limited time in channels that compound over a career.

```markdown
# BUILDING REPUTATION AS AN ENGINEER

## WITHIN YOUR COMPANY:

### Quality Work Foundation:
- Ship high-quality code consistently
- Write clear, thorough design docs
- Give thoughtful code reviews
- Be reliable on commitments

### Become the "Go-To" Person:
- Deep expertise in something important
- Known for helping others with [topic]
- First call for certain types of problems

### Visibility Through Contribution:
1. INTERNAL TALKS:
   - Present at team meetings
   - Give tech talks at larger venues
   - Share postmortems and learnings

2. DOCUMENTATION:
   - Author team knowledge bases
   - Write onboarding guides
   - Document tribal knowledge

3. MENTORSHIP:
   - Formally or informally mentor others
   - Be known for helping people grow
   - Create a positive reputation network

4. CROSS-TEAM IMPACT:
   - Help other teams succeed
   - Build reusable tools/libraries
   - Participate in org-wide initiatives

## OUTSIDE YOUR COMPANY:

### Writing (Blog/Articles):
- Technical blog posts
- Case studies from your work (properly anonymized)
- Tutorials and how-to guides

Benefits:
- Establishes expertise
- Generates opportunities
- Improves communication skills
- Gives back to community

### Speaking:
- Local meetups (low barrier)
- Conference talks (higher visibility)
- Podcasts and interviews

Tips:
- Start small (team → company → external)
- Practice talks multiple times
- Focus on practical lessons, not theory

### Open Source:
- Contribute to projects you use
- Create tools that solve real problems
- Maintain projects responsibly

Benefits:
- Code speaks for itself
- Network with other contributors
- Learn from quality codebases

### Social Media (Technical):
- Twitter/X: Engage thoughtfully on tech topics
- LinkedIn: Share achievements and insights
- Hacker News: Participate in discussions

Warning: Brand can also be damaged here.
Be thoughtful about what you post.

## WHAT TO AVOID:

❌ Self-promotion without substance
❌ Criticizing others publicly
❌ Overstating accomplishments
❌ Controversial non-work topics
❌ Complaining about employer

## BRAND AUDIT QUESTIONS:

1. What are you known for at work?
2. Who would recommend you and why?
3. If you left, what would people say?
4. What appears when someone googles you?
5. Would a hiring manager be impressed by your online presence?
```

### Junior → FAANG: Personal Brand Building as a Long-Term Investment

Early-career personal brand building is primarily about demonstrating genuine competence and engagement in a focused domain, not about visibility for its own sake. The engineers who build the most valuable early-career brands are the ones who go deep on something specific: they become the person who understands Go's concurrency primitives better than anyone on the team, or the person who has the deepest knowledge of the team's data pipeline. Domain expertise compounds because it makes you the natural person to consult on a class of problems, which creates visibility opportunities that general competence does not.

Writing about what you are learning is the most effective brand-building action available at early career. A technical blog post that explains a concept clearly and honestly, including the parts you initially got wrong, builds credibility faster than portfolio projects because it demonstrates both technical understanding and the intellectual honesty to acknowledge gaps. FAANG engineers who read well-written technical content by unknown engineers frequently reach out with hiring interest.

### Senior @ FAANG: Platform Thinking for Senior Brand Strategy

At senior level, personal brand is an organizational asset as much as an individual one. A staff engineer with a strong external reputation as a technical authority in distributed systems or infrastructure is more likely to attract strong candidates to their team, more likely to be successful in cross-company technical collaborations, and more likely to have their proposals taken seriously by organizational leadership.

The governance framework for senior brand building: identify the two or three technical domains where you have genuine differentiated expertise, and build your external presence around those domains. Conference talks, published technical writing, open-source contributions, and participation in technical standards bodies all contribute to domain authority. The key is consistency and specificity over time: being known as the person who thinks clearly about distributed consistency models is more valuable than being known as someone who writes interesting things broadly.

The internal brand dimension at senior level is equally important. Technical talks at internal engineering all-hands, well-written RFC proposals that circulate widely, and documented decisions that others reference are the internal brand signals that promotion committees and organizational leadership use to evaluate senior candidates. Building internal technical authority systematically, not just organically, is a deliberate practice at L6 and above.
