## 95.3 The Promotion Conversation

### Timing the Discussion

Initiating a promotion conversation at the wrong moment can signal poor judgment and undermine your case before it begins. Timing matters because your manager's receptiveness depends heavily on their current workload and the organizational context, a conversation held during a high-stress period or immediately after a setback anchors their impression of you to that negative moment rather than your accomplishments. Choose a window when you have recent visible wins, your manager has headspace for a developmental discussion, and the review cycle is close enough that the conversation translates into a concrete action plan.

```
GOOD TIMES TO DISCUSS:
• 1-2 months before review cycle
• After a major accomplishment
• During goal-setting discussions
• When taking on bigger scope

BAD TIMES:
• Right after a failure
• During a crisis
• When manager is stressed
• After just receiving feedback
```

### The Conversation

The goal of this conversation is not to argue your case but to close the information gap between how you see your performance and how your manager sees it. Most engineers who fail to get promoted aren't lacking in technical ability, they lack explicit alignment with their manager on what "ready" actually looks like at the next level. Treating this as a listening session rather than a pitch prevents defensiveness, surfaces the real criteria, and turns your manager into an ally who will advocate for you during calibration.

```
INITIATING:
"I'd like to discuss my path to [level]. Can we talk
about where I am relative to that bar and what I
need to focus on?"

DURING:
• Listen more than talk
• Take notes
• Ask clarifying questions
• Don't argue or get defensive

FOLLOW UP:
• Summarize understanding in email
• Create development plan
• Schedule regular check-ins
• Track progress against feedback
```

### Junior → FAANG: Starting the Promotion Conversation Before You Think You Are Ready

Most early-career engineers wait until they believe they deserve a promotion before having the conversation with their manager. This is too late. The promotion conversation should start twelve to eighteen months before your target promotion cycle, framed not as "I want a promotion" but as "I want to understand what the promotion bar is and whether my current trajectory puts me on track to meet it."

This framing serves two purposes. First, it gives you accurate information about the gap between your current performance and the promotion bar, while there is still time to close it. Second, it signals to your manager that you are taking your development seriously and thinking in career-scale timeframes rather than quarterly ones.

The most important question to ask in this conversation: "What specific evidence would you need to see in my next review packet to feel confident advocating for my promotion?" The answer to this question is your preparation target. Write it down and track progress against it explicitly.

### Senior @ FAANG: The Promotion Conversation as a Governance Contract

At senior level, the promotion conversation is a governance contract between you and your manager about what evidence is required and when it will be evaluated. This contract should be explicit, written, and reviewed quarterly. Verbal understandings about promotion timelines are not reliable because managers change, organizational priorities shift, and calibration standards evolve. Written records protect you when any of these things happen.

The conversation at L6 targeting L7 should include: agreement on which dimension of L7-level work is currently evidenced and which is not, a specific plan for generating the missing evidence in a defined timeframe, and a shared understanding of what external factors (headcount, org structure, business priorities) could affect the timeline. Any manager who is unwilling to have this conversation explicitly is not yet ready to advocate for you, which is itself important information about whether your current organization can support your growth.

Staff-level promotion cases at many FAANG companies also require skip-level and sometimes executive-level visibility. The promotion conversation with your manager should include explicit discussion of how to build this visibility: what forums exist for senior technical work to be presented to organizational leadership, and what actions you can take to ensure your work is known at the levels that influence the promotion committee.
