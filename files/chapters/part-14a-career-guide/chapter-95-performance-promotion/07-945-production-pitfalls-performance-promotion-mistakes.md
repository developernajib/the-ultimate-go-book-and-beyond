## 95.6 Production Pitfalls: Performance & Promotion Mistakes

### Pitfall 1: Assuming Good Work Gets Noticed Automatically

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer consistently delivered high-impact work for three years but rarely spoke in meetings, didn't document their accomplishments, and assumed their manager knew what they did. At promotion time, the committee asked: "What has this person accomplished?" The manager couldn't provide detailed examples. No promotion.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- "My work speaks for itself"
- Not tracking accomplishments
- Expecting managers to remember everything
- Waiting to be recognized instead of advocating

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Visibility Without Self-Promotion

## Running Accomplishment Document (Weekly):
- What did I ship?
- What impact did it have? (metrics)
- Who did I help/unblock?
- What did I learn?

## Monthly Summary to Manager:
Short email: "Here's what I accomplished this month..."
Makes their job easier, ensures they remember

## Quarterly Self-Review:
- Major accomplishments
- Impact on team/org goals
- Areas of growth
- What I want to work on next

## The Balance:
Not bragging, not invisible
Factual, evidence-based, helpful to those who
need to represent you in rooms you're not in
```

**Key Insight**: Promotion committees have limited information. Your manager is your advocate - make it easy for them to advocate by providing clear, documented evidence.

### Pitfall 2: Not Understanding Promotion Criteria

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer focused heavily on technical depth, becoming the team's best debugger and code reviewer. At promotion review, feedback was: "Strong L5 work, but no evidence of L6 scope or influence." They had optimized for the wrong criteria - L6 required cross-team impact, not just deep expertise.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Not reading level expectations carefully
- Assuming what got you here gets you there
- Focusing on strengths instead of gaps
- Not asking what's expected at the next level

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Level-Up Framework

## Step 1: Get the rubric
- Ask HR/manager for level definitions
- Find examples of successful promotions
- Understand each dimension

## Step 2: Honest self-assessment
For each dimension, rate yourself:
- Meeting expectations
- Below expectations (gap)
- Exceeding expectations (strength)

## Step 3: Focus on gaps, not strengths
You don't get promoted for being excellent
at things you're already good at.
You get promoted for meeting ALL criteria.

## Common L5 → L6 gaps:
- Cross-team influence
- Leading projects without authority
- Mentoring others
- Strategic thinking (not just execution)

## Ask your manager:
"What would I need to demonstrate to be a strong
L6 candidate?"
```

**Key Insight**: Promotions require meeting all criteria, not excelling at some. Find your gaps and deliberately address them.

### Pitfall 3: Waiting for the Perfect Project

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer waited for two years for a "promotion-worthy project" to be assigned to them. They passed on smaller opportunities, waiting for something bigger. Meanwhile, a peer took on several "small" cross-team initiatives, built influence, and was promoted. The waiting engineer is still waiting.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Waiting to be given opportunities
- Only valuing big, visible projects
- Not creating opportunities from existing work
- Expecting promotion-worthy work to be assigned

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Creating Promotion Opportunities

## From existing work:
- Turn a bug fix into a reliability improvement
- Turn a feature into a reusable framework
- Turn a problem into a cross-team initiative
- Turn helping one person into mentorship program

## Proactive approach:
1. Identify an org-wide problem
2. Propose a solution (RFC, doc)
3. Build coalition to address it
4. Lead the effort (even if not assigned)
5. Document the impact

## Portfolio approach:
Don't wait for ONE big thing.
Collect 3-5 medium things that together
demonstrate next-level scope.

## The mindset shift:
"When will they give me a promotion project?"
→ "What can I turn into a promotion project?"
```

**Key Insight**: The best candidates create promotion-worthy work from ordinary situations. They don't wait - they reframe and expand.

### Pitfall 4: No Sponsor or Advocate

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer had a strong promotion packet with excellent work. In the calibration meeting, another engineer with similar work was promoted - and this engineer wasn't. The difference: the other engineer had a skip-level manager advocating strongly for them in the room. This engineer's manager presented but didn't advocate.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Only relationship is with direct manager
- No senior visibility on your work
- No one who will advocate in calibration rooms
- Not building relationships before needing them

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Building Sponsorship

## What sponsors do:
- Vouch for you in rooms you're not in
- Advocate during calibration
- Connect you to opportunities
- Provide career guidance

## Who can be sponsors:
- Skip-level manager
- Senior ICs who see your work
- Managers of adjacent teams
- Directors who've seen your presentations

## How to build sponsorship:
1. Do visible, high-quality work
2. Present at team/org meetings
3. Help their teams succeed
4. Ask for feedback and advice
5. Keep them informed of your accomplishments

## The ask (after building relationship):
"I'm working toward [next level]. I'd value your
support in [calibration/packet review/etc].
Would you be willing to advocate for me?"
```

**Key Insight**: Calibration is not a pure meritocracy. It's influenced by who advocates for you and how credibly they do it. Build those relationships early.

### Pitfall 5: Getting Discouraged After One Rejection

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer was passed over for promotion and interpreted it as "they don't value me." They stopped trying, disengaged, and eventually left for another company at the same level. If they had stayed and addressed the feedback, they likely would have been promoted within a year.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Taking rejection personally
- Not seeking specific feedback
- Giving up after first attempt
- Leaving before proving yourself (and then re-proving at new company)

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# Handling Promotion Rejection

## Immediate (within 1 week):
1. Schedule feedback meeting with manager
2. Ask specifically: "What was missing?"
3. Get concrete examples and suggestions
4. Don't argue or get defensive

## Processing:
- It's okay to be disappointed
- Separate emotion from action
- Most successful people were rejected first

## Action plan (within 2 weeks):
- Identify the 2-3 gaps clearly
- Create plan to address each one
- Set timeline for re-application
- Check in monthly on progress

## The Statistics:
- Most staff+ engineers were rejected at least once
- 50%+ of successful packets were revised and resubmitted
- Persistence is expected at senior levels

## Red flags (when to leave):
- Feedback is vague or inconsistent
- Goals keep changing
- Peers with less accomplishment are promoted
- Pattern of unfair treatment
```

**Key Insight**: Rejection is data, not a verdict. The path to senior levels usually includes setbacks. How you respond to rejection is itself a signal of seniority.

### Quick Reference: Performance & Promotion Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|--------------|---------|-----|
| Invisible work | "What did they accomplish?" | Document continuously, share summaries |
| Wrong criteria | Excellent at L5, not meeting L6 | Study level guide, focus on gaps |
| Waiting for projects | "When will they assign me something?" | Create opportunities from existing work |
| No sponsor | No one advocating in calibrations | Build senior relationships early |
| Giving up | Disengaged after rejection | Get feedback, make plan, try again |
| Only manager | Single point of failure | Multiple visibility and support |

### Junior → FAANG: The Pitfall of Invisible Good Work

The most expensive early-career promotion pitfall is assuming that excellent technical work will be noticed and rewarded without any communication effort on your part. FAANG companies have highly structured review processes where the evidence considered is specifically what is in the review packet. Work that is not documented, not surfaced to your manager, and not connected to measurable outcomes does not exist in the promotion process, regardless of its actual quality.

The practical fix is mechanical: maintain a brag document with a new entry every time you complete something worth noting. Each entry should have: what you did, what changed as a result, and what evidence exists. Review this document with your manager monthly, not just at review time. This turns your manager into an informed advocate who can speak specifically and credibly about your impact, rather than an advocate who is working from a general positive impression.

The pitfall of relying on a single manager as your only source of visibility is related. If your manager leaves the company or transfers to another team in the review cycle, your promotion case is in danger. Build visibility with your skip-level and with senior engineers outside your immediate team throughout the year so that your manager's advocacy is supplemented by independent knowledge of your work.

### Senior @ FAANG: Promotion Pitfalls That Are Specific to Senior-Level Transitions

The most damaging senior-level promotion pitfall is conflating impact with activity. Senior engineers sometimes respond to promotion feedback about insufficient organizational impact by doing more things rather than doing higher-impact things. A month of high-velocity L5-scope work produces more activity but not more L6 evidence.

The discipline at senior level is to explicitly filter your work for organizational leverage. Before taking on a new project or initiative, ask: if I execute this excellently, does it generate evidence for L7-level impact or L6-level impact. The honest answer to this question determines whether the work should be prioritized or delegated. Senior engineers who consistently optimize for high-value work and delegate or decline low-leverage work generate promotion evidence faster than engineers who optimize for utilization.

The governance pitfall that is specific to L6: waiting for your manager to identify the promotion opportunity rather than driving it yourself. At L6, the expectation is that you operate with significant autonomy including autonomy over your own career development. A senior engineer who needs their manager to identify the right project for their promotion case has already demonstrated a gap in the strategic self-direction that L7 requires.
