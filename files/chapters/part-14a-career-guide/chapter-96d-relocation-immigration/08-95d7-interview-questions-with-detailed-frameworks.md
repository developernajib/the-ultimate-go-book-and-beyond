## 96D.7 Interview Questions with Detailed Frameworks

### Question 1: "Why are you relocating?" or "Why did you relocate?"

**What Interviewers Are Testing**:
- Whether you've thought through the decision
- Commitment to the location (will you stay?)
- Whether you have realistic expectations
- How you handle major life decisions

**Framework for Answering**:

```
STRUCTURE YOUR RESPONSE:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  1. PULL FACTORS (Positive reasons to go TOWARD new location)    │
│     - Career opportunity                                         │
│     - Industry hub / job market                                  │
│     - Family or personal reasons                                 │
│     - Quality of life factors                                    │
│                                                                  │
│  2. NOT PUSH FACTORS (Don't emphasize running FROM something)    │
│     - Avoid: "I hate the weather in my current city"             │
│     - Avoid: "Cost of living is killing me"                      │
│     - Avoid: "I can't stand my current company"                  │
│                                                                  │
│  3. LONG-TERM COMMITMENT                                         │
│     - Show you've researched the area                            │
│     - Mention you're planning to put down roots                  │
│     - Reference community, housing, etc.                         │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Example Strong Answer**:

"I'm relocating to Seattle for a few reasons. First, it's one of the major tech hubs with incredible companies and engineering talent - I want to be where the most interesting problems are being solved.

Second, this specific opportunity at your company aligns perfectly with my background in distributed systems. I've been following your work on [specific project] and it's exactly the kind of challenge I'm looking for.

Third, I've visited Seattle several times and really connect with the outdoor culture. I'm an avid hiker, and having mountains and water so close is genuinely exciting for me.

I've already been researching neighborhoods and I'm planning to buy a home within the first year - this isn't a trial run for me. Seattle is where I want to build the next phase of my career."

**Red Flags to Avoid**:
- "My spouse got a job there" (unless they ask about family)
- "I'll see if I like it" (sounds temporary)
- "Anywhere is better than [current city]" (negative framing)

### Question 2: "How will you handle the transition while ramping up at a new job?"

**What Interviewers Are Testing**:
- Practical planning skills
- Self-awareness about challenges
- Whether you'll be distracted during onboarding
- Maturity in handling complexity

**Framework for Answering**:

```
SHOW YOU'VE PLANNED:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  BEFORE START DATE:                                              │
│  ├── Temporary housing arranged                                  │
│  ├── Essentials shipped or packed                                │
│  ├── Banking/logistics handled                                   │
│  └── 1-2 weeks buffer before starting                            │
│                                                                  │
│  FIRST MONTH:                                                    │
│  ├── Focus on work during work hours                             │
│  ├── Handle logistics evenings/weekends                          │
│  ├── Temporary housing = no pressure to rush                     │
│  └── Set clear boundaries                                        │
│                                                                  │
│  REALISTIC ACKNOWLEDGMENT:                                       │
│  ├── "Some distraction is inevitable"                            │
│  ├── "I've planned to minimize it"                               │
│  └── "I'll communicate if I need flexibility"                    │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Example Strong Answer**:

"I've thought carefully about this. Relocating and starting a new job simultaneously is challenging, so I've planned to separate the two as much as possible.

I'm negotiating a start date that gives me 10 days after arrival to handle immediate logistics - setting up the apartment, getting essentials, learning the commute. By day one, I want to walk in focused on the job, not worried about where my furniture is.

During my first month, I'll be in temporary housing. That removes the pressure to make a permanent housing decision while I'm learning a new codebase. House hunting will happen on weekends, not during work hours.

I'll also be transparent. If there's a day when I need to leave early for a housing inspection, I'll let my manager know. But I'm committed to making sure the move doesn't impact my ramp-up. I've done this before, and the key is front-loading the logistics so they don't compete with work."

### Question 3: "Your immigration status seems complex. How should we think about your sponsorship needs?"

**What Interviewers Are Testing**:
- Clear communication about requirements
- Whether you've done your research
- Risk level for the company
- How you handle sensitive topics professionally

**Framework for Answering**:

```
BE CLEAR AND CONFIDENT:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  CURRENT STATUS:                                                 │
│  "I'm currently on [visa type] valid through [date]"             │
│                                                                  │
│  WHAT'S NEEDED:                                                  │
│  "For this role, I would need [specific sponsorship]"            │
│                                                                  │
│  PROCESS CLARITY:                                                │
│  "The typical process is [timeline and steps]"                   │
│                                                                  │
│  COMPANY IMPACT:                                                 │
│  "For the company, this means [costs, timeline, obligations]"    │
│                                                                  │
│  ALTERNATIVES:                                                   │
│  "If [issue], I also have [alternative path]"                    │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Example Strong Answer**:

"I'm currently on an H-1B visa with a major tech company, valid through October 2026. I've been through the lottery process and am already authorized to work in the US.

For this role, I would need an H-1B transfer. The good news is that transfer doesn't require the lottery - I can start working as soon as the transfer petition is filed, while it's being processed. Most companies use premium processing, which guarantees a 15-day decision.

I should also mention that my current employer has filed my green card - the I-140 was approved last year. This means my priority date is portable. If I join your company, I'd bring that with me, and your immigration team would file a new PERM but my place in line stays the same.

The practical impact is low. No lottery risk, straightforward transfer, and I've been through this process before. I'm also working on building an O-1 case as a backup, though I don't think I'll need it for this situation."

**Key Points**:
- Don't be vague or evasive - companies need clarity
- Show you understand the process (reduces perceived risk)
- Mention alternatives if applicable
- Be matter-of-fact, not apologetic

### Question 4: "Tell me about a time you had to adapt to a new environment quickly."

**What Interviewers Are Testing**:
- Adaptability and resilience
- How you handle uncertainty
- Problem-solving in unfamiliar situations
- Whether you'll integrate well in their team

**Framework for Answering (STAR)**:

```
RELOCATION EXAMPLES WORK WELL:

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  SITUATION:                                                      │
│  "I relocated from [origin] to [destination] for [role]"         │
│                                                                  │
│  TASK:                                                           │
│  "I needed to become effective in my new role while also         │
│   adapting to [new culture/environment/context]"                 │
│                                                                  │
│  ACTION:                                                         │
│  1. How you approached learning the new environment              │
│  2. What resources or support you used                           │
│  3. How you balanced work and adaptation                         │
│  4. Proactive steps you took                                     │
│                                                                  │
│  RESULT:                                                         │
│  - How quickly you became effective                              │
│  - Feedback you received                                         │
│  - Relationships you built                                       │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Example Strong Answer**:

"When I relocated from India to Seattle for Amazon, I had three weeks to become effective in a new country, new company, and new timezone - while my family was still in India.

The biggest challenge was the culture difference, both corporate and social. In my previous company, decisions were more hierarchical. Here, I was expected to disagree openly and drive my own priorities.

I adapted by being intentional. First, I scheduled coffee chats with five teammates in my first two weeks - not just work talk, but understanding how things actually worked. Second, I asked my manager for direct feedback weekly: 'What am I doing well? What should I change?' Third, I joined the Seattle hiking group at Amazon - it forced me to meet people outside my team and gave me something to do on weekends while my family wasn't there yet.

By month three, I'd shipped my first feature, been invited to architecture reviews, and had a core group of colleagues I could rely on. My manager specifically mentioned in my first review that I ramped faster than expected given the relocation complexity."

### Junior → FAANG: Relocation Interview Questions as Forward-Looking Competence Signals

Interviewers ask about relocation for two reasons: to assess whether you have thought through the practical implications and are committed to the transition, and to understand what motivated the move. Both questions are opportunities to demonstrate thoughtfulness and intentionality rather than reactive decision-making.

The answer frame: briefly acknowledge the challenge, describe how you prepared for and managed it, and connect it to positive outcomes. "Moving cross-country for a new role required significant planning, and I invested in that preparation by [specific actions]. The transition was challenging at first, but by [specific milestone] I had [specific positive outcome]." This frame demonstrates the problem-solving and resilience that FAANG companies look for in every context.

### Senior @ FAANG: Relocation as a Strategic Career Narrative at Senior Level

At senior level, "why did you relocate" and "how did you navigate immigration" are often asked in behavioral interview contexts as evidence of long-term career thinking, managing complex personal circumstances, and making high-stakes decisions with incomplete information. These are the same skills that L6 technical decisions require.

Frame your relocation story explicitly as a decision-making story: what information you gathered, what tradeoffs you evaluated, what you decided and why, and what you learned from the outcome. This connects personal experience to the professional competency being assessed and demonstrates the judgment that senior-level interviews are designed to probe.
