### Making the Rent vs Buy Decision

The rent-versus-buy decision depends on how long you plan to stay, local market conditions, and your financial situation. In expensive tech hubs, renting often makes more financial sense even for high earners. Run the numbers before assuming that buying is always the better choice.

### Junior → FAANG: Domestic Relocation Decision Framework for Early Career

Relocating within the US for a first FAANG job is one of the most financially significant decisions an early-career engineer makes, and it deserves analysis proportional to its impact. The key calculation: net financial benefit after cost of living adjustment, accounting for state income tax differences, housing cost differences, and the relocation package. A $50,000 total compensation increase from San Francisco relative to your current market may yield $15,000 in actual financial improvement after accounting for Bay Area housing costs, state income tax, and relocation expenses.

The rent-versus-buy question during the first year in a new city almost always resolves to rent. You do not yet know which neighborhoods you prefer, which commute patterns work for you, and whether you will stay long enough to recover transaction costs on a home purchase. Most financial advisors recommend renting for at least one to two years after a major relocation before purchasing.

### Senior @ FAANG: Domestic Location Optimization at Senior Level

At senior level, domestic location decisions include the option of staying where you are and taking a remote role, which is a location optimization that was not broadly available before 2020. The financial analysis requires comparing: the compensation adjustment (if any) for your location, the housing cost difference between your current location and the hub where the role is nominally based, the career cost of reduced physical presence with your team, and the quality of life value of staying in your current community.

The governance consideration: FAANG companies that implemented location-adjusted compensation have created a structural incentive for engineers to live in lower cost-of-living markets and accept lower nominal compensation that represents higher real purchasing power. Whether this is the right tradeoff depends on career stage, organizational influence requirements, and personal priorities. It deserves explicit analysis rather than defaulting to either "of course I'll move" or "of course I'll stay remote."
