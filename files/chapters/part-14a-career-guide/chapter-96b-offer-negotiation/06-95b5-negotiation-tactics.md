## 96B.5 Negotiation Tactics

### Initial Response to Offer

The first minutes after an offer arrives set the tone for everything that follows. The rule: express enthusiasm, take time, never accept or counter on the call:

```
WHEN YOU RECEIVE THE OFFER:

Step 1: Express Enthusiasm (Genuine)
"Thank you so much for the offer! I'm really excited
about the opportunity to work at [Company]."

Step 2: Don't React to the Number
Even if it's lower than expected, stay neutral.
Never say "That's lower than I expected" or show disappointment.

Step 3: Ask for Time
"I'd like to take some time to review the full
details. When do you need a decision by?"

Step 4: Ask for Written Offer
"Could you send me the full offer details in writing
so I can review everything carefully?"

WHAT NOT TO DO:
✗ Accept immediately (even if it's great)
✗ Reject immediately
✗ Show disappointment or frustration
✗ Start negotiating on the spot
✗ Mention other companies unprompted
```

### Counter Offer Structure

A well-structured counter offer presents your request clearly, justifies it with market data, and maintains a collaborative tone. The goal is to negotiate, not demand. Frame your counter as a conversation about reaching a package that works for both sides.

```
THE PERFECT COUNTER OFFER FORMULA:

1. GRATITUDE
   "Thank you again for the offer. I'm very excited about [Company]."

2. ENTHUSIASM
   "After [interview/conversation], I'm even more convinced
   this is the right opportunity for me."

3. THE ASK (SPECIFIC)
   "Based on my research and [competing offer/experience/market data],
   I'm looking for a total compensation of $X."

4. JUSTIFICATION (BRIEF)
   "This reflects my [X years of experience in Y] and
   [specific valuable skill or competing offer]."

5. COLLABORATION
   "I'm confident we can find a package that works for both of us."

EXAMPLE:

"Hi [Recruiter],

Thank you again for the offer to join [Company] as a Senior Engineer.
I'm very excited about the opportunity and the team.

After reviewing the offer details and considering my current
situation, I'd like to discuss the compensation. I have a competing
offer with a total compensation of $380,000, and I'm hoping
[Company] can match or come closer to that figure.

Specifically, I'm looking for:
- Base salary: $225,000 (up from $200,000)
- Sign-on bonus: $50,000 (up from $25,000)
- RSU grant: $450,000 (up from $350,000)

I'm very motivated to join [Company] and confident we can
find an arrangement that works well for both of us.

Looking forward to discussing further.

Best,
[Your Name]"
```

### Negotiating Without Competing Offers

A competing offer is the strongest lever, but its absence does not mean you have none. These are the levers that work on their own:

```
STRATEGIES WHEN YOU DON'T HAVE LEVERAGE:

1. USE MARKET DATA
   "Based on levels.fyi and my research, the market rate
   for this role is $X-$Y. I'd like to discuss getting
   closer to the market midpoint."

2. EMPHASIZE YOUR VALUE
   "Given my experience with [specific valuable skill]
   and my track record of [specific achievement],
   I believe I bring above-market value."

3. BE SPECIFIC ABOUT NEEDS
   "I'm currently at $X total comp. To make this move
   work for my family, I'd need to see at least $Y."

4. ASK QUESTIONS FIRST
   "Is there flexibility in the offer?"
   "What would it take to get to $X?"
   "Are there other components that could be adjusted?"

5. NEGOTIATE NON-SALARY ITEMS
   - Sign-on bonus (often most flexible)
   - Start date
   - Remote work flexibility
   - Title/level
   - Extra vacation days
   - Relocation assistance
```

### Component-by-Component Strategy

When the recruiter says base salary is capped, negotiate equity. When equity is fixed, negotiate signing bonus. When everything seems fixed, negotiate start date, remote work, or relocation. Different compensation components have different levels of flexibility, and knowing which ones to push on gives you more room to improve your total package.

```
WHICH COMPONENTS ARE MOST FLEXIBLE?

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  COMPONENT      │ FLEXIBILITY │ NOTES                           │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Sign-on Bonus  │ ★★★★★      │ Easiest to increase              │
│  RSU Grant      │ ★★★★☆      │ Usually room to negotiate        │
│  Start Date     │ ★★★★☆      │ Almost always flexible           │
│  Title/Level    │ ★★★☆☆      │ Sometimes possible               │
│  Base Salary    │ ★★☆☆☆      │ Often has strict bands           │
│  Annual Bonus % │ ★☆☆☆☆      │ Usually fixed by level           │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

STRATEGY:
If base salary is stuck at band maximum, push for:
1. Higher sign-on bonus to boost Year 1 comp
2. More RSUs to boost Years 2-4 comp
3. Earlier start date for sign-on (if split over 2 years)
4. Level bump (changes all bands upward)
```

### Junior → FAANG: Choosing Tactics That Match Your Negotiating Position

At early career, your negotiating position is primarily defined by the presence or absence of competing offers and your willingness to walk away. If you have a competing offer, use it: "I have a competing offer at X total compensation and I'm deciding between the two this week, is there any flexibility here." If you do not have a competing offer, your primary tactic is market rate anchoring: "Based on levels.fyi data for this role in this market, I was expecting something in the range of X to Y."

The single most effective early-career tactic is the pause before the counter. When you receive an offer, say "thank you, I'm very excited about this opportunity, I'd like to review the details and get back to you by Friday." This pause accomplishes three things: it prevents you from accepting in the moment before you have evaluated the offer, it gives you time to research and prepare your counter, and it signals to the recruiter that you are a deliberate decision-maker who takes the process seriously.

### Senior @ FAANG: Tactical Sequencing in Multi-Round Senior Negotiations

At senior level, negotiation is typically a multi-round process rather than a single exchange. Understanding the typical sequence and how to manage it gives you significant advantage. Round one is the initial offer. Round two is your counter, typically by email with specific numbers anchored to market data and/or competing offers. Round three is the company's improved offer. Round four is either acceptance or a final ask.

The tactical principle for senior multi-round negotiations: save your most compelling leverage for round three, not round one. If you reveal a competing offer in your first counter, you have limited your future leverage. If you mention market rate data in round one and introduce a competing offer in round two, you have created a two-lever escalation that is significantly more effective.

The level bump tactic deserves explicit treatment: if you believe you are being evaluated at a level below what your scope justifies, address the leveling conversation separately from the compensation conversation. A level bump changes the entire compensation band upward and is worth substantially more than a within-band adjustment. However, it requires a strong case about scope and is typically only viable for candidates with significant leverage from competing senior offers or internal sponsorship.
