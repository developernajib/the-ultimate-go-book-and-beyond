## 96B.7 Special Situations

### Negotiating as a New Grad

New-grad offers have less room than senior offers, but less is not none. Where the flexibility actually lives:

```
NEW GRAD NEGOTIATION REALITY:

The truth: Less room to negotiate, but still possible.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  WHAT'S TYPICALLY FIXED:                                         │
│  ├── Base salary (set by level/band)                             │
│  ├── Equity grant (standard new grad package)                    │
│  └── Bonus percentage (level-based)                              │
│                                                                  │
│  WHAT'S POTENTIALLY FLEXIBLE:                                    │
│  ├── Sign-on bonus (can often add or increase)                   │
│  ├── Start date                                                  │
│  ├── Location                                                    │
│  ├── Team placement                                              │
│  └── Relocation package                                          │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

LEVERAGE FOR NEW GRADS:
- Competing offers (strongest)
- Return offer from internship
- Strong competing company names
- Specialized skills (ML, systems, etc.)
- PhD premium (if applicable)

EXAMPLE SCRIPT:
"I have a competing offer from [Google/Meta/etc.] at $X.
Is there any flexibility in the sign-on bonus to help
close the gap?"
```

### Negotiating as Senior/Staff Engineer

At senior and staff levels the negotiable surface widens well beyond base salary:

```
SENIOR ENGINEER NEGOTIATION ADVANTAGES:

1. More negotiation room at higher levels
2. Equity becomes larger portion (more to negotiate)
3. Experience is harder to replace
4. Specific expertise commands premium

STRATEGIES:

LEVEL NEGOTIATION:
"Based on my experience leading [specific initiative]
and my track record at [current company], I believe
I should be considered for [higher level]. Could we
discuss leveling?"

EQUITY EMPHASIS:
At senior levels, push harder on equity:
- More impactful to long-term comp
- Often more room than base salary
- Refreshers set a higher bar

SCOPE NEGOTIATION:
"I'm interested in a role where I can [specific scope].
Could we discuss how this position might grow?"
```

### Handling a Downlevel Offer

A downlevel offer (interviewing at L6, offered L5) is common and negotiable. The response framework:

```
WHEN THEY OFFER YOU A LOWER LEVEL:

This is common. Companies are conservative with leveling.

DON'T:
✗ Take it personally
✗ Accept without discussion
✗ Assume it's final

DO:
✓ Ask for specific feedback on the leveling decision
✓ Provide concrete examples of higher-level work
✓ Negotiate a review timeline

SCRIPT:
"I noticed the offer is for [L4] while I was expecting [L5]
based on our discussions. Could you help me understand the
leveling decision?

In my current role, I've been operating at [higher level]
by [specific examples: leading projects, mentoring,
technical decisions]. I'd like to discuss whether there's
flexibility here, or alternatively, what a path to
[higher level] would look like in the first 6-12 months."

FALLBACK:
If they won't budge on level, negotiate:
- Higher compensation within the lower level band
- Documented 6-month level review
- Written expectations for promotion
```

### Counter Offer from Current Employer

When your current employer makes a counter offer after learning you plan to leave, the dynamic shifts. You are no longer negotiating with a prospective employer, you are weighing a retention offer from people who know your work against a new opportunity. Evaluate this carefully, because the underlying reasons you decided to leave usually do not change.

```
YOUR CURRENT EMPLOYER MAKES A COUNTER OFFER:

Statistics: 50-80% of people who accept counter offers
leave within 18 months anyway.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  REASONS TO BE SKEPTICAL:                                        │
│                                                                  │
│  ├── Why did it take a resignation to get fair pay?              │
│  ├── Trust may be damaged ("flight risk")                        │
│  ├── The reasons you wanted to leave probably remain             │
│  ├── You may be first on the layoff list                         │
│  └── Promotion path may be affected                              │
│                                                                  │
│  WHEN IT MIGHT MAKE SENSE:                                       │
│                                                                  │
│  ├── You genuinely love the job/team                             │
│  ├── The only issue was compensation                             │
│  ├── Counter offer includes promotion/scope change               │
│  └── New opportunity has significant red flags                   │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

IF YOU CONSIDER ACCEPTING:
- Get it in writing immediately
- Ensure it includes any promised changes (not just money)
- Have an honest conversation about trust going forward
- Be prepared for awkwardness
```

### Junior → FAANG: Special Situations That Require Different Preparation

New grad negotiation is a special situation because the band compression at entry level is real: FAANG companies have less negotiating flexibility for new grad offers than for experienced hire offers because the entry-level bands are narrower. However, total compensation is still negotiable. The most movable lever for new grad negotiations is the signing bonus, which is often one to three times the base salary at FAANG and can be negotiated independently of the RSU grant.

Return offer negotiation for former interns is a specific situation where you have more information than in a cold offer: you have first-hand knowledge of the team, the culture, and the work. You also have the leverage of knowing that the company has already invested in training you. This is a stronger negotiating position than a cold candidate, and it should be used accordingly.

Counter-offer situations from your current employer are psychologically complex. The general principle: if you would not have been looking for a new role if your current employer had offered you this compensation six months ago, that is important information about the relationship. Counter-offers are statistically associated with departure within 12 to 18 months because the underlying reasons for looking typically persist.

### Senior @ FAANG: Complex Special Situations at Senior Level

Senior special situations include negotiating through a company acquisition, negotiating for a newly created role without an established band, and negotiating compensation when your primary reason for leaving is not compensation but organizational dysfunction. Each requires a different approach.

Acquisition negotiation: when your company is being acquired and you are being retained, the leverage dynamics are unusual. The acquiring company wants to retain you and has budget to do so, but you may not have the information advantage of knowing the market range for your level in the new organization. Request a leveling conversation before accepting any retention package and insist on understanding how your current level maps to the acquiring company's leveling system before evaluating any compensation numbers.

Newly created role negotiation: when a company is creating a new senior or staff-level role for you specifically, the band is often not established and the range is more negotiable than in standard hiring. This is an opportunity to anchor the compensation band for the role through your negotiation, which affects not just your compensation but potentially the band for everyone hired into that role in the future.
