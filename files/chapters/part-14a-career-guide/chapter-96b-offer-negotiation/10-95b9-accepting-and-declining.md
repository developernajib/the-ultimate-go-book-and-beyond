## 96B.9 Accepting and Declining

### The Acceptance Process

Accepting an offer involves more than saying yes. You need to confirm the negotiated terms in writing, complete pre-start paperwork, and begin planning your departure from your current role. Missing steps here can lead to misunderstandings about your start date, compensation, or benefits.

```
AFTER YOU ACCEPT:

1. SEND WRITTEN ACCEPTANCE
   Email confirmation with offer details

2. COMPLETE BACKGROUND CHECK
   - Be honest on all forms
   - Discrepancies can cause rescinded offers
   - If you have concerns, address proactively

3. CONFIRM START DATE
   - In writing
   - Account for any relocation needs
   - Don't leave current job until background clears

4. COMPLETE NEW HIRE PAPERWORK
   - I-9 documents
   - Tax forms
   - Benefits enrollment
   - Direct deposit

5. COORDINATE WITH CURRENT EMPLOYER
   - Give notice only after background check clears
   - Standard is 2 weeks, senior roles may be more
```

### Declining Other Offers Gracefully

How you decline matters: recruiters change companies, and the person you decline today sources candidates for your dream team in three years:

```
IMPORTANT: Decline promptly and gracefully.

Tech is a small world. The recruiter you ghost today might be:
- At your dream company next year
- Hiring for a better role you want
- Connected to people at your new company

TIMELINE: Decline within 24-48 hours of accepting elsewhere

EMAIL TEMPLATE:
"Hi [Name],

Thank you so much for the offer and for all your help
throughout the process. After careful consideration,
I've decided to accept another opportunity.

This was a difficult decision - I was genuinely impressed
by [something specific]. I hope we can stay in touch,
and I wish you and the team the best.

Thank you again,
[Your Name]"
```

### On Reneging

Reneging, accepting an offer and then backing out before starting, is the most professionally damaging thing you can do in a job search. The consequences extend far beyond one company: recruiters talk to each other, and your reputation in the industry may suffer for years. Understand the risks before you put yourself in this position.

```
RENEGING: Accepting then backing out before starting

THE HARD TRUTH:
- It's legal but burns bridges
- Recruiters talk to each other
- Company may blacklist you
- It's a small industry

WHEN IT MIGHT BE JUSTIFIED:
- Significantly better offer (not just 10% more)
- Major life change
- Red flags emerged after accepting
- Dream job you couldn't turn down

IF YOU MUST RENEGE:
1. Do it as early as possible
2. Call (don't email) if possible
3. Be honest but brief
4. Apologize sincerely
5. Accept that the bridge is burned
6. Don't do it more than once in your career

BETTER APPROACH:
- Ask for deadline extensions
- Be honest: "I'm waiting on another offer"
- Make decision when you have all information
```

### Junior → FAANG: Professional Acceptance and Declining Without Burning Bridges

Accepting and declining offers professionally is a career-long reputation management exercise. The engineering community, particularly within a specific domain or city, is smaller than it appears. The recruiter you decline today may be the hiring manager you interview with in five years. The engineer who referred you to a company you decline will hear about how you handled the process.

Accepting: send a written acceptance email within the deadline, express genuine enthusiasm for the opportunity, confirm the start date, and ask for confirmation of the details in writing. Read the offer letter carefully before signing: understand the equity grant size, vesting schedule, cliff date, and any non-compete clauses. Non-competes are enforced with varying rigor across jurisdictions but they are worth understanding before signing.

Declining: communicate the decline as early as possible once the decision is made. Do not delay out of awkwardness. A brief, professional email that expresses gratitude, gives a one-sentence reason (another opportunity was a better fit for where I'm trying to go), and closes warmly is the standard. No further explanation is required.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Acceptance Process and Organizational Commitment Governance

At senior level, the acceptance process includes a set of pre-employment conversations that early-career offers do not require. Before sending your written acceptance, confirm the following in writing or by explicit verbal agreement with the hiring manager (not just the recruiter): the reporting structure and who you will report to, the scope and mandate of the role including which decisions you are expected to own, the timeline for building your team or inheriting your team, and any specific commitments about resource allocation or executive sponsorship that were discussed during the process.

These are not contractual commitments in most cases, but having them documented in an email before acceptance creates a shared record that is useful if organizational context changes after you join. Senior engineers who skip this step sometimes find themselves in roles with different scope than what was discussed, and the post-acceptance period is a much weaker position from which to renegotiate scope than the pre-acceptance period.

The governance principle for senior declinations: at L6 and above, declining a FAANG offer is a relationship management event as much as a logistical one. Send a personalized note to the hiring manager (not just the recruiter) thanking them for the process and explaining briefly what drove your decision. This maintains the relationship at the level where future re-engagement is natural and warm, not awkward.
