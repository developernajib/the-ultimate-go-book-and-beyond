### Beyond Compensation Factors

The factors that dominate long-term career value are mostly not in the offer letter:

```
CRITICAL NON-COMP FACTORS TO EVALUATE:

1. YOUR FUTURE MANAGER
   - Did you meet them? What was your impression?
   - Ask: "What happened to the last person in this role?"
   - Research them on LinkedIn
   - Bad manager = bad experience regardless of pay

2. TEAM HEALTH
   - Team tenure (high turnover = red flag)
   - Team growth or contraction
   - How do team members talk about the work?

3. SCOPE AND GROWTH
   - What will you learn?
   - Path to next level?
   - Interesting problems?

4. COMPANY TRAJECTORY
   - Stock likely to go up or down?
   - Layoff risk?
   - Market position?

5. LIFESTYLE
   - Commute / remote policy
   - On-call requirements
   - Work hours culture
   - Vacation actually taken vs offered
```

### The Gut Check

After all the spreadsheets and analysis, your intuition still matters. If something feels wrong about an offer despite the numbers looking good, pay attention to that signal. Use this final checklist to make sure you have considered the decision from every angle before committing.

```
FINAL DECISION CHECKLIST:

□ Can I see myself excited to work here in 2 years?
□ Will I learn and grow?
□ Do I respect the people I met?
□ Is the compensation fair for my market value?
□ Does this move make sense for my career arc?
□ Can I explain why I took this job in future interviews?
□ Am I running toward this opportunity or away from something?

IF STILL UNDECIDED:
- Talk to someone who works there (not the recruiter)
- Ask for another call with the hiring manager
- Sleep on it one more night
- Trust your instincts

Remember: There's rarely a "perfect" choice.
Both options are probably good. Pick one and commit.
```

### Junior → FAANG: Making the Decision Without Regret

The most common decision-making failure at early career is optimizing for the wrong variable. Engineers who are early in their careers should generally optimize for learning environment and mentorship quality over total compensation, because the skill development in your first three to five years has compounding effects on your career trajectory that outweigh the financial difference between a well-compensated junior role and a very-well-compensated junior role.

This does not mean accepting below-market compensation: it means that when two offers are both at or near market rate, the tie-breaker should be team quality and learning opportunity, not the $10K difference in base salary. Engineers who join excellent teams at fair compensation grow faster, reach senior levels earlier, and earn more in total over their careers than engineers who optimize for maximum first-job compensation.

### Senior @ FAANG: Decision Framework for Senior Moves and the Opportunity Cost Analysis

At senior level, the decision framework includes an opportunity cost analysis that early-career decisions do not require. Unvested equity at your current employer is real financial value that you forfeit by leaving, and the make-whole calculation in a competing offer may or may not fully compensate for it. Model this explicitly: what is the value of unvested RSUs at current and pessimistic stock price scenarios, how does the signing bonus in the new offer compare, and what is the break-even timeline.

The governance consideration at senior level is organizational timing. Senior engineers who leave in the middle of a significant technical initiative they are leading create real costs for their current organization. This is not a reason to stay if the opportunity cost of staying is higher, but it is a consideration in how and when you give notice and how you structure the transition. Senior engineers who manage this responsibly maintain stronger professional networks than those who leave abruptly at critical moments.

The final decision principle: the best career decisions are the ones made from a position of genuine choice, not desperation or fear. If you are evaluating multiple offers while employed, you have options. Make the choice deliberately, implement it cleanly, and commit to it fully.
