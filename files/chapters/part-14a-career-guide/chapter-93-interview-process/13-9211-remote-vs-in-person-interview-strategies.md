## 93.12 Remote vs In-Person Interview Strategies

Remote interviews have become standard. Understanding the differences helps you perform your best in either format.

### Remote Interview Best Practices

Remote rounds remove the whiteboard but add failure modes of their own. The practices that matter:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              REMOTE INTERVIEW SETUP                              │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  TECHNICAL SETUP:                                                │
│  ├── Camera: Eye level, good angle                               │
│  ├── Lighting: Light source in front of you                      │
│  ├── Audio: Headphones with microphone recommended               │
│  ├── Background: Neutral, not distracting                        │
│  ├── Internet: Wired connection if possible                      │
│  └── Backup: Phone hotspot ready, phone number exchanged         │
│                                                                  │
│  BEFORE INTERVIEW:                                               │
│  ├── Test video platform (Zoom, Meet, Teams)                     │
│  ├── Close unnecessary applications                              │
│  ├── Silence phone and notifications                             │
│  ├── Have backup login ready (browser if app fails)              │
│  ├── Position notes where you can glance (not read)              │
│  └── Have water nearby                                           │
│                                                                  │
│  DURING INTERVIEW:                                               │
│  ├── Look at camera, not screen (eye contact)                    │
│  ├── Nod and react visually (helps without audio cues)           │
│  ├── Speak slightly slower than in person                        │
│  ├── Ask "Can you see my screen?" when sharing                   │
│  ├── Pause longer after questions (latency)                      │
│  └── If tech fails, stay calm, switch to backup                  │
│                                                                  │
│  CODING PLATFORM PREP:                                           │
│  ├── Practice on CoderPad, HackerRank, etc. beforehand           │
│  ├── Know keyboard shortcuts (auto-complete, run, etc.)          │
│  ├── Test screen sharing before interview                        │
│  └── Have local IDE ready as backup                              │
│                                                                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Remote-Specific Challenges

```
CHALLENGE: Building Rapport Without Physical Presence

IN-PERSON:
├── Small talk walking to room
├── Physical handshake, body language
├── Natural conversation flow
└── Energy from environment

REMOTE EQUIVALENT:
├── Brief small talk before starting
├── Verbal acknowledgment ("That's a great point")
├── Slightly more animated expressions
└── Deliberate positive energy

---

CHALLENGE: Technical Difficulties

PREPARATION:
□ Test audio/video 30 minutes before
□ Have backup device ready
□ Exchange phone numbers with recruiter
□ Know how to rejoin meeting
□ Have ethernet cable ready

WHEN THINGS GO WRONG:
1. Stay calm - interviewers understand tech issues
2. Communicate immediately: "I'm having audio issues"
3. Try quick fixes: rejoin, switch to phone audio
4. Use backup: "Can we switch to phone for audio?"
5. Don't panic - it's not held against you

---

CHALLENGE: Screen Sharing and Coding

TIPS:
├── Practice screen sharing before interview
├── Increase font size (easier to read remotely)
├── Have a clean desktop (no personal items visible)
├── Share specific window, not entire screen
├── Close sensitive tabs/apps before sharing
└── Keep talking while typing (silence feels longer remotely)
```

### In-Person Interview Advantages

Onsite loops give you signal channels that video calls flatten. Use them deliberately:


### Junior → FAANG: Adapting Your Performance to the Format

Remote interviews have become the default format at most FAANG companies, and early-career candidates who treat them as equivalent to in-person often underperform because they miss the specific behavioral adaptations required.

The most important remote-specific habit: look at the camera, not at the interviewer's face on screen. Looking at the face feels natural and is actually less effective than looking at the camera, which creates the appearance of direct eye contact for the interviewer. This sounds trivial but it changes the social dynamic of the conversation in a measurable way.

For whiteboarding in remote system design rounds: use a shared tool like Excalidraw or the built-in whiteboard in your video platform, and narrate every component you draw as you draw it. Do not draw in silence and then explain. The narration during drawing is the signal the interviewer needs to assess your reasoning process, not the final diagram.

In-person interviews carry a different set of adaptations. Energy and affect are more visible in person, which is an advantage for candidates who are naturally expressive and a disadvantage for candidates who are nervous and show it physically. Deliberate pacing (writing on the whiteboard more slowly, pausing before answering) projects confidence in a way that feels unnatural to practice but registers positively to interviewers.

### Senior @ FAANG: Remote Interview Governance for Multi-Loop Senior Searches

Senior candidates running multiple concurrent remote interview loops face a logistics and cognitive load challenge that requires explicit management. The risk is not performing poorly in individual rounds but performing inconsistently across a loop because the isolation of remote format removes the social anchoring that in-person interviews provide.

A governance practice that mitigates this: before each round, spend two minutes reviewing the one thing you want the interviewer to take away from this specific conversation. In a remote format, you do not get the ambient social calibration of in-person interaction, so you need to be more intentional about what you communicate in the limited time available.

For staff-level remote interviews that include architecture presentations or extended design discussions: the production quality of your shared artifacts matters more remotely than in-person because there is no physical whiteboard presence to compensate for. A clean, well-organized diagram with clear component labels and explicit tradeoff annotations is evaluated differently than a sketch that would be perfectly clear in person but reads as unclear on screen. Invest in the presentation layer of technical artifacts for remote senior interviews.
