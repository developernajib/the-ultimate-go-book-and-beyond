## 96.4 Financial Planning for Engineers

### Compensation Optimization

Most engineers systematically underestimate their total compensation because they focus on base salary and overlook the significant value in equity grants, bonus targets, and tax-advantaged benefits. A $20,000 difference in annual base salary is often smaller than the difference between maxing a 401(k) with employer match versus not, or between understanding RSU vesting cliffs versus letting grants lapse. Auditing every component at each job change and promotion negotiation ensures you capture the full economic value of your role.

```
TOTAL COMPENSATION AWARENESS:

Base Salary: Negotiate on hire, each promotion
Equity: Understand vesting, refresh grants
Bonus: Know target and actual history
Benefits: Max 401(k) match, HSA, ESPP

EQUITY CONSIDERATIONS:
• RSUs: Actual value, taxed when vested
• Options: Potential value, exercise strategy
• Startup equity: High risk, high reward
• Diversification: Don't hold all company stock
```

### Financial Independence

The Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE) framework is particularly achievable for software engineers because high compensation combined with disciplined savings rates produces outsized compounding effects. The core insight is that financial independence is primarily a function of savings rate rather than income level: an engineer earning $150k and saving 50% reaches independence far faster than one earning $300k but saving 10%. For engineers, equity events and remote work cost reductions can meaningfully accelerate this timeline beyond what salary alone would predict.

```
FIRE PRINCIPLES:

SAVINGS RATE:
• 10-20%: Retire at 65
• 30-40%: Retire at 50
• 50%+: Retire in 15 years

TECH ADVANTAGE:
• High salaries enable high savings
• Equity can accelerate wealth
• Remote work reduces costs

KEY STRATEGIES:
1. Live below your means
2. Max tax-advantaged accounts
3. Invest in low-cost index funds
4. Build emergency fund (6 months)
5. Avoid lifestyle creep
```

### Junior → FAANG: Financial Planning Fundamentals That Compound from Year One

The most important financial decision for early-career FAANG engineers is the lifestyle inflation decision. When your total compensation increases significantly at your first FAANG role relative to your previous income or student budget, the difference between investing most of that increase and spending most of that increase compounds dramatically over a 15-year horizon.

The specific habits that produce financial freedom for engineers: maximize tax-advantaged accounts first (401K to employer match, then Roth IRA if eligible, then 401K to maximum), diversify RSUs when they vest rather than holding concentrated employer stock, and delay significant lifestyle expense increases until you have established an investment rate of at least 25 to 30 percent of take-home pay.

The emergency fund recommendation in this section is not optional: six months of expenses in liquid, low-risk savings gives you the psychological freedom to make career decisions based on what you want rather than what you need, which becomes more valuable as your career advances and your options multiply.

### Senior @ FAANG: Financial Architecture at L6 and the Independence Horizon

At L6 total compensation, financial independence is achievable within ten to fifteen years for most engineers who invest consistently and avoid major lifestyle inflation. Understanding this horizon explicitly changes how you make career decisions: a senior engineer who knows they can reach financial independence in twelve years has a fundamentally different decision calculus than one who believes they must maximize current compensation indefinitely.

The senior financial architecture priorities: maximize and optimize equity through tax-loss harvesting, asset location across taxable and tax-advantaged accounts, and deliberate estate planning that protects the wealth you are building. These optimizations require specialist financial planning advice but produce meaningful improvements in after-tax wealth accumulation at senior compensation levels.

The governance principle for senior financial planning: treat your financial plan as a governance document that you review annually with a fee-only financial advisor. Just as you would not architect a distributed system without an explicit design and regular review, your financial architecture deserves the same systematic attention.
