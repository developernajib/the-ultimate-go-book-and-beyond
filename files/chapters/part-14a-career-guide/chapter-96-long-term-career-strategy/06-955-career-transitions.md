## 96.5 Career Transitions

### When to Leave

Staying too long at a company where growth has plateaued is one of the most common and costly career mistakes engineers make. The opportunity cost compounds: each year of stagnation is a year where market value drifts below what a new role could establish, skills atrophy relative to the industry, and the network remains static. Distinguishing genuine signals, blocked growth, toxic culture, learning plateau, from transient frustrations that would follow you to any employer is the critical judgment call that separates a well-timed move from one you regret.

```
TIME TO LEAVE WHEN:
• Learning has stopped
• Growth is blocked
• Culture is toxic
• Better opportunities exist
• Personal reasons (location, life changes)

NOT GOOD REASONS:
• Temporary frustration
• Grass-is-greener syndrome
• Running from problems you'll face elsewhere
• Only for money (unless significant)
```

### Making Transitions

Not all career moves carry the same risk profile, and choosing the right type of transition for your current situation determines both the probability of success and how quickly you recover if the move does not work out as planned. A lateral move to a new company preserves your existing level and skill set while offering fresh challenges. A level change often requires external validation because internal promotion committees are conservative about leveling up. A domain or track change trades short-term seniority for long-term optionality. Mapping the transition type to your risk tolerance and current stability is the foundation of a deliberate move.

```
TYPES OF TRANSITIONS:

SAME TRACK, DIFFERENT COMPANY:
• Senior Engineer → Senior Engineer
• Lowest risk, potential comp increase
• Fresh start, new challenges

LEVEL CHANGE:
• Sr Eng → Staff Eng
• May need job change to level up
• External often easier than internal

PATH CHANGE:
• IC → Management (or reverse)
• Consider trying at current company first
• Can often go back if doesn't fit

DOMAIN CHANGE:
• Frontend → Backend → Infra
• Possible but expect some level reset
• Leverage transferable skills
```

### Junior → FAANG: When to Stay and When to Move at Early Career

The "when to leave" question at early career is simpler than it feels: stay until you have absorbed the primary lessons the current role offers and established the evidence base needed for your next move. Leaving too early means carrying less experience and fewer accomplishments than you could have. Staying too long means stagnating in a role that has stopped developing you.

Practical signals that it is time to consider moving: you have stopped encountering problems you do not know how to solve, your manager has told you that further growth opportunities are limited in the current role, or you have been at the same level for more than two years without a clear path to the next. Any one of these signals is worth exploring. All three together is a strong signal.

The domain transition question: moving from one technical domain to another (from application development to infrastructure, from backend to platform engineering) typically requires accepting a level reset at the new employer. This is often worth it if the new domain offers significantly better long-term career trajectory or compensation, but it should be entered with clear expectations.

### Senior @ FAANG: Career Transition Strategy at Senior Level and the Leverage Assessment

At senior level, the career transition decision includes an organizational leverage assessment that early-career decisions do not. Before leaving a senior role, evaluate: what unique organizational capital do you have in your current role (relationships, domain authority, project ownership) that you will forfeit by leaving, and how long will it take to rebuild equivalent capital in a new organization.

The leverage reconstruction timeline for senior engineers is typically longer than engineers expect. Building the organizational credibility needed to drive significant technical change in a new organization takes six to twelve months at minimum, even for senior engineers with strong external reputations. The compensation premium from an external move needs to be weighed against this productivity cost.

The counter-intuitive senior transition insight: the engineers who make the strongest external moves are typically the ones who have built the most organizational capital in their current role, not the ones who are least satisfied. Having organizational leverage to walk away from is a negotiating position. Having organizational satisfaction to walk away from is a compensation and scope optimization problem.
