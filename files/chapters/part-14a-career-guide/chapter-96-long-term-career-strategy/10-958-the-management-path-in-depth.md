## 96.9 The Management Path In Depth

### Engineering Manager Progression

Transitioning from individual contributor to engineering manager requires shifting the primary productivity measure from personal code output to team throughput and organizational health. The following roadmap covers the skill gaps, relationship building, and early leadership opportunities that prepare engineers for the management transition.

### Manager vs IC at Each Level

Compensation and scope for managers and ICs converge at senior levels but differ significantly in daily work. Understanding how the two tracks compare at each level helps you make an informed decision about which path fits your strengths and goals.

```markdown
# COMPENSATION AND SCOPE COMPARISON

## Big Tech Approximate Equivalence:

| IC Level | Rough Manager Equivalent |
|----------|-------------------------|
| Senior (L5) | Manager (M1) |
| Staff (L6) | Senior Manager (M2) |
| Senior Staff (L7) | Director |
| Principal (L8) | Senior Director |
| Distinguished (L9) | VP Engineering |

## Reality Check:
- At most companies, IC and management pay similarly through Staff level
- Director+ management often pays more than equivalent IC
- IC ceiling exists at most companies (Principal is rare)
- VP/C-level only accessible through management

## Day-to-Day Reality:

### Staff Engineer Day:
- 30% coding/technical work
- 30% design docs, reviews, mentoring
- 20% meetings (technical)
- 20% influencing, strategizing

### Engineering Manager Day:
- 50% meetings (1:1s, team, cross-functional)
- 20% hiring and people management
- 15% planning and coordination
- 15% technical review and guidance
- 0% coding (typically)

## Which Is "Better"?
Neither. Different people thrive in each.
The best answer is the one that matches your energy.
```

### Transitioning to Management

```markdown
# IC TO MANAGEMENT TRANSITION

## Before You Commit:

### Test Drive:
- Lead an intern or junior engineer
- Take on a tech lead role
- Run a project end-to-end
- Handle a difficult interpersonal situation

### Ask Yourself:
- Do I enjoy helping others succeed?
- Am I comfortable with conflict?
- Can I let go of coding?
- Am I energized by organizational problems?
- Can I handle not having "my code"?

## The Transition:

### What You'll Miss:
- Flow state of coding
- Clear completion of tasks
- Direct creation of things
- Technical mastery feeling
- Individual contribution credit

### What You'll Gain:
- Multiplication of impact
- Shaping team culture
- Developing people
- Broader organizational influence
- Different kind of problem-solving

## First 90 Days as New Manager:

### Week 1-2:
- 1:1 with every team member
- Understand current state
- Identify immediate issues
- Build trust

### Month 1:
- Establish 1:1 cadence
- Understand team dynamics
- Quick wins if possible
- Don't make big changes yet

### Month 2-3:
- Address obvious issues
- Start shaping team processes
- Begin performance conversations
- Hire if needed

## Common New Manager Mistakes:

1. Coding too much
   → Your job is now different

2. Not having 1:1s
   → This is your primary tool

3. Avoiding difficult conversations
   → Problems fester, address early

4. Micromanaging
   → Set direction, give space

5. Playing favorites
   → Be fair and transparent
```

### Returning to IC

```markdown
# MANAGEMENT TO IC TRANSITION

## Why People Return:
- Missed hands-on work
- Discovered management isn't for them
- Wanted technical challenges
- Burned out from people management
- Life circumstances changed

## Making the Transition:

### The Challenge:
- Skills may have atrophied
- Technology moved on
- May need to "prove" yourself again
- Some perceive as "demotion" (it's not)

### The Approach:
1. Be honest about why you're returning
2. Expect some ramp-up time
3. Apply management experience
4. Don't manage from IC role
5. Accept temporary discomfort

### What You Bring:
- Understanding of organizational dynamics
- Communication and influence skills
- Broader perspective on tech decisions
- Experience managing projects
- Empathy for manager challenges

## Interview Narrative:
"I tried management and learned a lot about leadership and
organizations. I discovered my greatest energy comes from
hands-on technical work. Management experience made me a
better engineer - I now understand how to work effectively
across teams and communicate technical concepts to non-
technical stakeholders."
```

### Junior → FAANG: Management Path Early Signals Worth Developing Now

Even if you are not targeting the management path in the next two to three years, developing some management-adjacent skills at early career makes you a more effective engineer and opens the path if you change your mind later.

The most valuable management-adjacent skills to develop: giving structured feedback on others' work (in code reviews, in design discussions, and to mentees if you have them), facilitating productive technical disagreements toward decisions, and learning to communicate technical concepts clearly to people with different technical backgrounds. These skills compound on the IC path as well as preparing you for management.

The specific management skill that early-career engineers most dramatically underestimate: translating technical constraints into business language. Engineers who can explain why a technical decision matters to a product manager or business stakeholder in terms of customer outcomes, cost, or risk are substantially more effective collaborators and substantially more promotable than engineers who cannot.

### Senior @ FAANG: Engineering Management Path Governance at Director and Above

The management path case study in this section describes individual engineering management (EM) and senior EM. At director level and above, the organizational operating model changes fundamentally: you are managing managers rather than individual contributors, and the skills that made you a successful EM are necessary but not sufficient for the director role.

The governance consideration at director level: your organizational leverage comes primarily from your ability to hire, develop, and retain excellent engineering managers, not from your own technical judgment. The director who continues to operate primarily through technical influence rather than organizational leadership is not scaling the role appropriately, and this mis-operating pattern is the most common reason technically excellent senior EMs fail to succeed at director level.

The political capital dimension at director level: directors are participants in organizational strategy decisions that affect headcount, product direction, and team structure. Building the relationships and credibility with product and business leadership that give you voice in these decisions is a qualitatively different challenge than the technical credibility-building that precedes it. The engineers who succeed most at director level are the ones who invest in this organizational relationship development as deliberately as they invested in technical credibility earlier in their careers.
