## 94.7 Check-ins and Feedback

Regular check-ins with your manager are your most important tool for staying on track during your first 90 days. These meetings give you a structured opportunity to share your progress, surface blockers, and, most critically, get feedback before small issues become big problems. Many new hires assume that no news is good news, but in reality your manager may have concerns they have not yet raised. Being proactive about asking for feedback shows maturity and gives you the chance to adjust your approach early.

### Regular Check-ins

```
WITH YOUR MANAGER:
• Weekly 1:1s (minimum)
• Discuss progress and blockers
• Ask for feedback
• Share concerns

SUGGESTED AGENDA:
1. What I accomplished this week
2. What I'm working on next
3. What I'm blocked on
4. Questions I have

QUESTIONS TO ASK:
• Am I meeting expectations?
• What should I focus on more?
• What could I do differently?
• Any concerns?
```

### 30/60/90 Day Reviews

Structured milestone reviews at 30, 60, and 90 days serve a different purpose than weekly check-ins, they zoom out from day-to-day tasks to evaluate whether your overall trajectory is correct. Asking explicitly "am I meeting expectations?" at each milestone forces a concrete answer rather than vague reassurance, giving you actionable data to either continue your current approach or course-correct before a formal review cycle locks in a negative impression.

```
30-DAY CHECK-IN:
• Am I ramping up as expected?
• What should I focus on next 30 days?
• Any feedback on how I'm doing?
• Is there anything you'd like me to do differently?

60-DAY CHECK-IN:
• How is my contribution level?
• Am I working on the right things?
• Any growth areas to focus on?
• What does success look like at 90 days?

90-DAY CHECK-IN:
• How has my first quarter been?
• Am I meeting expectations for the role?
• What are my goals for the next quarter?
• What should my development focus be?
```

### Junior → FAANG: Making Check-Ins Work for You

The check-in questions in this section are a starting point but they need to be customized to your specific situation each week. The most effective check-ins are not status updates where you report what you did. They are structured conversations where you surface one thing that went well (with a specific reason), one thing you are uncertain about, and one explicit ask. This format is efficient for your manager and produces useful feedback more reliably than open-ended status updates.

The single most important question to ask in your week-four check-in: "Is there anything I am doing or not doing that you would want to flag now before it becomes a pattern?" This question gives your manager explicit permission to give you critical feedback early when it is cheap to act on, rather than waiting until a performance review when it is expensive. Most managers will not give this feedback spontaneously: they are waiting to see if the pattern continues.

### Senior @ FAANG: Check-Ins as Bidirectional Calibration at L6

At senior level, check-ins with your manager serve a calibration function that is different from the feedback-gathering function at early career. You are not primarily asking "how am I doing." You are calibrating your understanding of organizational priorities, ensuring your manager's view of your impact matches your own, and surfacing organizational dynamics that might affect your technical direction.

The senior-level check-in question that is most often skipped: "Is there anything I am planning to work on in the next 30 days that you think I should deprioritize given organizational constraints I may not be aware of?" This question surfaces the invisible political and resource constraints that senior engineers often collide with unnecessarily. A manager who knows you are planning a significant refactor will tell you that the team is about to go through an org change that makes the timing bad. Without asking this question explicitly, you will likely proceed and then be surprised.

Governance practice: keep a written record of what you discuss in each check-in and what feedback you received. Review this record monthly to identify patterns: are you consistently getting feedback on the same dimension, is your manager consistently expressing concerns about the same area of your work. Written records also protect you in the event that manager expectations shift and there is a question about what guidance you received during onboarding.
