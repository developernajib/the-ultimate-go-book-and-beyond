## 94.9 Production Pitfalls: First 90 Days Mistakes

The mistakes described in this section are drawn from real incidents at real companies. Each pitfall includes what went wrong, why it happened, and a concrete alternative approach you can use instead. These are not hypothetical scenarios, they are patterns that onboarding coaches and engineering managers see repeatedly, and understanding them ahead of time can save you months of lost credibility.

### Pitfall 1: Trying to Change Everything Too Fast

**Real Incident Report**: A senior engineer joined from Google and immediately started criticizing everything: "At Google, we did it this way." "This architecture is wrong." "Why don't you have X?" Within weeks, they were isolated. The team resented the newcomer who hadn't earned credibility but was telling everyone they were wrong.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Criticizing current systems before understanding them
- Proposing major changes in first month
- Comparing to previous company constantly
- Not learning the "why" behind current decisions

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# The "Earn Before You Spend" Framework

## Month 1: Learn and Build Credibility
- Ask "why" instead of "this is wrong"
- Assume there are reasons you don't understand
- Ship something small successfully
- Build relationships, not judgment

## Month 2: Propose Small Improvements
- Suggest changes to things you now understand
- Start with low-risk improvements
- Earn trust through successful small changes

## Month 3: Larger Proposals
- Now you have credibility AND context
- Your suggestions come with understanding
- People will listen because you've proven yourself

## Instead of:
"At my last company, we did X"

## Say:
"I'm curious why we do Y. Is there history there?"
"Have we considered X? I've seen it work, but I'm
not sure if it applies here."
```

**Key Insight**: "Trust is earned in drops and lost in buckets." New hires who criticize early lose credibility that takes months to rebuild.

### Pitfall 2: Not Building Relationships Intentionally

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer focused purely on code for their first 90 days. They were productive but didn't know anyone outside their immediate team. When they needed cross-team collaboration for a major project, they had no relationships to draw on. A peer who had spent time networking was chosen to lead the project instead.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Heads-down on code, no networking
- Only talking to immediate teammates
- Skipping social events and coffees
- Not meeting key stakeholders

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# First 90 Days Relationship Map

## Week 1-2: Immediate Team
- 1:1 with every team member
- Understand their role and expertise
- Learn who knows what

## Week 3-4: Adjacent Teams
- 1:1 with leads of teams you'll work with
- Understand dependencies and interfaces
- Learn team dynamics and history

## Month 2: Key Stakeholders
- Product managers you'll work with
- Engineers in related domains
- Your skip-level manager

## Month 3: Broader Network
- Engineers you admire
- People in roles you aspire to
- Cross-functional partners (design, data)

## Coffee Chat Template:
"I'm new and trying to understand how things work.
I'd love to learn about your team and how we might
work together. Do you have 20 minutes for coffee?"
```

**Key Insight**: Your network determines your opportunity surface. Engineers who invest in relationships in their first 90 days get better projects and faster promotions.

### Pitfall 3: Suffering in Silence When Blocked

**Real Incident Report**: A new hire spent three days stuck on a build issue that a teammate could have solved in 10 minutes. They were afraid to ask for help and look incompetent. When their manager found out, they were more concerned about the wasted time than impressed by the independence.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Not asking questions to avoid looking dumb
- Spending hours on things others could answer quickly
- Not escalating blockers
- Waiting to be asked instead of proactively communicating

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# The 15-Minute Rule

## Before asking:
1. Try to solve it yourself (15-30 minutes)
2. Document what you tried
3. Formulate a specific question

## When asking:
"I'm trying to [goal]. I tried [X, Y, Z] but hit [problem].
Do you have any suggestions?"

## Never:
- Be blocked for more than 2 hours without escalating
- Ask without showing you tried first
- Ask the same question twice without notes

## Good questions are:
- Specific (not "it doesn't work")
- Show effort (what you tried)
- Teachable (you'll learn the answer, not just get unstuck)

## Who to ask:
1. Onboarding buddy
2. Team's designated "newbie helper"
3. Team channel
4. Manager (for escalation)

## Cultural note:
At most companies, asking questions is valued.
Being blocked silently is not.
```

**Key Insight**: Managers universally report preferring engineers who ask questions over those who struggle silently. The cost of a wrong answer to a question is near zero. The cost of wasted days is high.

### Pitfall 4: Focusing Only on Technical Work

**Real Incident Report**: A new senior engineer shipped features efficiently but never wrote documentation, skipped team meetings, and didn't participate in on-call rotation until forced. At their first review: "Strong technical work, but not integrating into the team culture. Not demonstrating senior-level behaviors." No promotion path until they changed.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Only measuring success by code shipped
- Treating documentation and meetings as waste
- Not participating in team responsibilities
- Not understanding what "senior" means at this company

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# First 90 Days Success Dimensions

## Technical (necessary but not sufficient):
□ Shipped meaningful code
□ Quality PRs with tests
□ Participated in code reviews

## Cultural:
□ Attended team meetings and participated
□ Joined on-call rotation
□ Participated in team social events

## Process:
□ Wrote or improved documentation
□ Followed team processes
□ Asked about unfamiliar practices before bypassing

## Relationship:
□ Met 1:1 with teammates
□ Built relationships with stakeholders
□ Had feedback conversations

## Communication:
□ Shared status proactively
□ Asked questions publicly (helps others too)
□ Documented learnings

## At your level, understand:
What does "good" look like at this company?
Ask your manager explicitly.
```

**Key Insight**: Every company weights these dimensions differently. At Netflix, autonomy is prized. At Amazon, leadership principles matter. Ask what's valued - don't assume.

### Pitfall 5: Not Seeking Feedback Until It's Too Late

**Real Incident Report**: An engineer assumed no news was good news. At their 6-month review, they learned their manager had concerns about their communication style that had been festering for months. If they'd asked for feedback at month 1, they could have course-corrected. Now there was a documented concern.

**The Anti-Pattern**:
- Waiting for formal reviews to get feedback
- Assuming silence means things are fine
- Not asking specific questions about performance
- Not documenting your own accomplishments

**The Better Approach**:
```markdown
# First 90 Days Feedback Loop

## Week 2: First Check-in
"I've been here a week. Am I ramping up at the
pace you expected? Anything I should adjust?"

## Week 4: Early Feedback
"I want to make sure I'm on track. What am I doing
well? What should I do differently?"

## Week 8: Mid-90-Day Check
"As I approach my first 90 days, how am I doing
against expectations? Any gaps I should address?"

## Week 12: 90-Day Review
"I want to reflect on my first 90 days. What went
well? What should I focus on going forward?"

## Questions to ask:
- "What would 'exceeding expectations' look like?"
- "What should I stop, start, or continue?"
- "Any blind spots I should be aware of?"
- "How can I better support the team?"

## Document everything:
Keep a "brag doc" with your accomplishments
You'll need this for performance reviews
```

**Key Insight**: Early feedback is easy to act on. Late feedback becomes a performance issue. Create feedback loops before problems have time to accumulate.

### Quick Reference: First 90 Days Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|--------------|---------|-----|
| Change too fast | "At my last job..." | Earn credibility first, propose later |
| Isolation | No relationships outside team | Intentional networking in all 90 days |
| Silent struggle | Blocked for days | 15-minute rule, ask with context |
| Code-only focus | "Strong technically, poor culturally" | All dimensions: technical, cultural, process |
| No feedback | Surprised at review | Proactive feedback at weeks 2, 4, 8, 12 |
| No documentation | Forgot what you did | Keep brag doc from day 1 |

### Junior → FAANG: Which Pitfalls Are Hardest to Recover From

Most first-90-days pitfalls are recoverable if caught early. Two are not: breaking production without having asked for review, and being perceived as arrogant about technical decisions before demonstrating competence in the team's codebase.

Breaking production in your first month is recoverable if you handle it correctly: own the incident immediately, do not hide it, participate in the post-mortem constructively, and ship the fix. Engineers who try to minimize or conceal an incident they caused in their first month are rarely forgiven. Engineers who handle it with full ownership are often respected more after the incident than before it.

The arrogance perception is harder to correct because it is often invisible to the person creating it. If you find yourself in conversations where you are explaining how things were done "at my last company" in a way that implies the current approach is inferior, stop. This pattern is extremely common among engineers moving from smaller companies to FAANG, and it consistently produces negative reactions from teammates who built the current system.

### Senior @ FAANG: Production Pitfalls at Senior Level and the Stakes Multiplier

Production pitfalls in the first 90 days are more consequential at senior level because the expectation is that experienced engineers apply their judgment to avoid them, not just respond correctly after the fact. An early-career engineer who deploys without proper review is making a rookie mistake. A senior engineer who does the same is demonstrating a judgment gap that is harder to contextualize.

The senior-specific pitfall that appears most often: driving a significant technical change in the first 90 days before fully understanding the team's deployment and operational risk tolerance. Different teams have dramatically different risk tolerance: some deploy multiple times per day with automated rollback, some have weekly release windows with manual approval gates. A senior engineer who imports their previous team's norms without assessing the current team's norms will create process conflicts that take months to unwind.

Governance practice: in your first two weeks, explicitly ask your manager to describe the team's risk tolerance framework. How are production changes reviewed and who approves them, what is the escalation path when an engineer is uncertain about the blast radius of a change, and what is the team's incident response process. Knowing these answers before you need them is the difference between a senior engineer who operates safely in a new environment and one who creates unnecessary incidents during their first quarter.
