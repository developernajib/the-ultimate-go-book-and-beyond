## 94.12 Level-Specific Onboarding Considerations

What "good onboarding" looks like varies dramatically depending on your experience level. A junior engineer is expected to spend most of their first month learning, while a staff engineer is expected to start forming strategic opinions within weeks. Understanding the expectations for your specific level helps you calibrate your pace, avoid unnecessary anxiety, and focus your energy on the activities that matter most for someone at your career stage. This section breaks down the first 90 days for each major engineering level so you can tailor your approach.

### Junior/Early Career (0-2 years experience)

For engineers early in their career, the primary goal of the first 90 days is building context, understanding the codebase, the deployment process, and the team's working norms, rather than shipping impressive features. Managers at this level measure success by trajectory: are you asking better questions each week, are you requiring less hand-holding on similar tasks, and are you starting to catch your own mistakes before review? Moving deliberately through small, well-scoped tickets while documenting what you learn accelerates this ramp-up far more than trying to take on large, complex work prematurely.

```markdown
# Junior Engineer First 90 Days

## EXPECTATIONS (Lower Than You Think):

### Month 1:
- Learn, don't ship
- Ask lots of questions
- Complete small, well-defined tasks
- Make mistakes (expected and okay)

### Month 2:
- Contribute small fixes and features
- Start code reviewing (for your own learning)
- Understand one area moderately well
- Begin building relationships

### Month 3:
- Regular contributor to team work
- Can work independently on known areas
- Starting to have opinions
- Helping future new hires

## WHAT MANAGERS ACTUALLY WANT FROM JUNIORS:

### Do:
✓ Ask questions (many, often)
✓ Communicate when blocked
✓ Take notes obsessively
✓ Ask for feedback frequently
✓ Be curious about "why"
✓ Admit when you don't know
✓ Document what you learn

### Don't:
✗ Pretend to understand when you don't
✗ Struggle silently for hours
✗ Compare yourself to seniors
✗ Expect to be productive immediately
✗ Skip fundamentals to look advanced
✗ Take criticism personally

## ACCELERATION STRATEGIES:

### Find a Mentor:
Not your manager, someone who:
- Has 2-3+ years more experience
- Is willing to teach
- You can ask "dumb" questions

### Pair Extensively:
- Ask to pair on unfamiliar tasks
- Watch seniors work, ask questions
- Learn patterns by observation

### Read Code More Than You Write:
- Ratio should be 80/20 reading/writing
- Review PRs even if you're not assigned
- Study how experienced people solve problems

### Build Fundamentals:
- Learn your editor deeply
- Master git
- Understand debugging tools
- Learn to write good tests

## COMMON JUNIOR MISTAKES:

1. Comparing to seniors: They have years of context you don't
2. Not asking questions: Seniors expect questions from juniors
3. Going too fast: Learning deeply > shipping quickly
4. Isolation: You need more contact with team, not less
5. Imposter syndrome spiral: Everyone felt this, it's normal
```

### Mid-Level (2-5 years experience)

```markdown
# Mid-Level Engineer First 90 Days

## EXPECTATIONS (Higher Than Junior, But Still Learning):

### Month 1:
- Ramp up faster than juniors (you know how to learn)
- Contribute small features by week 2-3
- Build relationships proactively
- Learn domain AND company culture

### Month 2:
- Independently own medium-sized work
- Contributing to code reviews meaningfully
- Understanding team dynamics and politics
- Starting to influence technical decisions

### Month 3:
- Full contributor
- Can estimate work reasonably
- Starting to mentor juniors
- Clear path to first significant project

## MID-LEVEL SPECIFIC CHALLENGES:

### The "Mid-Level Trap":
At this level, you're expected to be productive but also growing.
Don't just execute - also:
- Understand the "why" behind decisions
- Learn what "senior" looks like at this company
- Identify gaps in your skills
- Build relationships beyond immediate team

### Transferring Skills:
You have experience, but it may not all transfer:

DO TRANSFER:
- Problem-solving approaches
- Debugging skills
- Code review mindset
- Testing practices
- Communication skills

DON'T ASSUME TRANSFERS:
- Domain knowledge
- Team dynamics
- Company-specific patterns
- Tool choices
- Process preferences

### Avoiding the "We Did It This Way" Problem:
Your experience is valuable, but:
- Learn why things are done this way first
- Propose improvements later, with context
- Frame suggestions as "have you considered" not "you should"

## WHAT TO FOCUS ON:

### Technical:
- Learn the domain deeply
- Understand system architecture
- Build broad knowledge of team's scope
- Know adjacent systems

### Professional:
- Build network across org
- Understand promotion criteria
- Find mentors in senior roles
- Learn company decision-making

### Leadership (Preparing for Senior):
- Start helping juniors
- Take ownership of problems
- Communicate proactively
- Document your learnings for others
```

### Senior Engineer (5+ years experience)

```markdown
# Senior Engineer First 90 Days

## EXPECTATIONS (High, But Still Have Time):

### Month 1:
- Understand team's domain and systems
- Build key relationships
- Identify opportunities for impact
- Deliver first contribution (smaller scope okay)

### Month 2:
- Contributing independently on significant work
- Providing valuable code reviews
- Starting to influence technical direction
- Building cross-team relationships

### Month 3:
- Owning major workstream or project
- Mentoring others on team
- Proposing improvements with credibility
- Clear technical direction for next quarter

## SENIOR-SPECIFIC CHALLENGES:

### The "Senior at Last Company" Problem:
You were senior there, but here you're new.

WHAT TO RECALIBRATE:
- Decision-making authority (lower until you earn it)
- Technical credibility (must be rebuilt)
- Relationship capital (starts at zero)
- Domain knowledge (new domain)

### Balancing Act:
❌ Too passive: "I'm just learning, don't want to step on toes"
❌ Too aggressive: "At my last company, we did it better"
✓ Right balance: "I've learned X, curious about Y, could we consider Z?"

### First 30 Days as Senior:
1. Listen more than talk
2. Ask "why" before "why not X"
3. Find quick wins that don't require much context
4. Build relationships with key people
5. Understand decision-making processes

### Days 30-60:
1. Start having (private) opinions
2. Propose small improvements
3. Take on meaty project
4. Begin mentoring
5. Contribute to technical discussions

### Days 60-90:
1. Drive technical decisions in your area
2. Propose larger improvements
3. Lead a significant effort
4. Have visible impact
5. Establish yourself as go-to person for something

## WHAT SENIOR ENGINEERS SHOULD ASK:

### Week 1:
1. "What are the biggest technical challenges facing the team?"
2. "What decisions have been deferred that I could help with?"
3. "Where do you see opportunity for improvement?"
4. "What should I know about team dynamics?"

### Week 2-4:
1. "What would the ideal solution look like for X?"
2. "What constraints led to the current approach?"
3. "Who are the key stakeholders for technical decisions?"
4. "What's the appetite for change?"

### Month 2-3:
1. "Would it be helpful if I led [initiative]?"
2. "I've noticed X - would it be valuable to address?"
3. "How can I be most useful to the team?"
4. "What should my goals be for next quarter?"

## SENIOR ONBOARDING ANTI-PATTERNS:

### 1. "The Expert Who Knows Better"
Behavior: Immediately criticizing everything
Reality: You don't have context, even if you're right
Fix: Earn credibility first, propose later

### 2. "The Passive Senior"
Behavior: Acting like a junior, waiting to be told what to do
Reality: You're expected to be more proactive
Fix: Propose, don't wait; lead, don't follow

### 3. "The Isolated Contributor"
Behavior: Heads down on code, no relationship building
Reality: Senior impact comes through influence
Fix: Build relationships intentionally

### 4. "The Process Imposer"
Behavior: Trying to implement your old company's process
Reality: Process change requires trust and context
Fix: Understand first, then propose adaptations
```

### Staff+ Engineer Onboarding

```markdown
# Staff+ Engineer First 90 Days

## THE UNIQUE CHALLENGE:

At Staff+ level, your job is fundamentally different:
- Less about personal coding, more about organizational impact
- You're paid for judgment, not just execution
- Your influence comes from trust, which starts at zero
- You must figure out WHAT to do, not just HOW

## 90-DAY FRAMEWORK FOR STAFF+:

### Days 1-30: Listen and Learn

GOALS:
- Understand organizational structure and politics
- Map technical landscape and key systems
- Identify the pain points people complain about
- Build relationships with decision-makers
- Understand what "success" means at this company

ACTIVITIES:
- 1:1s with all key stakeholders
- Read every design doc from past year
- Attend all architecture/tech lead meetings
- Shadow on-call for different teams
- Review incident postmortems

QUESTIONS TO ANSWER:
- What are the biggest technical challenges?
- What's been tried before? Why did it fail?
- Who are the key technical decision-makers?
- What's the relationship between teams?
- Where are the political landmines?

### Days 30-60: Form Opinions and Test Them

GOALS:
- Have a point of view on important topics
- Test your understanding with trusted peers
- Identify 2-3 high-impact opportunities
- Build coalition for change

ACTIVITIES:
- Write up your observations (share with manager)
- Have "what if" conversations with key people
- Propose small improvements to build trust
- Start contributing to strategic discussions

QUESTIONS TO ANSWER:
- What's the highest leverage change I could drive?
- Who needs to be convinced?
- What's the risk if I'm wrong?
- What's the sequencing?

### Days 60-90: Drive Impact

GOALS:
- Lead or significantly influence a major initiative
- Establish yourself as an authority in your area
- Have clear goals for the next 6 months
- Be seen as a valuable addition to leadership

ACTIVITIES:
- Propose and start major initiative
- Mentor senior engineers
- Write influential design docs or tech specs
- Present at team/org all-hands

## STAFF+ ONBOARDING TIPS:

### Build Social Capital Intentionally:
- Meet with every Staff+ and above
- Understand org chart and informal power structures
- Find allies who can support your initiatives
- Understand historical conflicts and alliances

### Navigate Politics:
- Understand who has power and influence
- Learn what's been tried and failed
- Identify who might oppose changes and why
- Build relationships before proposing changes

### Establish Credibility:
- Ship something meaningful (even if small)
- Have insightful comments in design reviews
- Solve a hard problem someone else couldn't
- Help others succeed

### Avoid Common Staff+ Mistakes:
❌ Coming in with "the answer" before understanding the problem
❌ Alienating existing technical leaders
❌ Focusing on technical solutions without organizational buy-in
❌ Underestimating the importance of relationships
❌ Moving too fast without building coalition
```

### Junior → FAANG: Using Level Expectations to Avoid the Most Common First-Year Failure

The level-specific expectations in this section define the performance bar you will be evaluated against at your first formal review. Reading them before your onboarding begins lets you calibrate your daily decisions: is what I am doing today consistent with what an engineer at my level is expected to be doing at this stage.

The most common first-year failure at early career is misreading the level expectations as a ceiling rather than a floor. If the expectations for L4 say "understands one system well enough to make changes independently," the engineers who get strong performance reviews are not the ones who barely meet this bar: they are the ones who exceed it by also starting to contribute to code reviews, asking good questions in design discussions, and demonstrating increasing ownership of their project area. Understanding the bar as a minimum, not a maximum, changes how you invest your time.

### Senior @ FAANG: Staff-Level Onboarding and the Coalition-Building Imperative

The anti-pattern at staff level that this section identifies (moving too fast without building coalition) is the most consequential failure mode in senior onboarding and deserves extended treatment.

Coalition-building is not political maneuvering: it is the recognition that significant technical changes in large organizations require organizational alignment as a precondition for success. A staff engineer who proposes a major architectural change in month two without having established that their technical judgment is trusted, without having understood the competing priorities that make the change difficult, and without having built relationships with the engineers who will be asked to execute the change is optimizing for being right while ignoring the requirement to be effective.

The maturity model for staff-level onboarding: month one is observation and question-asking with no proposals. Month two is sharing observations in collaborative framing, not proposal framing ("I've noticed X, I'm curious whether that's been a discussion point on the team"). Month three is proposing a specific intervention on a scoped problem where you have already established that the team sees the problem and trusts your ability to help address it. This cadence feels slow but it produces durable change. Faster cadences produce technically interesting proposals that never ship.
