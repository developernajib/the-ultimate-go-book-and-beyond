## 94.3 Building Relationships

Building relationships is arguably more important than writing code during your first 90 days. The engineers who onboard most successfully are the ones who invest early in getting to know their teammates, stakeholders, and leadership, because those relationships determine which projects you get assigned to, how quickly you get unblocked, and how your work is perceived. You should approach relationship-building with the same intentionality you bring to learning a new codebase: have a plan, prioritize the most important connections first, and schedule time for it rather than hoping it happens naturally.

### Key People to Meet

Prioritizing by tier prevents the common mistake of scheduling too many introductions at once, which dilutes each conversation and leaves you without the context to ask meaningful questions. Meeting your manager and direct teammates first gives you the organizational frame you need to make every subsequent conversation more productive, you will understand the team's priorities, its pain points, and its vocabulary before you sit down with stakeholders or skip-level leadership.

```
FIRST PRIORITY (Week 1-2):
• Your manager
• Your team members
• Your onboarding buddy
• Immediate stakeholders

SECOND PRIORITY (Week 2-4):
• Skip-level manager
• Adjacent team leads
• Product manager(s)
• Key engineers from dependent teams

THIRD PRIORITY (Month 2-3):
• Senior leadership
• Cross-functional partners
• Mentors in other areas
```

### 1:1 Conversation Starters

Open-ended questions like these surface the undocumented knowledge that lives only in people's heads, the historical decisions behind a confusing API, the political sensitivity around a particular service, or the unwritten norms that determine whether a PR gets approved or stalls for weeks. Asking "what should I know that isn't documented?" signals intellectual humility while extracting the tacit knowledge that no onboarding doc will ever capture.

```
FOR PEERS:
• What are you working on?
• What's your background?
• What do you like most about working here?
• What should I know that isn't documented?
• What advice do you have for someone new?

FOR STAKEHOLDERS:
• What does your team do?
• How do we work together?
• What are your biggest challenges?
• What can I do to make our collaboration successful?

FOR LEADERSHIP:
• What are the team's priorities this quarter?
• What does success look like for the team?
• What concerns do you have about the team?
• What should I focus on?
```

### Junior → FAANG: Relationship Building as a Career Multiplier

Early-career engineers at FAANG companies who build strong relationships with senior engineers on their team receive measurably better mentorship, more interesting projects, and more generous performance evaluations than equally capable engineers who do not invest in these relationships. This is not a claim about politics: it is a claim about information flow. Senior engineers who know you and trust your judgment share context, flag opportunities, and advocate for you in settings you do not have access to.

The most effective relationship-building approach at early career is being genuinely helpful. Offer to help with code reviews. Take on the documentation update that nobody wants to do. Ask your teammates about the parts of the system they are proud of and genuinely engage with the answer. Helpfulness and curiosity are the fastest paths to being trusted by people whose trust you have not yet had time to earn through demonstrated judgment.

Practical target: by the end of week four, have had at least one substantive conversation with every person on your immediate team. Substantive means you know something about what they are working on, what they find interesting, and what they find frustrating.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Relationship Strategy and Influence Network Design

At senior level, relationship building is a deliberate organizational strategy, not a social activity. The engineers who operate effectively at L6 have a mapped understanding of who controls decisions in their domain and the adjacent domains they need to influence, and they invest in those relationships with intentional regularity.

Identify five key stakeholders in your first month: your manager, your skip-level manager, the most influential technical peer on your team, the most influential technical peer in the adjacent team you are most likely to collaborate with, and one senior product or engineering manager who controls the roadmap for your area. Schedule a recurring 30-minute conversation with each of these people in your first 90 days. These conversations do not need an agenda beyond understanding their perspective on priorities and technical direction.

The governance principle: influence at senior level is built on credibility, and credibility is built on a track record of making good calls in situations where the outcome was uncertain. The relationship network you build in your first 90 days determines who observes those calls and who advocates for your credibility in the rooms you cannot be in.
