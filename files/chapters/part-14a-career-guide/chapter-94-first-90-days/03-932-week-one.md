## 94.2 Week One

Your first week sets the tone for your entire onboarding experience. The primary goal is not to write code, it is to get your environment running, meet the people you will be working with, and understand the immediate expectations your manager has for you. Most new hires underestimate how much administrative and access setup is required on day one, so having a checklist ensures nothing falls through the cracks. Completing these basics quickly frees you to focus on learning the codebase and team dynamics for the rest of the month.

### Day 1 Checklist

```
ADMINISTRATIVE:
□ Complete HR paperwork
□ Get badge/access
□ Set up laptop and accounts
□ Access email and calendar
□ Join required Slack/Teams channels

TEAM:
□ Meet your manager
□ Meet your team members
□ Get team meeting schedule
□ Understand immediate expectations

SETUP:
□ Development environment
□ Code repository access
□ Documentation access
□ VPN and tools

QUESTIONS FOR YOUR MANAGER:
• What are your expectations for my first 30 days?
• What should my first project be?
• Who are the key people I should meet?
• What does success look like in this role?
```

### First Week Goals

Treat this checklist as a contract with yourself rather than a suggestion. The first week is uniquely low-stakes, you are not yet expected to ship features, so it is the safest time to ask foundational questions, explore the repository without pressure, and establish the habits (daily check-ins, note-taking, proactive communication) that will define how you work for months to come. Missing these goals in week one means compensating for the gaps under deadline pressure later.

```
□ Complete all onboarding requirements
□ Have your dev environment working
□ Clone and explore key repositories
□ Read team documentation
□ Meet everyone on your immediate team
□ Attend all scheduled meetings
□ Have daily check-ins with your buddy/manager
□ Start your first small task
```

### Junior → FAANG: Week One Priorities and the Setup Investment

Week one at a FAANG company involves more administrative complexity than most early-career engineers expect. Access provisioning, security compliance training, equipment setup, and onboarding sessions from HR and legal all compete for time with technical setup. The engineers who handle week one most effectively treat it as purely logistical: the goal is to have a working development environment, understand the deployment process at a high level, and have met their immediate teammates. Nothing more.

The instinct to contribute code in week one is almost always counterproductive. You do not yet understand the norms well enough to contribute correctly, and a failed pull request in week one from a new hire creates a worse first impression than no pull request at all. Resist it.

One practical tip: write down every question you have in week one in a running document. Ask your onboarding buddy or manager a batch of these questions in your first one-on-one rather than interrupting people ad hoc. Batch questioning is more respectful of other people's time and also gives you a record of your onboarding questions that is useful when you are eventually in the position of onboarding someone else.

### Senior @ FAANG: Senior Week One: Stakeholder Identification and Early Observation

At senior level, week one should include an explicit stakeholder identification exercise alongside the standard setup. Before you can drive any meaningful technical change, you need to understand the organizational landscape: who makes architectural decisions, whose opinion carries informal weight that does not match their title, which adjacent teams are most likely to be affected by changes you might drive, and where the current technical debt is concentrated.

The most effective senior onboarding practice in week one is a listening tour: schedule 30-minute conversations with six to eight people across the team and adjacent teams. Ask the same core questions to each: what is the team working on that excites you most, what is the biggest technical challenge you face, and is there anything a new senior engineer should understand about how this team operates that is not written down anywhere. The answers will be inconsistent, which is itself important information about where the team lacks alignment.

Governance signal: a senior engineer who arrives and immediately starts proposing solutions has failed to understand that their first week-one job is information gathering. The most respected senior engineers in their new organizations typically describe their first month as "I said almost nothing and listened to almost everything," followed by "then I had something worth saying."
