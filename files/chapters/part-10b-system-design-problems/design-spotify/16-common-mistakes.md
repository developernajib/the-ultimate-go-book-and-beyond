## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

These mistakes show up repeatedly in interviews. Each one reveals a gap in understanding how audio streaming, recommendations, or rights management work at production scale.

1. **Full files instead of segments.** Streaming entire files wastes bandwidth every time a user skips. A skip 30 seconds into a 4-minute track throws away roughly seven eighths of what you shipped, and skips are a large fraction of all plays. Segmentation also enables adaptive bitrate: without it, you cannot switch quality mid-track when the network degrades.
2. **No CDN caching strategy.** Saying "put it on a CDN" without addressing the long-tail distribution of music misses the point. You cannot cache 80M songs at every edge node. The top 1% of tracks need proactive push. The rest rely on origin fetch with edge caching on first access. Without this distinction, cold starts for less popular tracks create unacceptable latency spikes.
3. **Brute-force similarity for recommendations.** Computing cosine similarity between a user vector and all 80 million track vectors takes seconds. At 200M daily users, that is computationally infeasible. Approximate Nearest Neighbor libraries (Annoy, FAISS) reduce lookup time to milliseconds by trading a small amount of recall for orders-of-magnitude speedup.
4. **Wrong play counting for royalties.** A play only counts for royalty purposes if the user listens for at least 30 seconds. Simply counting "stream started" events would overcount plays (users who skip after 5 seconds) and result in incorrect royalty payments, a financial and legal liability. The system must track actual listen duration per play event.
5. **Single search index for all content types.** Songs, artists, albums, playlists, and podcasts have different schemas and different relevance signals. A single flat index cannot apply type-specific boosting (e.g., exact artist name match should rank higher than a song title match). Separate indices per content type with a query-time merge give better results.
6. **No offline sync for premium users.** Premium subscribers expect to download music for offline listening on planes and subways. This requires encrypted downloads (AES-GCM keyed to the device), device-limit enforcement (typically 5 devices per account), and license expiry (downloads become unplayable after 30 days without re-authentication).

7. **Not handling licensing expiry.** Tracks can lose licensing in specific regions overnight when a label agreement ends or a rights dispute arises. Without a real-time rights management system, users in affected regions can still find and play tracks they should not have access to, creating legal liability. The system needs a rights service that propagates takedowns within minutes: removing the track from search results, blocking new stream requests, and invalidating CDN-cached segments.

8. **Monolithic recommendation model.** Different product surfaces need different recommendation strategies: homepage discovery emphasizes novelty and diversity, radio mode emphasizes genre consistency, search suggestions prioritize exact-match relevance, and "made for you" playlists balance familiarity with exploration. A single collaborative filtering model cannot optimize for all these objectives simultaneously. Production systems use multiple specialized models that share the same underlying user and track embeddings but apply different ranking objectives.

9. **Ignoring audio normalization.** Without loudness normalization, switching from a quietly mastered jazz track to a loudly mastered pop song produces a jarring volume jump. The ITU-R BS.1770 standard defines how to measure perceived loudness. During transcoding, the pipeline computes each track's integrated loudness (in LUFS) and stores it as metadata, and the client applies a per-track gain adjustment at playback time so all tracks sound equally loud against a platform target (Spotify normalizes to -14 LUFS).
