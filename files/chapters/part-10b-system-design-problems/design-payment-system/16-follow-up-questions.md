## Follow-up Questions

Interviewers often ask follow-up questions to test the depth of your understanding beyond the core payment flow. The answers below are intentionally concise, mirroring the level of detail you would give in a 5-minute follow-up discussion. Each answer hits the key points an interviewer expects without going into full implementation detail.

**Q: Chargebacks?** A chargeback occurs when a customer disputes a charge through their card issuer. The process: the card network (Visa/Mastercard) notifies the payment platform, funds are immediately held from the merchant's balance, and the merchant submits evidence (receipts, shipping confirmation, communication logs) by a deadline the processor sets, typically a week or two, inside the network's longer response window. The issuer makes the final decision. If the merchant loses, the held funds go back to the customer and the merchant also pays a chargeback fee, commonly around $15. Note the asymmetry that makes disputes worth engineering for: the fee is charged whether or not the merchant wins, so a disputed payment costs money even when the merchant is entirely in the right.

Monitor the dispute rate per merchant, because sustained rates put the merchant into a network monitoring program with fees and, eventually, loss of card acceptance. Get the specifics right if you cite them: Visa retired the old Dispute Monitoring Program (with its familiar 0.9% threshold) in April 2025, replacing it with the Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP), which measures a combined fraud-plus-dispute ratio rather than disputes alone. The merchant "Excessive" threshold tightened to 1.5% in April 2026, above a minimum monthly event count. The number to remember for an interview is not the exact percentage, which moves, but that the thresholds are enforced against your acquirer, who passes the pressure to you, which is why a payment platform must monitor this per merchant and act on it before the network does.

**Q: PCI DSS?** The system achieves SAQ A (Self-Assessment Questionnaire A), the lightest compliance level, by never storing, processing, or transmitting raw card data. Card numbers flow directly from the customer's browser to the processor via their client-side SDK (Stripe.js, Adyen Drop-in). The payment platform only handles tokenized references. All communication uses TLS 1.3 with modern cipher suites. Under PCI DSS v4.0, mandatory since April 2025, SAQ A is no longer the free pass it once was: merchants whose own pages host the redirect or embed the processor's payment form now owe quarterly ASV scans and script-integrity controls alongside the annual self-assessment. The reason is worth knowing, because it is a genuinely instructive attack. Magecart-style compromises never touched the card data at rest, they injected a script into the merchant's own checkout page and skimmed the card as the customer typed it, before the redirect. Outsourcing card handling moved your risk, it did not delete it, and the page that loads the payment form is still yours to defend. If you ever need to process raw card data, for example for phone orders, you must upgrade to SAQ D, which requires substantially more controls and, above certain volumes, on-site assessment by a QSA.

**Q: Manual review queue?** Not all fraud decisions are binary. Transactions with a fraud score below 0.3 are auto-approved (low risk), those above 0.85 are auto-blocked (high risk), and those between 0.3 and 0.85 are routed to a manual review queue. The SLA for manual review is 30 minutes, longer delays cause poor merchant experience because their customers are waiting for order confirmation. The review team sees all fraud signals (velocity features, ML score breakdown, customer history) and makes a judgment call. Their decisions feed back into the ML model as training data, continuously improving the automated scoring.

**Q: Exactly-once?** Five mechanisms layer together, and the useful framing is that each one catches what the one before it misses: (1) the merchant provides an idempotency key with every request, so retries replay the cached result, (2) a Redis distributed lock (SETNX with TTL) collapses two concurrent copies of the same request so they cannot both miss the cache, (3) a `UNIQUE` constraint on the idempotency key in PostgreSQL, which is the actual guarantee, because the cache can evict and the lock's TTL can expire while its holder is still working, and neither of those may result in a second charge, (4) the platform's transaction ID is used as the processor's idempotency key, so a retry against the processor returns the original charge rather than creating a second, and (5) a background reconciliation job queries the processor for every transaction stuck in "pending" past 5 minutes, resolving the crashes that happen between the charge and the write.

Say "effectively exactly-once" and know why the hedge is honest. You cannot achieve true exactly-once across a network boundary you do not control. What you build is at-most-once at the card network, via idempotency keys, plus at-least-once recovery, via reconciliation, and the two together converge on exactly-once given time. The gap in between is real and measured in minutes, which is precisely why the transaction row is written before the charge: it is the evidence that lets reconciliation close the gap.

**Q: Merchant payouts?** Payouts run as a nightly batch job. The available balance is computed as: captured payments minus refunds minus platform fees minus any holds (for disputes or risk review). Funds are transferred via ACH (US), SEPA (EU), or local rail (other regions). A minimum threshold of $25 prevents uneconomical transfers (bank transfer fees apply). Settlement timing is typically 1-3 business days from the payout initiation. High-volume merchants may negotiate same-day or next-day settlement. The payout job records a corresponding ledger entry (debit merchant available balance, credit merchant bank account) to maintain audit trail integrity.

**Q: How do you handle a payment processor that returns success but your database write fails?** This is a critical edge case. The processor has charged the customer but your system has no record. Solutions: (1) WAL-style logging before processor call, (2) Reconciliation job that queries processor for recent transactions and creates missing records, (3) Idempotent retry with the same processor reference. The reconciliation pipeline catches any gaps.

**Q: How would you implement subscription billing?** Subscription engine stores plan, billing cycle, and next charge date. A scheduler triggers charges on the billing date. Handle failed charges with retry policy (retry days 1, 3, 5, then suspend). Proration for mid-cycle upgrades/downgrades. Dunning emails for failed payments. Invoice generation with line items.

**Q: How do you prevent refund fraud?** Validate that refund amount does not exceed original payment. Track cumulative refunds per payment. Require manager approval for refunds above threshold. Monitor refund velocity per merchant and per customer. Automated flags for merchants with refund rates above 5%.

**Q: How do you handle multi-party payments (marketplace)?** Split payments into sub-payments per seller. Each sub-payment has its own lifecycle. Platform takes a commission (separate ledger entry). Escrow pattern: hold funds until delivery confirmed, then release to seller. Requires separate ledger accounts per marketplace seller.

**Q: How would you support cryptocurrency payments?** Separate payment processor adapter for blockchain transactions. Handle confirmation delays (Bitcoin: 10-60 minutes for 6 confirmations). FX rate locking with expiry (crypto price quoted for 15 minutes). Wallet management for hot/cold storage. Different settlement model (on-chain settlement vs traditional T+1).

**Q: How do you handle regulatory requirements across countries?** PSD2 Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) for EU payments, implemented via 3D Secure 2 for card-not-present, which shifts liability for fraudulent chargebacks to the issuer when the customer authenticates. Know the exemptions, since they are where the engineering lives: low-value, transaction risk analysis, and merchant-initiated transactions can skip the challenge, and the design question is that requesting an exemption trades a conversion win against keeping the liability. Different tax calculation per jurisdiction. Data residency requirements (EU data stays in EU). Anti-money laundering (AML) transaction monitoring with suspicious activity reporting.
