## Interview Follow-Ups

These questions test your understanding of the critical trade-offs in exchange design: latency versus durability, availability versus correctness, and performance versus regulatory compliance.

**Q: How do you ensure sub-millisecond matching latency?**

The entire matching critical path is designed to avoid anything with unpredictable latency. The order book is fully in-memory (heap-based), so no database reads or writes occur during matching. One goroutine per symbol eliminates lock contention, the only synchronization is the channel send from the order gateway to the matching goroutine, which is sub-microsecond. The WAL write is the only I/O operation on the critical path: it uses `O_DIRECT` to bypass the OS page cache (eliminating write-back latency variability) and `fsync` to guarantee the data reaches stable storage. On modern NVMe SSDs, this WAL write completes in under 50 microseconds. The matching algorithm itself (heap insertion/deletion) is O(log n) where n is the number of orders at a price level, completing in under 1 microsecond for typical book depths. Total end-to-end matching latency: under 500 microseconds. Go's garbage collector is the main risk to deterministic latency, production deployments tune `GOGC` and use `GOMEMLIMIT` to minimize GC pauses.

**Q: How do you handle the matching engine failing?**

A hot standby matching engine continuously replays the same WAL stream as the primary. Because the matching logic is deterministic (same input sequence always produces the same output), the standby's in-memory order book is an exact replica of the primary's. On primary failure (detected by missed heartbeats after a configurable timeout), the standby promotes itself to primary. A distributed lock (etcd or ZooKeeper) with a fencing token prevents split-brain: if the old primary recovers, its stale epoch number causes the order gateway to reject its commands. A standby that is already following the stream fails over in seconds, because its book is current. The 30-second figure is the cold-start path: loading the most recent snapshot and replaying the WAL written since it. That budget is what sets the snapshot interval. Replay runs at about 500,000 entries per second and the 2GB snapshot takes roughly 5 seconds to load, leaving 25 seconds, or 12.5 million entries. At a peak of 1M orders/sec that means snapshotting every 10 seconds. During failover, the exchange briefly stops accepting new orders (a "trading halt" of a few seconds), which is acceptable because exchanges have built-in mechanisms for resuming after halts.

**Q: How do you prevent a rogue algorithm from manipulating prices?**

Market manipulation prevention operates at multiple levels. Circuit breakers (LULD, Limit Up/Limit Down) prevent trades from printing outside a price band computed around a reference price, which is the average trade price over the preceding five minutes. The band is 5% for Tier 1 securities (S&P 500, Russell 1000, and selected ETPs) and 10% for other NMS securities above $3, and it widens near the open and close where price discovery is genuinely more volatile. Note the mechanism: the band does not halt the stock on a fast move, it stops trades printing through it. Only if the security sits in a limit state for 15 seconds without moving back inside the band does trading pause, for five minutes. Per-trader rate limiting caps order submission at 1,000 orders per second, preventing any single participant from flooding the matching engine. Wash trades are blocked at the source by the matching engine's self-trade prevention, which never crosses a trader against their own resting order. Surveillance still runs wash trade detection on top of that, because the version worth catching is coordinated across related accounts that the engine sees as different traders, and its purpose is to manufacture fake volume. Spoofing detection watches for orders layered on one side and pulled the moment the opposite side reacts, followed by that same trader executing on the other side. Resist the tempting shortcut of flagging a high cancel-to-trade ratio on its own: market makers cancel the overwhelming majority of their orders as a matter of course, so the ratio alone indicts the liquidity providers and misses the manipulation. All flagged events are logged to a surveillance database for compliance review, and repeated violations trigger automatic account suspension.
