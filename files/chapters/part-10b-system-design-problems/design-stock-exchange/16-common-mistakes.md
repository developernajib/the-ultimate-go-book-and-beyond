## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

These mistakes are the most common reasons candidates fail stock exchange design interviews. Each one reflects a misunderstanding of either the performance constraints or the correctness requirements of financial systems.

1. **Floating-point prices.** Be precise about why, because the usual hand-wave ("floats are non-deterministic") is not true and an interviewer may know it. IEEE 754 addition of two `float64` values is correctly rounded and perfectly reproducible: two machines adding the same pair get bit-identical results. The real problem is that decimal prices are not representable in binary at all, and it bites immediately. A trader improves a bid of `$0.05` by a penny, and `0.05 + 0.01` is `0.060000000000000005`, while the `0.06` sitting on the book is `0.059999999999999998`. Those are different numbers. The two orders display the same price, and the engine does not believe they are at the same price. Sweep every penny increment from $0.01 to $200.00 and 6,515 of the 20,000 land somewhere other than the price they name. That is what destroys a price level, and with it the price-time priority that is the entire product. Accumulated fills drift the same way: adding `0.1` ten times gives `0.99999999999999989`, so the fills stop summing to the order quantity. Divergence between primary and standby is a real risk too once a compiler contracts expressions into FMA or reassociates them, but a book breaks long before you get there. All prices and quantities must be integer cents or ticks.

    One Go-specific trap worth knowing, since it is how people convince themselves floats are fine: `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` evaluates to `true` in Go when written literally. Untyped constant expressions are folded at compile time with arbitrary precision, so that test measures the compiler and not the arithmetic. Assign them to `float64` variables first and the same comparison is `false`.

2. **Global lock on order book.** Using a single mutex for all 10,000 symbols means that matching AAPL blocks matching GOOGL, even though they are completely independent. Each symbol needs its own goroutine (or at minimum its own lock). With Go's lightweight goroutines, 10,000 goroutines are trivial, the runtime handles them efficiently with work-stealing schedulers.

3. **No WAL before matching.** If the system matches a trade and then crashes before persisting it, the trade is lost. Both the buyer and seller believe they executed a trade, but the exchange has no record. The fix is to write every order to the WAL before running the matching algorithm. The WAL write adds 50 microseconds of latency (NVMe with O_DIRECT) but guarantees that the order can be replayed on recovery.

4. **Market orders resting on book.** A market order that cannot be fully filled (because the opposite side of the book is empty or does not have enough volume) should be canceled, not left resting on the book. A resting market order has no price, so it would match at any price when a counterparty arrives, potentially executing at a wildly unfavorable price. Limit orders are the only order type that should rest on the book.

5. **Confusing lossy market data with lossy trades.** Order book updates can be lossy when each update is a complete snapshot, because a missed update is simply superseded by the next one. Say that condition out loud, since it is the part that makes the design sound: a delta feed may not be dropped silently, and that is why real feeds number every message per symbol so a client can spot the gap and re-sync. Trade executions cannot be lossy because they have legal and financial significance. A missed trade notification means a trader does not know they own a position, which can lead to unhedged risk. Trades must be delivered via a guaranteed-delivery channel (Kafka with persistence).

6. **No pre-trade risk checks.** Without margin validation and position limits, a rogue algorithm (or a hacked account) can submit orders that create positions far exceeding the trader's capital. A single erroneous order for 1 million shares of a $100 stock creates a $100 million exposure. Pre-trade risk checks (margin sufficiency, maximum order size, maximum position per symbol) must run before the order enters the matching engine.

7. **Forgetting settlement.** A matched trade is not complete until settlement, which for US equities is T+1 (one business day after the trade date). Settlement involves the clearing house netting all positions, the seller delivering securities, and the buyer delivering cash. Candidates who stop at matching and do not mention settlement or clearing miss a critical part of the system.

8. **Single matching engine without failover.** The matching engine is a single logical point of failure, and a production exchange cannot stop trading while someone restarts a process. The standard design is a hot standby that consumes the same input sequence as the primary and maintains an identical order book (deterministic replay makes this possible), so failover is a promotion rather than a rebuild and completes in seconds, dominated by how long the heartbeat takes to declare the primary dead rather than by any state transfer. This is also why determinism (integer prices, sequenced inputs) is not a nicety: without it, the standby's book diverges and failover corrupts the market.

9. **Using HTTP for institutional order flow.** Institutional brokers connect over persistent TCP sessions speaking the FIX protocol, which provides session-level sequence numbers, gap detection, and replay semantics that REST cannot. Forcing brokers through an HTTP gateway adds connection-establishment latency on the critical path and loses the session recovery guarantees the industry depends on. REST is fine for retail-facing APIs, never for the institutional gateway.

10. **Ignoring GC pauses.** A garbage-collected language on the matching path means occasional multi-millisecond pauses, an eternity when matching latency is measured in microseconds. Go engines mitigate with object pooling (sync.Pool for order structs), pre-allocated ring buffers instead of per-message allocation, GOGC and GOMEMLIMIT tuning, and keeping the hot path allocation-free. If you cannot show the interviewer where allocations happen in your matching loop, you have not designed for the latency target.
