## Follow-up Questions

These questions test knowledge of exchange mechanics beyond the core matching engine. Each one represents a significant feature area that a real exchange must support.

**Q: Opening/closing auction?** Continuous matching (the normal order book) does not work well at market open because there is no established price, the first random order would set the price, creating an unfair advantage. Instead, exchanges use a call auction: orders are collected during a pre-open period (typically 15-30 minutes before the opening bell), and a single clearing price is calculated that maximizes total matched volume. All matched orders execute at this uniform price simultaneously, establishing a fair opening price with maximum liquidity. The closing auction uses the same mechanism and is critically important because the closing price serves as the reference price for ETF net asset value calculations, options settlement, and index composition.

**Q: Spoofing detection?** Spoofing is placing orders you intend to cancel to create an illusion of supply or demand, manipulating other participants into trading at unfavorable prices. The trap in this answer is reaching for a cancel-to-fill ratio as the detector. A high cancel rate is not evidence of anything: market makers legitimately cancel well over 95% of their orders as they requote against a moving market, so that threshold flags the participants providing most of the liquidity and catches no one. The signal is intent, and intent shows up in the shape of the behavior rather than in the ratio. What surveillance actually looks for is the pattern: orders layered on one side at progressively better prices, pulled the instant the opposite side reacts, followed by the same trader executing on the other side, which is the part that reveals the display was never meant to trade. Real-time pattern detection runs via Flink over the order event stream, flagging candidates for compliance review, and a human decides. Confirmed spoofing results in account suspension and regulatory referral.

Worth knowing precisely where the law sits, because candidates routinely misattribute it: Dodd-Frank's anti-spoofing provision (Section 747) amended the Commodity Exchange Act and covers futures, enforced by the CFTC, and it requires proving intent to cancel before execution. There is no equivalent spoofing statute in the federal securities laws, so on a stock exchange the SEC charges the conduct as manipulation under the general anti-fraud provisions, Exchange Act Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5.

**Q: Stock splits?** When a company announces a 2-for-1 split, every share becomes two shares at half the price. The exchange processes this as an overnight batch: all open orders for that symbol have their quantities multiplied by the split ratio and prices divided by the split ratio, all position records are updated, and LULD circuit breaker bands are recalculated for the new price level. This operation must be atomic, either all adjustments succeed or none do, to prevent a partially updated order book. Matching is paused during the maintenance window (typically 30 minutes before market open) to prevent orders from executing at stale prices.

**Q: Options trading?** Options add enormous complexity to exchange design. Each underlying stock may have 100,000+ option contracts (combinations of strike prices and expiration dates), each needing its own order book. Exercise and assignment logic runs at expiration: in-the-money options are automatically exercised, and the clearing house assigns counterparties randomly from the pool of short option holders. Risk management requires real-time Greeks calculation (delta, gamma, vega, theta) to assess portfolio exposure. Complex order types like spreads (simultaneous buy/sell of related options) and straddles (buy call and put at the same strike) require atomic multi-leg matching. Portfolio margining, which evaluates risk across all positions rather than per-position, requires a real-time risk engine that recalculates margin requirements on every trade.

**Q: Network partition between primary and standby matching engines?** The danger is split-brain, both nodes believing they are primary and processing orders. Fencing tokens solve it: the standby must acquire a higher epoch number from a consensus service (etcd or ZooKeeper) before accepting orders, and every downstream system rejects messages carrying a stale epoch. The old primary can keep matching in isolation, but nothing it produces is accepted, so the market sees exactly one authoritative engine.

**Q: How would you implement a dark pool?** A dark pool hides order book depth: participants see no resting orders, and matches execute at the midpoint of the public market's NBBO (National Best Bid and Offer). It runs as a separate matching engine with midpoint-only crossing logic, no pre-trade transparency, and post-trade reporting to the consolidated tape. The design conversation is about the trade-off: institutions avoid market impact for large blocks, at the cost of fragmenting public price discovery.

**Q: Market data during extreme volatility?** Conflation and prioritization. During bursts, aggregate book updates into periodic snapshots (publish every 10ms rather than every change) and offer conflated feeds for slower consumers, the latest state supersedes missed intermediate states. The invariant to state explicitly: trade confirmations ride a separate guaranteed-delivery channel and are never queued behind market data backlog.

**Q: Regulatory audit trail requirements?** Every order event (submit, modify, cancel, fill, reject) is persisted with nanosecond timestamps, immutable and retained for years. The WAL is the source of truth, archived append-only to object storage, and the surveillance engine runs in parallel with matching for real-time manipulation detection. The bar to articulate: a regulator can request a full deterministic replay of any trading day, and the WAL plus sequenced inputs makes that possible.

**Q: Cross-listed securities?** A security trading on multiple exchanges requires a consolidated view of the best prices (the NBBO) and, in the US, Regulation NMS obliges routing marketable orders toward the best displayed price. That means a smart order router that tracks quotes across venues and routes accordingly, and a design discussion about latency arbitrage: the router's view of a remote exchange is always slightly stale, and HFT firms profit from exactly that gap.
