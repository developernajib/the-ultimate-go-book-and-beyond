## Key Design Decisions

Each decision below resolves a specific tension between scale, accuracy, and resource usage. Understanding the trade-offs is what interviewers look for beyond just naming the technologies.

**Bloom Filter for URL Dedup:** Storing 10 billion URLs in an exact hash set would require approximately 1TB of memory (10B URLs at 100 bytes each). A Bloom filter with a 1% false positive rate needs only 12GB, a 100x reduction. Bloom filters have zero false negatives, so a URL already added always tests positive and is never crawled twice. That guarantee is only as good as the implementation: a filter whose bit writes are not atomic will lose updates under concurrent workers and manufacture the false negatives the structure is supposed to make impossible.

The trade-off runs the other way: about 1% of genuinely new URLs test positive and are skipped permanently. At 10 billion pages that is on the order of tens of millions of pages the crawler will never fetch and will never know it missed. A crawler that can tolerate that simply accepts it, and this design does. One that cannot confirms each Bloom positive against an exact store such as a sharded Redis set, paying a network round trip on every positive to recover the lost pages. Note which way that variant cuts: because most discovered links are repeats, most lookups are positives, so confirming them means the exact store is consulted for the large majority of links. The filter then buys you little on the hot path and mostly serves as insurance for the negatives. Pick deliberately rather than assuming the filter is free.

**Kafka Partitioned by Domain:** Partitioning the URL frontier by domain hash ensures that all URLs from the same domain land on the same Kafka partition, and each crawler worker consumes one partition. This makes politeness enforcement straightforward: the worker simply rate-limits itself per domain within its assigned partition. No cross-worker coordination is needed on the hot path, which is the whole point, since a shared rate limiter would mean a network round trip before every fetch. Be clear that the politeness guarantee rests entirely on this invariant. An in-memory limiter is only authoritative because exactly one consumer ever handles a given domain. During a consumer group rebalance, where two consumers can briefly hold the same partition, that invariant lapses and so does the guarantee.

The real cost is partition skew. A domain with 100 million URLs and a 1 req/sec limit needs over three years to drain, so its partition's backlog grows without bound while other partitions sit idle. Throttling does not make skew manageable, it is what causes the backlog. Mitigating it means capping per-domain queue depth and shedding low-priority URLs, splitting very large domains across sub-partitions keyed by host rather than registered domain, and accepting that the crawler will never exhaust the largest sites. Coverage of a huge domain is a prioritization problem, not a throughput problem.

**SimHash for Content Dedup:** Exact content comparison at 10 billion pages would require O(N squared) comparisons, which is computationally infeasible. SimHash reduces each page to a 64-bit fingerprint where similar content produces similar fingerprints. A threshold of 3 or fewer differing bits out of 64 is the operating point Manku, Jain, and Das Sarma validated at Google across 8 billion pages, and it catches what it is meant to catch: mirrors, printer-friendly versions, and CMS-generated duplicates, all of which are near-byte-identical. It does not catch loosely similar pages, and you should distrust any claim that maps 3 bits onto a tidy similarity percentage, since the relationship depends on document length. Measurements are in the core components section. If you need to cluster substantially rewritten pages, use MinHash over shingles with an explicit Jaccard threshold instead. The banding technique (splitting the 64-bit hash into 8 bands of 8 bits) makes lookup tractable by comparing only documents that share at least one identical band, and the pigeonhole principle guarantees that pruning this way never misses a true match: 3 bad bits can spoil at most 3 of the 8 bands.

**Adaptive Recrawl:** The web changes unevenly: news sites update hourly while documentation pages stay static for months. A fixed recrawl interval wastes bandwidth on static pages and misses updates on dynamic ones. The adaptive scheduler tracks each URL's change rate over its crawl history. If a page changed on 80% of recent visits, the interval halves (minimum 1 hour). If it rarely changes, the interval doubles (maximum 30 days). This focuses the crawler's limited bandwidth on the pages that matter most, improving index freshness without increasing total crawl volume.
