## Follow-up Questions

These questions probe operational and edge-case knowledge that interviewers use to test depth beyond the core architecture. Practice answering each in 30-60 seconds.

**Q: How do you support 1000-person webinars?** The architecture splits into two tiers. Panelists (speakers, moderators) use the interactive SFU path with full duplex audio/video. Attendees connect to a CDN-like one-way stream with 3-5 seconds of latency, which is acceptable because they are primarily watching. Chat and Q&A run over a separate WebSocket channel with its own fan-out logic. This separation lets you scale to hundreds of thousands of attendees without scaling the SFU.

**Q: How does virtual background work?** Virtual backgrounds run entirely on the client using a machine learning model (such as MediaPipe or TensorFlow Lite) that segments the person from the background at 30fps. The segmentation mask is used to replace or blur the background before the video frame is encoded and sent to the SFU. The SFU never sees the original background, it receives the already-composited frame, so no server-side processing is needed.

**Q: How do you implement live transcription?** Audio is tapped at the SFU (a copy of the audio track is sent to the transcription pipeline) and forwarded to an ASR (Automatic Speech Recognition) service such as Whisper. Speaker diarization identifies who is talking based on the audio source. Transcription results stream back to clients via WebSocket with 1-3 seconds of latency. The transcription service is a separate, horizontally scalable component that does not affect media routing. Diarization is nearly free here, which is worth pointing out: a standalone recording needs acoustic diarization to guess who spoke, but the SFU already knows, because each participant arrives on its own track with its own SSRC. And as with recording, this feature disappears the moment E2EE is on, since the tap at the SFU produces ciphertext.

**Q: How does key rotation work when someone leaves?** This question hides a trap, and the answer depends on which of the two independent key layers you mean. The DTLS-SRTP layer is hop-by-hop and *pairwise between each client and the SFU*: every participant negotiates its own DTLS session and its own SRTP keys with the server, and no participant ever holds another participant's SRTP keys. So a departing participant already cannot decrypt anyone else's media, with or without rotation, and rotating SRTP on their exit protects nothing. Candidates who answer "rotate SRTP so the leaver cannot decrypt future media" have quietly assumed everyone shares one SRTP key, which is a mesh property, not an SFU one. Rotation on roster change is a real requirement, but at the *other* layer: the E2EE group key is genuinely shared by all participants, so when someone leaves the host must generate a new meeting key and rewrap it for whoever remains, otherwise the leaver decrypts the rest of the meeting from captured packets. The same rotation on join denies a newcomer the frames sent before they arrived.

**Q: How do you handle a participant with poor network?** One principle drives the whole ladder: audio is the call, video is a luxury. Nobody leaves a meeting because the video went grainy, everybody leaves when they cannot hear. So every step spends video to protect audio, in this order: step down the simulcast layer (720p to 360p to 180p) -> turn on Opus in-band FEC and raise the reported packet-loss percentage, which buys redundancy at the cost of a little audio bitrate and one frame of latency -> drop video entirely and go audio-only, which frees the bulk of the budget for the ~50 Kbps that audio actually needs -> only now reduce the audio bitrate itself and lean on DTX so silence costs nothing -> if sustained loss stays above roughly 30%, disconnect gracefully with a "poor connection" notice, because at that point retransmits and FEC are adding load to a link that is already failing. Note the ordering: FEC comes *before* cutting audio bitrate, since FEC costs bitrate. Reversing those two steps means spending your remaining budget on redundancy you then take the budget away from. Each step is reversible, and the upgrade path uses the hysteresis from the adaptive bitrate controller so a link that recovers does not immediately flap back down.
