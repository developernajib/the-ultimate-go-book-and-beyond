# System Design: Ticket Booking System - Concert/Event Ticketing

> When 100,000 concert tickets go on sale at once, the system faces billions of requests in a day, most of them in the opening minutes (Ticketmaster reported 3.5 billion total system requests during the 2022 Eras Tour onsale, four times its previous peak). That is not a normal traffic pattern, it is a coordinated stampede.

Ticket booking is the ultimate burst-traffic problem. Unlike most systems that deal with gradual load growth, a high-demand concert sale creates a traffic spike that dwarfs normal operation by three or four orders of magnitude, 5 million users competing for 100,000 seats within the first minute. The entire system architecture is shaped by this fundamental asymmetry: you must design for the stampede, not for the average day.

The scale numbers define the problem. During a Taylor Swift-level sale, the system absorbs billions of requests over the sale day from 100 million registered users, concentrated brutally in the first minutes. Normal browsing traffic is negligible by comparison. Each seat must be sold exactly once, overselling even a single ticket is a contractual and legal failure. The virtual queue must fairly sequence 5 million concurrent users while the booking service admits roughly 10,000 users per minute, about 167 bookings per second (limited by the payment processor's rate limits, not by internal systems).

The three hardest engineering problems are the thundering herd (solved by a virtual queue backed by a Redis sorted set that transforms an unmanageable spike into a controlled, throttled stream of users entering the booking flow), atomic seat reservation (a Redis Lua script that atomically checks availability and creates a time-limited hold, backed by a PostgreSQL transaction as the second line of defense against overselling), and bot mitigation (multi-signal scoring combining IP rate limits, device fingerprinting, behavioral analysis, and proof-of-work challenges to prevent scalpers from monopolizing the queue).
